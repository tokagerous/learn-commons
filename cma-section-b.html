Unit 01 · B.1 · Strategic Planning

Strategic Planning: Mission, Goals, and the Planning Process

Before any number gets budgeted, the organisation has to answer three questions: who are we, where are we going, and how will we know we got there?

§1.1Why "planning" is more than scheduling

Planning is the management function of choosing among alternative courses of action. Without it, a company runs on momentum — last year's decisions repeated, with no feedback loop. Planning creates that loop: set a target, allocate resources, measure variance, adjust.

The planning hierarchy moves from abstract to concrete:

LevelTime horizonOutputOwner
MissionPermanentWhy we exist; who we serveBoard / founder
Vision5–20 yearsWhat we aspire to beExecutive team
Strategic goals3–5 yearsMeasurable targets supporting the visionExecutive team
Tactical plans1–2 yearsDepartmental plans that ladder upMiddle management
Operational plans<1 yearDay-to-day procedures and quotasFront-line supervisors
BudgetUsually 1 yearQuantified expression of the operational planEach cost / profit center
Analogy — a road trip

Mission = "we love driving." Vision = "drive across the country." Strategic goals = "reach the Pacific within a year." Tactical = "do it in 5 stages of a week each." Operational = "today's stage is Denver to Salt Lake City." Budget = "$320 of gas, $180 of food, 8.5 hours of driving." Each layer is testable; each layer gives meaning to the layer below.

§1.2Mission and vision — what they actually do

A mission statement answers what business are we in? — the customers we serve, the products we provide, the technology we use, the values we hold. A vision statement answers what do we want to become?

Examples (paraphrased):

  • Mission (Patagonia) — "Build the best product, cause no unnecessary harm, use business to inspire and implement solutions to the environmental crisis." → present-tense, descriptive of activity.
  • Vision (Disney's early statement) — "To make people happy." → future-tense, aspirational, inspiring.

The CMA exam tests your ability to identify which is which and to recognise that goals and objectives flow from the mission, not the other way around.

§1.3Goals vs. objectives — a distinction worth knowing

GoalsObjectives
Time horizonLong-term (3+ years)Short-term (≤1 year)
SpecificityBroad, qualitativeSpecific, quantitative — SMART
Example"Become the cost leader in our industry""Reduce manufacturing cost per unit by 8% by Q4"

SMART objectives

Specific · Measurable · Achievable · Relevant · Time-bound. The IMA outline tests this acronym directly. A goal stated as "improve customer service" fails the test; "raise NPS from 42 to 50 by Q4" passes.

§1.4The strategic planning process — six steps

  1. Establish mission, vision, and values. The compass.
  2. Analyse the environment — external (PESTEL, Porter's 5 Forces) and internal (resources, capabilities). See Unit 2.
  3. Set strategic goals for 3–5 years out, anchored in mission and informed by the analysis.
  4. Formulate strategy — corporate (which businesses?), business (how to compete in each?), functional (operations, marketing, finance plans). See Unit 3.
  5. Implement — translate strategy into tactical plans, allocate resources, set objectives, run the org.
  6. Evaluate and adjust — measure variance, learn, iterate. The plan is never finished; it's a living document.

Unit 02 · B.1 · Strategic Planning

Analysing External and Internal Environments

A strategy designed in a vacuum is wishful thinking. Before you choose a path, scan the world outside (what could happen to us?) and the world inside (what can we actually do?).

§2.1The general environment — PESTEL

PESTEL is a framework for scanning forces that affect every firm in the economy:

LetterForceExample impacts
PPoliticalTrade policy, government stability, regulation, lobbying
EEconomicGDP growth, interest rates, inflation, FX, unemployment
SSocialDemographics, lifestyle trends, attitudes, education
TTechnologicalR&D, automation, AI, e-commerce shifts
EEnvironmentalClimate, sustainability, supply-chain physical risk
LLegalEmployment law, IP, antitrust, consumer protection

§2.2The industry environment — Porter's Five Forces

PESTEL casts a wide net; Porter's Five Forces drills into the structure of one industry. The framework asks: how attractive is this industry, and where are the competitive pressures?

Porter's Five Forces — the industry structure that determines profitability

Each force, and what makes it strong

ForceStrong when…Implication
New entrantsLow capital req's, no economies of scale, easy access to distribution, weak brandsErodes margins as incumbents fight for share
SubstitutesCheap alternatives exist, switching cost is low, buyers feel the price-performance of substitutesCaps the price ceiling
Supplier powerFew suppliers, unique input, high switching cost, supplier could integrate forwardSqueezes input costs upward
Buyer powerConcentrated buyers, undifferentiated product, low switching cost, price-sensitiveSqueezes selling prices downward
Industry rivalryMany equally-sized competitors, slow growth, high fixed costs, undifferentiated, high exit barriersCompresses margins to economic profit ≈ 0
Common exam trap

"Threat of new entrants" is about potential entrants, not current rivals. If the question describes Coca-Cola and Pepsi competing, that's industry rivalry, not new entrants. Read the stem carefully.

§2.3The internal environment — resources, capabilities, core competencies

External analysis tells you what could happen to you. Internal analysis tells you what you can actually do.

  • Resources — tangible (cash, plants, locations) and intangible (brands, patents, culture). The raw inputs.
  • Capabilities — what the firm can do well by combining resources (e.g., "rapid new-product development").
  • Core competencies — the small subset of capabilities that are valuable, rare, hard to imitate, and have no substitute — the basis of competitive advantage.
VRIO test for sustainable advantage

A resource/capability creates sustainable advantage only if it is Valuable, Rare, costly to Imitate, and the firm is Organised to exploit it. Score 4/4 for sustainable advantage; 3/4 for temporary; lower for parity or disadvantage.

§2.4SWOT — bringing the two together

HelpfulHarmful
Internal originStrengthsWeaknesses
External originOpportunitiesThreats

SWOT is a summary of PESTEL + Porter (external) + VRIO (internal). The output should be specific: not "weak culture" but "high turnover in engineering — 28% annualised, 2× industry average."

§2.5Value chain analysis

Porter's value chain decomposes the firm into primary activities (those that directly create the product/service) and support activities (that enable them):

Primary activitiesSupport activities
Inbound logisticsFirm infrastructure
OperationsHuman resource management
Outbound logisticsTechnology development

For each activity, ask: do we add unique value here, or is it commodity work better outsourced? The competitive position is shaped by the differential efficiency or differentiation of each link.

Unit 03 · B.1 · Strategic Planning

Formulating and Implementing Strategies

Once you know the environment, the question is how do we win? Three classic frameworks — Porter's generic strategies, the BCG matrix, and the Ansoff matrix — give different lenses on the same choice.

§3.1The three levels of strategy

LevelQuestion answeredTools
CorporateWhich businesses should we be in?BCG matrix, Ansoff matrix, M&A, divestitures
BusinessHow do we compete in each business?Porter's generic strategies (cost / differentiation / focus)
FunctionalHow does each function (ops, marketing, finance, HR) support the business strategy?Department plans, KPIs, budget

§3.2Porter's generic strategies — pick one, commit

Broad marketNarrow market (niche)
Lower costCost leadership (Walmart, Costco)Cost focus (Aldi in some segments)
DifferentiationDifferentiation (Apple, BMW)Differentiation focus (Patagonia, Tesla early years)
Stuck in the middle

Porter argues that trying to be both the cheapest and the most differentiated typically delivers neither — operational compromises in one direction undermine the other. The exception is firms with structural advantages (e.g., Toyota's lean production) that lower cost without sacrificing quality.

§3.3The BCG growth-share matrix — corporate portfolio

For multi-business firms, the BCG matrix plots each unit on two axes: relative market share (high/low) and market growth rate (high/low). Four quadrants:

High market shareLow market share
High growthStars · Invest to maintain shareQuestion Marks (problem children) · Fix or divest
Low growthCash Cows · Milk for cash to fund StarsDogs · Divest, harvest, or shut down

The matrix's prescription is dynamic: today's Stars are tomorrow's Cash Cows; today's Question Marks must be turned into Stars or jettisoned. Cash Cows fund the next wave of investment.

§3.4Ansoff's product-market matrix — growth options

Existing productsNew products
Existing marketsMarket penetration · Sell more to same customers (lowest risk)Product development · New product to existing customers
New marketsMarket development · Take existing product to new customers/geographiesDiversification · New product, new market (highest risk)

Risk increases as you move away from the home quadrant. Most growth strategies sequence: penetrate, then develop product or market, and only then diversify (related diversification before unrelated).

§3.5Strategy implementation — where most plans die

Formulating a strategy on a slide deck is the easy part. Implementation requires aligning structure, systems, people, and culture:

  • Structure — does the org chart support the strategy? (e.g., a differentiation strategy may need a strong R&D function reporting to the CEO; cost leadership needs strong procurement and operations.)
  • Systems — IT, processes, KPIs, incentives. Reward what you want.
  • People — staffing, training, culture. McKinsey's "7-S" framework includes Skills, Staff, Style.
  • Resource allocation — capital and talent must flow to strategic priorities; the budget is the test.
  • Communication — every employee should be able to state the strategy in one sentence.
"Culture eats strategy for breakfast"

Peter Drucker's quip captures why strategy is implementation: a brilliant plan rejected by the culture that has to deliver it is worse than a mediocre plan everyone owns. Sustainable strategy change usually requires a slower, parallel cultural change — not a memo from the CEO.

§3.6Evaluation — measuring whether the strategy works

The final step closes the loop. Evaluation tools include:

  • Balanced scorecard (covered in Section C, Unit 12) — financial, customer, internal process, learning & growth.
  • Variance analysis — actual vs. budget, drilled into root cause.
  • Strategic milestones — qualitative checkpoints with go/no-go decisions.
  • Strategic-fit reviews — does the environment still match the assumptions of the strategy?

Unit 04 · B.1 · Strategic Planning

Other Planning Tools & Characteristics of Successful Plans

Beyond SWOT, PESTEL, and Porter, several specialised tools sharpen specific planning questions — scenario planning for uncertainty, contingency planning for shocks, situational analysis for diagnosis.

§4.1Scenario planning

When the future is genuinely uncertain (e.g., regulatory direction, technology adoption, geopolitical shifts), scenario planning beats single-point forecasts. Build 3–5 internally consistent stories about the future:

  1. Identify key driving forces and uncertainties (PESTEL is a starting point).
  2. Build distinct scenarios — typically a "best", "expected", and "worst" case at minimum, plus selected "wild card" scenarios.
  3. For each scenario, assess implications for the firm and identify robust strategies (those that perform reasonably well across multiple scenarios).
  4. Monitor leading indicators that signal which scenario is actualising.

§4.2Contingency planning

Contingency planning answers "what if [bad thing] happens?" — for specific, identifiable risks. Examples: data breach response, key-person succession, supply-chain disruption, FX shock. Each contingency plan has triggers, decision rights, and pre-positioned resources.

Scenario planningContingency planning
AimStrategic — explore multiple plausible futuresTactical — prepare response to specific events
TriggersSlow-moving leading indicatorsDiscrete event ("if X happens then …")
OutputStrategic options + monitoring frameworkSpecific response playbook

§4.3Situational analysis (the "5C's")

A diagnostic framework before strategy formulation:

  • Company — internal capabilities, positioning, financials
  • Customers — segments, needs, behaviours
  • Competitors — direct + indirect, current + potential
  • Collaborators — suppliers, channel partners, alliance members
  • Climate — the broader environment (≈ PESTEL)

§4.4Gap analysis

The difference between where you are and where you want to be. Quantify the gap (e.g., "current revenue $400M; goal $600M in 3 years; gap $200M"), then identify the strategic levers — penetration, development, M&A — that close it. Each lever gets a feasibility and resource estimate; the chosen mix becomes the strategy.

§4.5Characteristics of successful strategic plans

The IMA outline lists these traits explicitly. A successful plan is:

TraitWhat it means
Aligned with mission and visionEvery initiative connects to the long-term purpose
Realistic and resource-feasibleTargets aren't fantasies; resources are credibly available
MeasurableQuantitative milestones; objective evaluation
Time-boundedClear deadlines for each phase
CommunicableEvery employee can articulate it in 1–2 sentences
Flexible / adaptableBuilt to accommodate change without scrapping
Endorsed by leadershipVisible commitment from the top
Risk-consideredThreats identified, contingencies in place

§4.6Common failure modes

  • Plan-on-the-shelf — written then ignored. The plan must drive operating decisions.
  • No accountability — every initiative needs an owner, deadline, and budget.
  • Misalignment with budget — if money flows to legacy priorities while strategy targets new ones, strategy loses.
  • No mid-course correction — environments shift; plans that don't update become irrelevant.
  • Over-precision in turbulent environments — directional plans (broad, flexible) outperform specific plans when uncertainty is high.

Unit 05 · B.2 · Budgeting Concepts

Budgeting Concepts

A budget is the strategic plan expressed in numbers. Done well, it aligns the organisation around shared targets. Done badly, it becomes a political game played for slack.

§5.1Planning, budgeting, and performance evaluation — one cycle

The three activities form a closed loop:

§5.2Why budget? The five advantages

  • Communication & coordination — every department learns what others plan; bottlenecks surface in advance.
  • Resource allocation — scarce capital and headcount are explicitly assigned, not left to first-come-first-served.
  • Performance benchmark — actual results have a comparison point.
  • Motivation — measurable targets give managers something to aim for.
  • Strategic execution — the budget operationalises strategy; if money doesn't flow to strategic priorities, the strategy isn't real.

§5.3The five limitations

  • Time-consuming — typical Fortune 500 budgeting cycle is 3–6 months of finance + management time.
  • Rigid in turbulent environments — a rigid annual budget set in November ages quickly when the world shifts in March.
  • Encourages gaming — managers padded the request, then spent the slack to "use it or lose it."
  • Functional silos — departments optimise local budgets at the firm's expense (e.g., procurement gets bonus for cost savings; quality and downtime increase).
  • Backward-looking — anchored in last year's numbers rather than zero-based or activity-based reality.

§5.6Budgetary slack and goal congruence

Definition

Budgetary slack = the deliberate underestimation of revenue or overestimation of expense by managers when they propose budgets, so the targets are easier to beat. It looks generous to grant; it's expensive to live with.

Goal congruence = alignment between manager incentives and firm objectives. Slack creates incongruence: the manager wins (easy bonus) while the firm loses (artificially low expectations, sandbagged forecasts).

Mitigations:

  • Multi-year incentive plans — slack accrued today produces low targets tomorrow that managers must beat too.
  • Independent benchmarks — peer firms, industry stats, customer pricing as cross-checks on the manager's number.
  • Stretch + commit — set both an aggressive "stretch" target and a "commit" baseline; reward both.
  • Top-down boundaries — finance sets resource ceilings before bottom-up detailed budgeting.

§5.7Responsibility centers — who owns what

Controllable cost principle

A manager should be evaluated only on costs they can control. Allocated head-office costs, depreciation on inherited equipment, and corporate taxes are typically not controllable at the operating-unit level — including them in the manager's variance distorts incentives.

  • Top-management support — visible, not delegated to finance alone

Unit 06 · B.2 · Budgeting Concepts

Establishing Standards

A standard is a benchmark — what materials should cost, how much labour should take. Variance analysis (Section C) compares actual to standard. The choice of standard is a strategic and motivational lever.

§6.1Two big choices, three standard types

Standard typeDefinitionEffect on motivation
Ideal (theoretical, perfection)Achievable only under ideal conditions — perfect equipment, no breakdowns, no idle time, no wasteOften demotivating; always shows unfavourable variance; rarely used for evaluation
Currently attainable (practical, normal)Achievable with reasonable effort under normal operating conditions; allows for normal spoilage, breakdowns, downtimeMost motivating; the IMA's preferred basis for evaluation
Basic (historical)Historical performance benchmark — what we've achieved beforeUseful for trend tracking but not for setting today's expectations
Most-tested distinction

When the exam asks which standard to use for performance evaluation, the answer is almost always currently attainable — challenging enough to motivate improvement, realistic enough to be credible. Ideal standards are reserved for engineering-study purposes.

§6.2The two components of every input standard

Standard cost = Standard quantity × Standard price

For materials and labour, both pieces are standards in their own right:

Standard quantity per unit of output — engineered: how much input should be needed?
Standard price per unit of input — economic: what should we pay?

§6.3Sources of standards

  • Engineered standards — derived from engineering studies (time-and-motion, BOM analysis). Most rigorous; expensive to build; common for repetitive manufacturing.
  • Historical / observation-based — average past performance with adjustments. Cheap; risk of perpetuating past inefficiency.

§6.4Updating standards

Standards age. They should be reviewed at least annually, more often when:

  • Input prices have shifted materially (commodity cycle, wage settlements, FX)
  • Process changes (new equipment, lean redesign) make prior quantity standards obsolete
  • Continuous improvement programs (Kaizen budgeting) systematically tighten standards each cycle
  • Persistent same-direction variances signal that the standard, not performance, is wrong
Kaizen budgeting

Used by lean manufacturers (Toyota, Honda): the standard cost is reduced each period by an expected continuous-improvement amount (e.g., 1% per quarter). Forecasting Techniques & Regression Analysis

A budget number is only as good as the forecast behind it. Quantitative forecasting comes in three flavours: time-series (use the past), causal (use drivers), and qualitative (use judgment).

§7.1The forecasting taxonomy

Method familyExamplesBest for
Time-seriesMoving average, weighted MA, exponential smoothing, decomposition (trend + seasonality + cycle + irregular)Stable, recurring patterns; short horizons
Causal / regressionSimple linear regression, multiple regression, econometric modelsDrivers are known and forecastable
QualitativeDelphi, expert panels, market research, sales-force composite, executive opinionNew products, structural breaks, long horizons, no historical data

§7.2Time-series components

  • Trend (T) — long-run direction (e.g., +5%/year revenue growth)
  • Seasonality (S) — within-year repeating pattern (Q4 retail spike)
  • Cycle (C) — multi-year economic cycles (recessions)
  • Irregular / random (I) — unexplained noise

Decomposition: Y = T × S × C × I (multiplicative) or Y = T + S + C + I (additive). De-seasonalise before forecasting trend; re-apply seasonality afterwards.

§7.3Simple linear regression — Y = a + bX

Regression fits a straight line through a scatter of paired observations (X, Y) by minimising the sum of squared residuals (least-squares). The line tells you, for each X, the predicted Y.

Slope and intercept formulas
Slope  b = [n·Σ(XY) − (ΣX)(ΣY)] ÷ [n·Σ(X²) − (ΣX)²]
Intercept  a = (ΣY − b·ΣX) ÷ n  = Ȳ − b·X̄

For cost analysis specifically:

  • a (intercept) = estimated total fixed cost
  • b (slope) = estimated variable cost per unit of activity
  • X = activity driver (units, machine hours, DL hours)
  • Y = total cost

§7.4Goodness of fit — R²

Coefficient of determination
R² = (Variation explained by regression) ÷ (Total variation)

R² ranges from 0 to 1. R² = 0.92 means 92% of the variation in Y is explained by X. Higher R² means tighter fit and more reliable predictions. The correlation coefficient r = √R² (with the sign of the slope), ranging from −1 to +1.

Don't conflate fit with causation

A high R² says the line fits the data well — not that X causes Y. The driver must make economic sense (machine hours driving electricity cost, yes; ice-cream sales driving drowning rates, no — both are driven by summer).

§7.5Multiple regression — adding drivers

Y = a + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂ + … + bₙXₙ adds drivers. Each partial slope bᵢ = effect of a 1-unit change in Xᵢ holding others constant. Beware:

  • Multicollinearity — when X's are correlated with each other (e.g., DL hours and machine hours), individual coefficients become unstable even when overall fit is good.
  • Overfitting — adding too many variables to a small sample raises R² mechanically without true predictive power. Use adjusted R², which penalises added variables.
  • Spurious correlation — see ice-cream/drowning example.

§7.6Forecast accuracy measures

Three common error metrics
MAD (Mean Absolute Deviation) = Σ|Actual − Forecast| ÷ n
MSE (Mean Squared Error) = Σ(Actual − Forecast)² ÷ n
MAPE (Mean Absolute % Error) = Σ|Actual − Forecast|/Actual ÷ n × 100%

MAD treats all errors equally. MSE penalises big errors more heavily (squaring). MAPE expresses error as a percentage — comparable across scales.

Probability and Expected Value

When the future is uncertain, weight each possible outcome by its probability and sum. The result is the expected value — the single best summary of an uncertain forecast.

§9.1Expected value (EV) — the core formula

Expected value
EV = Σ (Pᵢ × Xᵢ)

where Pᵢ is the probability of outcome i and Xᵢ is the payoff (or cost) under that outcome. Probabilities must sum to 1.

§9.2Worked example — Q4 sales scenarios

ScenarioProbabilityQ4 salesP × Sales
Recession20%$2.0M$0.40M
Flat economy50%$3.5M$1.75M
Strong growth30%$5.0M$1.50M
Expected value100%$3.65M

$3.65M is the probability-weighted average. Use it for capital budgeting NPVs, contract pricing, and any decision-making under risk.

§9.3Decision trees — sequential decisions under uncertainty

For decisions involving sequential choices (do we run a market test? then do we launch?), build a tree:

  • Decision nodes (squares) — the manager picks
  • Chance nodes (circles) — nature picks, probabilities apply
  • Terminal nodes — payoffs

Solve backwards (from right to left): at each chance node, compute EV; at each decision node, pick the highest-EV branch. The tree's leftmost EV is the optimal strategy's expected payoff.

Value of perfect information (VPI)

If you could know the outcome before deciding, the expected payoff would be ΣPᵢ × (best decision under outcome i). The difference between this and the EV without information is the Value of Perfect Information — the maximum a decision-maker should pay for a forecast that resolves the uncertainty.

Example — VPI calculation

Two products, two markets. Best EV without info = $200K (product A). If I knew the market in advance, I'd choose A in market 1 ($250K) and B in market 2 ($300K). Each market has 50% probability.

  • EV with perfect info = 0.5 × $250K + 0.5 × $300K = $275K
  • EV without info = $200K
  • VPI = $275K − $200K = $75K — the cap on what to pay for the forecast

§9.5Decision rules under uncertainty

  • Maximax (optimist) — pick the alternative with the highest possible payoff. Ignores downside.
  • Maximin (pessimist) — pick the alternative with the highest worst-case payoff. Risk-averse.
  • Minimax regret — for each outcome, compute regret (how much worse than the best choice for that outcome). Pick the alternative with the smallest maximum regret.
  • Laplace (insufficient reason) — assume equal probabilities for all outcomes; compute EV; pick highest. Used when you can't even guess probabilities.

Unit 10 · B.4 · Budget Methodologies

Budget Methodologies

Six different ways to build a budget — pick the methodology that matches the volatility, the decision rhythm, and the strategic question you're trying to answer.

§10.1Master (annual) budget — the standard package

The master budget is the comprehensive, integrated set of budgets covering all aspects of operations for a single year. Two main components:

  • Operating budgets — sales, production, DM, DL, MOH, S&A, ending in the budgeted income statement
  • Financial budgets — capital, cash, ending in the budgeted balance sheet and budgeted cash flow statement

The master budget is built sequentially: sales drives production, which drives DM/DL/MOH, which combine into the budgeted COGS, which feeds the I/S; capital decisions and cash flows drop out into the financial budgets.

§10.2Static vs. flexible budgets

The variance trap

If actual sales are 10% above static-budget plan, all variable costs will be over the static-budget number — but that's expected, not bad performance. The flexible budget recasts the budget at actual volume, so any remaining variance is true price/efficiency news.

§10.3Project budgeting

Used for one-off, finite-life initiatives (a new ERP rollout, plant expansion, M&A integration). Defining traits:

  • Multi-period (often multi-year) but with a defined end
  • Cost-tracked separately from operating budgets
  • Earned-value management (EVM) measures progress: planned value, earned value, actual cost
  • Capital decisions tied to NPV, IRR (covered in CMA Part 2 capital budgeting)

§10.4Activity-based budgeting (ABB)

ABB inverts traditional budgeting: instead of starting with last year's budget, start with the activities the firm will perform. For each activity, estimate the cost driver volume and the cost per driver unit; sum across activities.

  1. Forecast demand and the production volume needed
  2. Identify the activities required to deliver that volume (setups, inspections, machine runs, customer orders)
  3. Estimate the volume of each activity (number of setups, inspections, etc.)
  4. Determine the cost per activity unit (using historical activity data or engineered rates)
  5. Multiply: activity volume × cost per activity = budgeted activity cost
  6. Sum activity costs to derive total budget

Strength: tightly couples cost to drivers, exposing inefficient activities. Weakness: data-intensive; ABB requires an existing ABC cost system.

Instead of one annual budget, maintain a budget that always extends, say, 12 months into the future. Each month (or quarter), drop the oldest period and add a new one at the end. Benefits:

  • Forces continuous re-evaluation of assumptions
  • Reduces gaming associated with annual cycles
  • Used heavily by tech firms and others in fast-changing environments

Estimating Fixed and Variable Costs

Two methods you must know cold: high-low (quick and approximate) and least-squares regression (precise). Both decompose a mixed cost into its fixed and variable components.

§13.1Why decompose? The cost-behaviour question

"Total cost" by itself is opaque. Knowing that $72,000 of utility cost in a month was actually $15,000 fixed + $4.50 per machine hour × 12,667 hours lets you:

  • Forecast cost at different production volumes
  • Compute contribution margin and break-even
  • Build flexible budgets for variance analysis
  • Decide on outsourcing, special orders, make-or-buy

§13.2The high-low method

  1. From observations of (activity, cost), pick the highest-activity period and the lowest-activity period.
  2. Compute slope (variable rate) using just those two points:
Variable cost / unit  = (Cost at high activity − Cost at low activity) ÷ (High activity − Low activity)

Fixed cost  = Total cost at high activity − (Variable cost / unit × High activity level)
   or equivalently  = Total cost at low activity − (Variable cost / unit × Low activity level)

Worked example

MonthMachine hoursTotal cost
March9,000$54,000
April (LOW)7,000$48,000
May11,000$60,000
June (HIGH)14,000$72,000
  • Variable rate = ($72,000 − $48,000) ÷ (14,000 − 7,000) = $24,000 / 7,000 = $3.43/hr (round to $3.43 or carry full decimals through).
  • Fixed cost = $72,000 − ($3.43 × 14,000) = $72,000 − $48,000 = $24,000 (using June). Cross-check: $48,000 − ($3.43 × 7,000) = $48,000 − $24,000 = $24,000 ✓.
  • Cost equation: Total Cost = $24,000 + $3.43 × machine hours.
High-low's limits

Uses only TWO data points — the high and the low. Any abnormal observation (a strike, a one-off shutdown, a one-off bonus) at either extreme distorts the line. Regression uses all points and is more robust.

§13.3Least-squares regression — when to use it

Section B Unit 7 covered the regression formulas. For cost estimation, the same line Y = a + bX gives:

  • a (intercept) = estimated fixed cost
  • b (slope) = estimated variable cost per unit of driver
  • R² = how much of the cost variability is explained by the chosen driver

If R² is low (say, < 0.5) the driver is poorly chosen — try another (machine hours vs. DL hours vs. units) or use multiple regression.

§13.4The relevant range

Cost relationships are linear within the relevant range — the band of activity over which the equation reliably holds. Outside it (extreme overtime, brand-new shift, additional plant), step costs and capacity constraints break the line.

Always state the relevant range with the cost equation. Don't extrapolate predictions far beyond the observed activity span.

§13.5Estimating total costs at a forecast volume

Total cost at activity X = Fixed cost + Variable rate × X

From the worked example: at 12,000 machine hours, total cost ≈ $24,000 + $3.43 × 12,000 = $24,000 + $41,160 = $65,160.

Ongoing Budgetary Reporting

A budget that lives only in a binder is dead. The reporting cadence — monthly variance reports, quarterly forecasts, exception alerts — is what turns the plan into a management tool.

§14.2Management by exception

Don't flag every variance — only those above a materiality threshold (e.g., >5% or >$10,000, whichever is greater). The manager's attention is finite; spending it on trivial variances dilutes the signal.

  • Require root-cause explanation for variances above threshold
  • Escalate persistent unfavourable variances upward

§14.3The "rolling forecast" supplement

Many firms supplement the static annual budget with a quarterly rolling forecast. Each quarter, the management team reforecasts the next 4 quarters; the budget targets aren't reset, but the rolling forecast informs operating decisions while the budget anchors compensation.

§14.4Reporting principles for the CMA

  • Timely — monthly reports within 5–10 business days of close
  • Relevant — focused on what each recipient can act on (controllability principle)
  • Concise — top-of-page exception alerts; details available but not in your face
  • Reliable — same source data, same calculations, audited periodically
  • Comparable — same format month to month so trends are visible
  • Forward-looking — pair backward variance with forward action items

Answering Budgeting Calculation Questions

A toolkit of patterns for the calculation-heavy CMA budget questions: production from sales, DM purchases, cash collections, payments to suppliers, and the budgeted income statement.

§15.1Pattern 1 — Production from sales

Production = Sales + Desired ending FG − Beginning FG

Try this: Sales 8,000 units; beginning FG 600; desired ending FG 800. Production = 8,000 + 800 − 600 = 8,200 units.

§15.2Pattern 2 — DM purchases (in lbs and dollars)

DM needed for production = Production units × DM per unit
DM purchases (lbs) = DM needed + Desired ending DM − Beginning DM
DM purchases ($) = DM purchases (lbs) × Price per lb

§15.3Pattern 3 — Cash receipts from credit sales

If the collection pattern is X% in month, Y% next month, Z% second next, then:

Cash in month M = X% × Sales(M) + Y% × Sales(M-1) + Z% × Sales(M-2) [+ cash sales]

Watch for: bad debts (the leftover %), the timing of cash sales (full amount in same period), and trade discounts.

§15.4Pattern 4 — Cash payments to suppliers

Mirror image of receipts: the firm typically pays X% of purchases in month, Y% next, etc., based on credit terms (e.g., net 30, 2/10 net 30).

Watch for purchase discounts

If terms are 2/10 net 30 and the firm pays within 10 days, cash outflow = 98% of purchase amount. Forecast both the discount taken and the cash impact.

§15.5Pattern 5 — Cash budget end-state

Beginning cash
+ Receipts
− Disbursements
= Cash before financing
± Net financing (borrow if < minimum, repay if surplus)
= Ending cash

§15.6Pattern 6 — Pro forma income statement

From operating budgets:

Sales − COGS = Gross profit
− S&A expense = Operating income
− Interest = Pretax income
− Tax = Net income

COGS computation under absorption costing requires the unit product cost (DM + DL + Variable MOH + Fixed MOH/unit) times units sold.

§15.7Common traps

  • Beginning vs. ending inventory direction — production = sales + ending − beginning. Reversing the signs is the most common error.
  • Bad debt % — compute as 100% − (sum of collection percentages). Don't assume it's the same as the rate in the question.
  • Cash sales — when present, recognise the full cash sale amount in the same period; only credit sales follow the pattern.
  • Depreciation in cash budget — never include; it's non-cash.
  • Year-end vs. month-end inventory targets — read the policy precisely (sometimes "20% of next month's sales", sometimes "30% of current month's COGS").

Top-Level Planning, Pro Forma Statements, and the FFS Method

For multi-year strategic planning and external-financing decisions, the right tool isn't a 12-tab master budget — it's the percent-of-sales / forecasted financial statement (FFS) method.

§16.1Pro forma financial statements

Pro forma ("for the sake of form") financial statements are projections — not actual results. They take projected sales as the driver and forecast every other line item that varies with sales. Used for:

  • Multi-year strategic planning (3–5 year forecasts)
  • Capital structure decisions (do we need to issue debt or equity?)
  • M&A valuation (project target's standalone results)
  • Loan applications and prospectuses

§16.2Sales forecasting for the medium- and long-term

Multi-year sales forecasts blend top-down (industry × market share) and bottom-up (channel × territory × product) views:

MethodStrengthWeakness
Trend extrapolationCheap, simpleAssumes past patterns continue
Macro × market shareAnchored in industry economicsRequires reliable industry forecasts and stable share
Bottom-up sales-force compositeFrontline knowledgeSlow, often optimistic
Regression on demand driversQuantifies driver effectsDrivers must themselves be forecastable
Scenario / probabilisticAccounts for uncertaintyComplex; presentation-heavy

§16.3The FFS / percent-of-sales method — the workhorse

  1. Forecast sales for each year of the planning horizon.
  2. Project line items that vary with sales as a constant % of sales — typically variable costs, AR, inventory, AP. This is the assumption that makes the method tractable.
  3. Project line items that don't vary with sales separately — fixed costs grow with capacity additions; depreciation depends on PP&E schedule; debt and equity follow capital structure decisions.
  4. Build the forecasted income statement. Sales (1) − Variable costs (% of sales) − Fixed costs − D&A − Interest − Tax = NI.
  5. Build the forecasted balance sheet. AR, inventory, AP, accrued expenses follow %-of-sales. PP&E from CapEx schedule. Equity = Beginning equity + NI − Dividends. Debt is the plug.
  6. Compute the financing gap = needed assets − projected liabilities & equity. This is the Additional Funds Needed (AFN).

§16.4The Additional Funds Needed (AFN) formula

AFN — quick estimate without the full pro forma
AFN = (A*/S₀) × ΔS  −  (L*/S₀) × ΔS  −  M × S₁ × (1 − d)

Where:
A*/S₀ = ratio of assets-that-grow-with-sales to current sales
L*/S₀ = ratio of spontaneous-liabilities to current sales (AP, accruals)
ΔS = change in sales (S₁ − S₀)
M = profit margin (NI / Sales)
S₁ = next-year sales
d = dividend payout ratio (so 1 − d = retention ratio)

What each term means

  • (A*/S₀) × ΔS — the increase in operating assets needed to support the sales increase
  • (L*/S₀) × ΔS — the spontaneous funding from increases in AP and accruals
  • M × S₁ × (1 − d) — internally generated funds = retained earnings on next-year's projected NI
  • AFN — the residual = external financing required (debt, new equity)

§16.5Worked example — AFN calculation

Worked example

S₀ (current sales) = $5M. S₁ (next year sales) = $6M. ΔS = $1M.

Operating assets that grow with sales = $4M (so A*/S₀ = 80%). Spontaneous liabilities = $0.6M (so L*/S₀ = 12%). Profit margin = 8%. Dividend payout = 30%.

  • Asset increase needed = 80% × $1M = $800K
  • Spontaneous funding = 12% × $1M = $120K
  • Retained earnings = 8% × $6M × (1 − 0.30) = $480K × 0.70 = $336K
  • AFN = $800K − $120K − $336K = $344K external financing required

§16.6Limitations of the FFS / AFN method

  • Assumes constant ratios — at scale changes (new plant, new product), ratios shift
  • Smooths capital lumpiness — real CapEx is lumpy (a new plant for $50M doesn't ramp linearly)
  • Doesn't reflect operating leverage — fixed costs that grow stepwise distort the linear model
  • Ignores capital structure choices — AFN tells you how much; not whether debt or equity
  • Not a substitute for full multi-period pro forma when the planning horizon is long
