Unit 1 · D.1

Cost behavior — fixed vs variable, mixed, relevant range

Costs behave in patterns. Variable scales with activity (per-unit constant); fixed doesn't (per-unit decreases as activity rises); mixed has both. Behavior holds only within the relevant range.

§1.1Direction of behavior

Cost typeTotal as activity ↑Per-unit as activity ↑
VariableIncreases proportionallyConstant
FixedConstant (within range)Decreases
MixedIncreases (less than proportionally)Decreases (toward variable rate)

§1.2High-low method

Variable rate = (High cost − Low cost) ÷ (High activity − Low activity)
Fixed = Total cost − (Variable rate × Activity)

Practice 1 — Per-unit fixed cost

As production volume rises (within the relevant range), per-unit fixed cost:

  • Rises
  • Stays the same
  • Falls
  • Doubles
Why: Total fixed is constant; spreading over more units lowers the per-unit charge — the basis of operating leverage.

Practice 2 — High-low

High 12,000 units cost $80K; low 4,000 units cost $40K. Variable rate per unit:

  • $5
  • $3.33
  • $10
  • $6.67
Why: ($80K − $40K) / (12K − 4K) = $40K / 8K = $5/unit. Fixed = $80K − ($5 × 12K) = $20K.

Practice 3 — Mixed cost

Cell phone bill: $40 monthly base + $0.10/min usage. This is:

  • Pure fixed
  • Pure variable
  • Mixed (semi-variable)
  • Step-fixed
Why: Fixed component ($40 base) + variable rate ($0.10/min) = mixed cost. Common test cue: a base fee + per-use charge.
Unit 2 · D.1

Actual, normal, and standard costs

Three approaches to product costing. They differ in when a cost rate is set and what it represents.

MethodDM & DLManufacturing OH
ActualActualActual
NormalActualPredetermined rate × actual activity
StandardStandard rate × standard quantityStandard rate × standard hours
Predetermined OH rate = Budgeted OH ÷ Budgeted activity
Applied OH = Predetermined rate × ACTUAL activity

Year-end variance between applied and actual OH closes to COGS (small) or prorated across WIP/FG/COGS (material).

Practice 1 — Predetermined rate

Budget OH $400K; budget DLH 20,000. Predetermined OH rate:

  • $25/DLH
  • $20/DLH
  • $50/DLH
  • $10/DLH
Why: $400K / 20,000 = $20/DLH. Applied OH later uses ACTUAL DLH × $20.

Practice 2 — Under-applied entry

Actual OH $310K; applied OH $295K. Year-end closing entry to COGS:

  • DR COGS $15K / CR Mfg OH $15K
  • DR Mfg OH $15K / CR COGS $15K
  • DR COGS $295K / CR Mfg OH $295K
  • No entry needed
Why: Actual > Applied = under-applied $15K. Raise COGS: DR COGS $15K / CR Mfg OH $15K.

Practice 3 — Standard cost meaning

A standard cost is best described as:

  • Last period's actual cost
  • The minimum cost ever achieved
  • An engineered, attainable target
  • The industry's average cost
Why: Standards are engineered targets — currently attainable with normal effort. Variances measure deviations from these targets.
Unit 3 · D.2

Variable & fixed overhead, allocation bases

Overhead = production cost that isn't direct materials or direct labor. Some scales with activity (variable OH: indirect materials, machine utility); some doesn't (fixed OH: rent, supervision, depreciation).

§3.1Allocating overhead

Pick a base that causes overhead — direct labor hours, machine hours, materials cost — and apply at a predetermined rate (Unit 2). A plantwide rate uses one base for the whole plant; departmental rates use a different base per department.

§3.2When to use which

  • Plantwide — products consume departmental resources in similar proportions. Simplest.
  • Departmental — departments differ in resource intensity (machine-heavy vs labor-heavy). More accurate.
  • Activity-based — overhead is large and product mix is diverse. Most accurate, most expensive.

Practice 1 — Allocation base choice

A CNC machining department's overhead is best allocated using:

  • Direct labor hours
  • Sales dollars
  • Machine hours
  • Number of employees
Why: Machine-intensive department → machine hours drive utility, depreciation, maintenance. Direct labor would distort costs.

Practice 2 — Plantwide vs departmental

A plantwide rate is most likely to distort product cost when:

  • All products use departments equally
  • Departments differ in resource intensity
  • The plant has only one department
  • Direct material is the largest cost
Why: One rate for everything works only when all products consume each department's resources in similar proportion. Otherwise, departmental rates are more accurate.

Practice 3 — Variable OH

Which is most likely a variable overhead item?

  • Factory rent
  • Plant manager's salary
  • Production-line equipment depreciation
  • Indirect materials (lubricants, fasteners)
Why: Indirect materials scale with output — variable. Rent, salary, depreciation are fixed within the relevant range.
Unit 4 · D.3

Direct material & direct labor variances

Variance analysis decomposes the gap between standard and actual into price/rate (per-unit cost difference) and quantity/efficiency (how much was used) effects.

§4.1Direct material

DM Price Variance = (Actual price − Standard price) × Actual quantity purchased
DM Quantity Variance = (Actual quantity used − Standard quantity allowed) × Standard price

§4.2Direct labor

DL Rate Variance = (Actual rate − Standard rate) × Actual hours
DL Efficiency Variance = (Actual hours − Standard hours allowed) × Standard rate

Standard quantity allowed = Actual output × standard quantity per unit. Same idea for labor: actual output × standard hours per unit.

Sign convention

Positive (actual > standard) = unfavorable (U); negative = favorable (F). For price/rate variances, paying MORE is unfavorable. For quantity/efficiency, USING MORE is unfavorable.

Practice 1 — DM price variance

Standard price $4/lb; actual price $4.20/lb; actual purchase 5,000 lb. DM price variance:

  • $1,000 U
  • $1,000 F
  • $200 U
  • $20,000 U
Why: ($4.20 − $4.00) × 5,000 = $0.20 × 5,000 = $1,000 U. Paid $0.20 more per pound on 5,000 pounds.

Practice 2 — DM quantity variance

Standard 3 lb/unit at $4 standard price; produced 1,000 units; actually used 3,200 lb. DM quantity variance:

  • $200 F
  • $0
  • $800 U
  • $800 F
Why: Standard quantity allowed = 1,000 × 3 = 3,000 lb. (3,200 − 3,000) × $4 = $800 U. Used 200 lb more than standard at $4 standard price.

Practice 3 — DL efficiency

Standard 2 hrs/unit at $25 standard rate; produced 500 units; actual 1,050 hours. DL efficiency variance:

  • $1,500 F
  • $1,250 U
  • $1,250 F
  • $2,500 U
Why: Standard hours allowed = 500 × 2 = 1,000. (1,050 − 1,000) × $25 = $1,250 U. Took 50 hours more than standard.
Unit 5 · D.3

Overhead variances (basic)

Overhead variances follow the same price/quantity logic but with twist: applied OH uses an estimated rate, so variances split between spending (rate accuracy) and volume (denominator activity).

§5.1Variable OH

VOH Spending = (Actual rate − Standard rate) × Actual hours
VOH Efficiency = (Actual hours − Standard hours allowed) × Standard rate

Identical structure to DL variance, just applied to variable overhead.

§5.2Fixed OH

FOH Budget Variance = Actual FOH − Budgeted FOH
FOH Volume Variance = Budgeted FOH − Applied FOH (= Std rate × Standard hours allowed)

Volume variance reflects under- or over-utilization of capacity. Higher actual production than budgeted = favorable volume variance (more units absorbing the same fixed cost).

Practice 1 — VOH efficiency

Standard VOH rate $5/DLH; standard hours allowed 2,000; actual hours 2,200. VOH efficiency variance:

  • $1,000 F
  • $1,000 U
  • $1,000 unfavorable
  • $11,000 U
Why: (2,200 − 2,000) × $5 = $1,000 U. (Same as choice C.) Took 200 hours more than standard at $5 rate.

Practice 2 — FOH budget variance

Budgeted FOH $40K; actual FOH $43K. Budget variance:

  • $3,000 U
  • $3,000 F
  • $0
  • Cannot be calculated
Why: Budget variance = Actual − Budgeted = $43K − $40K = $3,000 U. Spent more on fixed overhead than planned.

Practice 3 — Volume variance source

A favorable fixed overhead VOLUME variance arises when:

  • Actual fixed OH is lower than budgeted
  • Actual rate is lower than standard rate
  • Hours used were less than allowed
  • Actual production exceeded the level used to set the predetermined rate
Why: Volume variance = Budgeted FOH − Applied FOH (std rate × std hrs allowed). Higher actual production raises applied FOH above budget — favorable. The other choices describe budget or efficiency variances, not volume.
Unit 6 · D.4 Performance

Profitability analysis & ROI

Beyond company-level NI, analyze profit at finer cuts — by product, business unit, customer. The same lens applies: revenue minus directly attributable cost.

§6.1Cuts of profit

  • Product profitability — revenue per SKU minus traceable variable cost (and product-specific fixed) → gross or contribution margin per product.
  • Business unit profitability — segment revenue minus segment-level cost; used in segment reporting (Section A) and bonus calculations.
  • Customer profitability — customer revenue minus all costs to serve that customer (acquisition, support, customizations). Often surprises management.

§6.2Return on investment (ROI)

ROI = Net income ÷ Average investment (or assets)
= Profit margin × Asset turnover (DuPont 2-step)

ROI lets investments be compared on a common scale. Drawback: managers may reject good projects whose return is below the unit's current ROI (because accepting drags the average down). Residual income (RI = NI − Required return × Investment) avoids that incentive trap.

Practice 1 — ROI

Operating income $200K; average operating assets $1M. ROI:

  • 10%
  • 20%
  • 5%
  • 50%
Why: $200K / $1M = 20%. Decompose: assume sales $2M, then margin 10% × turnover 2.0 = 20% ROI.

Practice 2 — ROI weakness

A potential weakness of ROI as a divisional performance metric is:

  • It cannot be calculated below the firm level
  • It rewards risk-taking
  • Managers may reject projects that earn above the cost of capital but below the division's current ROI
  • It ignores asset utilization
Why: The dilution incentive: a manager at 25% ROI rejects a 18% project even if cost of capital is 12%. RI fixes this by charging a capital cost.

Practice 3 — Customer profitability

Customer profitability analysis often reveals:

  • A small fraction of customers generate most profit; some are unprofitable to serve
  • All customers contribute roughly equal profit
  • Customer acquisition cost is irrelevant
  • Service costs are the same for every customer
Why: The Pareto pattern is universal — typically 20% of customers generate 80% of profit, and 10-20% of customers may actually lose money once full service costs are loaded. Action: tier the service model to fit the profitability tier.
Unit 7 · D.5 Decisions

Breakeven & CVP analysis

Cost-volume-profit analysis links volume, contribution margin, and operating income. The breakeven point is the volume where total revenue equals total cost — zero operating profit.

§7.1Core formulas

Contribution margin = Sales − Variable cost
Contribution margin ratio = CM ÷ Sales
Breakeven units = Fixed costs ÷ Unit CM
Breakeven sales $ = Fixed costs ÷ CM ratio
Target profit units = (Fixed + Target profit) ÷ Unit CM

§7.2Margin of safety & operating leverage

Margin of safety = Actual sales − Breakeven sales (in $ or units)
Operating leverage = Contribution margin ÷ Operating income

High operating leverage means small sales changes drive big OI changes — sword that cuts both ways. Capital-intensive industries (airlines, telecoms) typically have higher operating leverage than service businesses.

Practice 1 — Breakeven units

Selling price $50; variable cost $30; fixed cost $80,000. Breakeven units:

  • 4,000
  • 2,667
  • 1,600
  • 2,000
Why: Unit CM = $50 − $30 = $20. BE units = $80K / $20 = 4,000. Each unit contributes $20 toward covering fixed; need 4,000 to clear $80K.

Practice 2 — CM ratio

Sales $500K; variable costs $300K. CM ratio:

  • 30%
  • 60%
  • 40%
  • 67%
Why: CM = $500K − $300K = $200K. CM ratio = $200K / $500K = 40%. Every additional dollar of sales adds 40 cents of CM.

Practice 3 — Target profit

Same as Q1; target operating profit $40,000. Required sales units:

  • 4,000
  • 5,000
  • 2,000
  • 6,000
Why: ($80K + $40K) / $20 = $120K / $20 = 6,000 units. Above breakeven (4,000), each additional unit's CM falls to OI; need 2,000 more units × $20 = $40K profit.
Unit 8 · D.5 Decisions

Sunk costs, opportunity costs, marginal analysis

When a manager evaluates an option (special order, make-vs-buy, sell-or-process-further), only the costs and revenues that change with the decision matter. The discipline of identifying these — and ignoring sunk costs — is marginal analysis.

§8.1Three cost concepts

  • Sunk cost — already spent; never relevant to a future decision.
  • Opportunity cost — value of the best foregone alternative; ALWAYS relevant.
  • Avoidable / differential cost — changes between options; what marginal analysis weighs.

§8.2Common decisions

DecisionCompare
Special orderIncremental revenue vs incremental variable cost (and any opportunity cost of capacity)
Make-vs-buyAvoidable cost of making vs purchase price (plus quality/control)
Sell or process furtherIncremental revenue vs separable processing cost
Drop a segmentLost CM vs avoidable fixed cost
Trap

Allocated overhead is often NOT relevant — much of it persists whether the decision goes either way. Always check whether each cost actually changes with the option chosen.

Practice 1 — Sunk cost

Six months ago, the company bought a $500K machine. It now offers a custom job that needs the machine. The $500K is:

  • Sunk — irrelevant to the decision
  • Variable cost of the job
  • Opportunity cost
  • Differential cost
Why: The $500K is gone regardless of the new decision. Sunk costs never affect the future-only choice. Relevant: incremental revenue from the job, incremental variable costs to do it, opportunity cost if the machine could otherwise be used profitably.

Practice 2 — Special order

Capacity is idle. A customer offers $24/unit for 5,000 units of a product that has variable cost $18/unit and allocated fixed cost $4/unit. Accept?

  • Reject — price below full cost
  • Reject — allocated fixed cost not covered
  • Accept — incremental margin $6/unit × 5,000 = $30,000
  • Reject unless price ≥ $22
Why: Idle capacity → fixed costs unchanged → only variable cost is differential. ($24 − $18) × 5,000 = $30K incremental contribution. Allocated fixed cost is irrelevant. Take it.

Practice 3 — Make vs buy

Currently making a part at $12 variable + $4 allocated fixed (of which $2 is avoidable). Outside vendor offers $13. Decision:

  • Make — total cost is $16 vs $13
  • Buy — saves $3/unit
  • Buy — saves $1/unit
  • Make — saves $1/unit (avoidable cost)
Why: Avoidable cost of making = $12 + $2 = $14. Buy at $13 saves $1/unit. The $4 allocated fixed cost trap: only $2 actually goes away if the company stops making the part. Total cost ($16) misleads.
