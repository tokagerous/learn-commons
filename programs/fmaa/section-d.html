Unit 1 · D.1 Measurement

Cost behavior — fixed vs variable, mixed, relevant range

A cost object is anything we want to measure cost for (a unit, a job, a customer, a department). Costs behave in patterns: variable scales with activity, fixed doesn't, mixed has both. Patterns hold only within the relevant range.

§1.1Cost objects, drivers, and direct vs indirect

A cost driver is what causes the cost to vary. A cost is direct if it can be traced to the object economically; otherwise indirect (overhead) and must be allocated.

§1.2Behavior types

TypeTotal cost as activity ↑Per-unit cost as activity ↑Example
VariableIncreases proportionallyConstantDirect materials
FixedConstant (within range)DecreasesFactory rent
MixedIncreases (less than proportionally)Decreases (toward variable rate)Utility bill (base fee + per-kWh)
Step-fixedJumps at thresholdsStep-down then flatOne supervisor per 10 workers

§1.3Relevant range and high-low method

The relevant range is the activity span over which behavior assumptions hold. Outside it, fixed costs may step (need a second machine, a second supervisor), and variable rates may change (volume discounts).

The high-low method separates a mixed cost using only two data points — highest and lowest activity:

Variable rate = (High cost − Low cost) ÷ (High activity − Low activity)
Fixed = Total cost − (Variable rate × Activity) [at either point]
Outliers distort high-low

Using extremes makes the result sensitive to one-off events (a strike month, a crash quarter). Regression analysis uses all data points and is more robust — but high-low is what FMAA tests.

Practice 1 — Per-unit fixed cost

As production volume rises within the relevant range, per-unit fixed cost:

  • Rises
  • Stays the same
  • Falls
  • Doubles
Why: Total fixed is constant; spreading over more units lowers the per-unit charge — the basis of operating leverage.

Practice 2 — High-low

High 12,000 units cost $80K; low 4,000 units cost $40K. Variable rate per unit:

  • $5
  • $3.33
  • $10
  • $6.67
Why: ($80K − $40K) / (12K − 4K) = $40K / 8K = $5/unit. Fixed = $80K − ($5 × 12K) = $20K.

Practice 3 — Mixed cost

Cell phone bill: $40 monthly base + $0.10/min usage. This is:

  • Pure fixed
  • Pure variable
  • Mixed (semi-variable)
  • Step-fixed
Why: Fixed component ($40 base) + variable rate ($0.10/min) = mixed cost.

Practice 4 — Direct vs indirect

A factory supervisor's salary, with respect to ONE product line out of three:

  • Direct cost
  • Variable cost
  • Period cost
  • Indirect (overhead)
Why: Tracing time to one specific product line isn't economical, so the salary is allocated as overhead. With respect to the factory as a whole, it's direct factory cost.

Practice 5 — Step-fixed example

"One quality inspector for every 25 production workers" describes:

  • Variable cost
  • Step-fixed cost
  • Mixed cost
  • Fixed cost (always)
Why: Cost jumps in steps as production crosses thresholds (25, 50, 75 workers). Inside one step, behavior is fixed; across steps, it appears variable. Common for supervision and equipment capacity.
Unit 2 · D.1 Measurement

Actual, normal, and standard costs

Three philosophies for charging production with costs. They differ in when a rate is set (actual vs predetermined) and what the rate represents (last-period actual, this-period budgeted, or an engineered standard).

§2.1Three approaches

MethodDM & DLManufacturing OHProsCons
ActualActualActualReal cost; no estimatesVolatile; lags invoices
NormalActualPredetermined rate × actual activitySmooths OHYear-end variance to clean up
StandardStandard rate × standard quantityStandard rate × standard hoursVariances surface deviationsRequires standard-setting effort

§2.2Predetermined OH rate (normal & standard)

Predetermined rate = Budgeted total OH ÷ Budgeted activity
Applied OH = Predetermined rate × ACTUAL activity

The difference between applied OH and actual OH at year-end is the over- or under-applied overhead. Closed to COGS (small) or prorated across WIP, FG, and COGS (material).

§2.3Standard cost types

  • Currently attainable standard — assumes normal, expected effort and waste. Most common operational benchmark.
  • Theoretical (ideal) standard — perfect efficiency, zero waste. Useful as engineering benchmark, never as bonus target.
  • Basic standard — held constant for years; useful for trend analysis but increasingly stale.
AAU / AAO mnemonic

Actual > Applied = Under-applied (we didn't apply enough; raise COGS). Actual < Applied = Over-applied (we applied too much; lower COGS).

Practice 1 — Predetermined rate

Budget OH $400K; budget DLH 20,000. Predetermined OH rate:

  • $25/DLH
  • $20/DLH
  • $50/DLH
  • $10/DLH
Why: $400K / 20,000 = $20/DLH.

Practice 2 — Under-applied

Actual OH $310K; applied OH $295K. Year-end closing entry to COGS:

  • DR COGS $15K / CR Mfg OH $15K
  • DR Mfg OH $15K / CR COGS $15K
  • DR COGS $295K / CR Mfg OH $295K
  • No entry
Why: Actual ($310K) > Applied ($295K) = under-applied $15K. Raise COGS: DR COGS $15K / CR Mfg OH $15K.

Practice 3 — Standard cost meaning

A "currently attainable" standard:

  • Assumes perfect efficiency with zero waste
  • Equals last year's average actual cost
  • Reflects normal expected effort and waste
  • Stays unchanged for many years
Why: Currently attainable = realistic target with normal effort and acceptable waste. Theoretical/ideal = perfection. Basic = unchanged for years (trend benchmark).

Practice 4 — Use of normal costing

A primary advantage of normal costing over actual costing is that it:

  • Eliminates all year-end OH variance
  • Removes the need for budgets
  • Costs each unit at its actual full cost
  • Smooths the per-unit overhead charge across periods
Why: Normal costing locks the OH rate at the start of the year (predetermined) and multiplies by ACTUAL activity. Idle months no longer inflate per-unit cost; busy months no longer deflate it.
Unit 3 · D.1 Measurement

Product/service costing systems — job-order, process, ABC

Three systems for accumulating product cost. Job-order tracks each unique job. Process averages cost per unit across continuous production. Activity-based costing (ABC) traces overhead to activities and then to products via cost drivers.

§3.1Job-order costing

For unique or batched output (custom homes, audit engagements, ad campaigns). Each job carries its own cost record:

  • Direct materials traced to the job.
  • Direct labor traced to the job.
  • Overhead applied via predetermined rate × actual activity (Unit 2).

Cost flow through three inventory accounts:

DM used = Beg RM + Purchases − End RM
COGM = Beg WIP + DM + DL + Applied OH − End WIP
COGS = Beg FG + COGM − End FG

§3.2Process costing

For high-volume, homogeneous output (paint, soft drinks, gasoline). Costs accumulate by department and divide by equivalent units of production (EUP) — partially completed units expressed as fully-completed equivalents based on % complete.

FIFO and weighted-average are the two main calculation methods; FMAA emphasizes the concept rather than detailed mechanics.

§3.3Activity-based costing (ABC)

Traditional costing pools all overhead and applies one rate (often DLH-based). When products differ in complexity, that one rate distorts. ABC traces overhead to activities (machine setup, quality inspection, order processing), then assigns each pool to products via the activity's cost driver.

ActivityDriverHierarchy
Machine setupNumber of setupsBatch-level
Quality inspectionInspectionsBatch-level
Direct laborLabor hoursUnit-level
Engineering changesEngineering hoursProduct-level
Plant security(can't drive)Facility-level

ABC pays off when (1) overhead is large vs DM/DL, (2) products differ in complexity, and (3) decisions depend on accurate full cost. Specialty / low-volume products under traditional costing usually look cheaper than they really are; ABC reveals the truth.

Practice 1 — Job-order vs process

Which firm is most likely to use process costing?

  • Custom home builder
  • Public accounting firm
  • Soft-drink bottler
  • Movie production studio
Why: A bottler produces millions of identical units through continuous processes — perfect for process costing.

Practice 2 — COGM

Beg WIP $20K; DM used $80K; DL $50K; applied OH $60K; End WIP $25K. COGM:

  • $190K
  • $185K
  • $210K
  • $165K
Why: $20K + $80K + $50K + $60K − $25K = $185K.

Practice 3 — When ABC helps most

ABC provides the GREATEST improvement in product-cost accuracy when:

  • Overhead is large and product complexity varies widely
  • All products use identical batches
  • Direct labor is the largest cost
  • The company sells one product
Why: Traditional costing distorts when overhead is high AND products differ in resource consumption. Otherwise the cost of operating ABC exceeds the accuracy benefit.

Practice 4 — ABC effect on low-volume

Compared with traditional volume-based costing, ABC typically:

  • Lowers low-volume specialty product cost
  • Has no effect on individual product costs
  • Raises high-volume product cost
  • Raises low-volume specialty product cost AND lowers high-volume product cost
Why: Specialty products consume disproportionate setup, engineering, inspection — but traditional costing spreads overhead by labor or volume, masking those costs. ABC reveals them.

Practice 5 — Cost hierarchy

"Engineering changes specific to one product line" is what type of activity?

  • Unit-level
  • Batch-level
  • Product-level (sustaining)
  • Facility-level
Why: The Cooper-Kaplan hierarchy: unit (per-unit), batch (per-batch, e.g. setup), product (per-product-line, e.g. engineering, dedicated marketing), facility (whole plant). Product-level costs are allocated based on which products they support, not units made.
Unit 4 · D.2 Overhead

Variable & fixed overhead — classification

Overhead = production cost that isn't direct materials or direct labor. Splitting it into variable (scales with activity) and fixed (doesn't) is essential for variance analysis, budgeting, and CVP.

§4.1Examples

Variable OHFixed OH
Indirect materials (lubricants, fasteners)Factory rent
Indirect labor (machine operators paid hourly)Plant manager's salary
Machine utility (electricity for production)Equipment depreciation (SL)
Quality inspection consumablesProperty tax on factory
Variable plant maintenance (per machine hour)Insurance on factory

§4.2Mixed overhead

Some OH lines have both components. Utility bills typically have a base service charge + per-unit consumption rate. Maintenance can have a base contract + per-machine-hour. Use high-low or regression to split into fixed + variable per Unit 1.

§4.3Corporate vs departmental

Larger overhead structures distinguish:

  • Plant overhead — supports the whole factory.
  • Departmental overhead — specific to one department.
  • Corporate (G&A) — supports the whole company; often classified as period cost (S&A) rather than product cost.

Allocating corporate G&A to products requires a base (revenue, headcount, asset base) and is often controversial — it's why divisional managers complain about "corporate overhead allocations" eating their margin.

Period vs product

Manufacturing overhead is product cost (capitalized into inventory). Corporate G&A and selling expenses are period costs (expensed when incurred). Don't co-mingle them when computing inventory value.

Practice 1 — Variable OH

Which is most likely a VARIABLE overhead item?

  • Factory rent
  • Plant manager's salary
  • Production-line equipment depreciation
  • Indirect materials (lubricants, fasteners)
Why: Indirect materials scale with output — variable. Rent, salary, depreciation are fixed within the relevant range.

Practice 2 — Fixed OH

A factory's monthly rent of $20,000 regardless of production volume is:

  • Fixed manufacturing overhead
  • Variable manufacturing overhead
  • Direct cost
  • Period cost
Why: Fixed manufacturing OH — unchanged with volume; capitalized into inventory under absorption costing. Don't confuse with corporate G&A (period cost).

Practice 3 — Mixed OH split

High activity 5,000 MH cost $30K; low 2,000 MH cost $18K. Variable OH rate per machine hour:

  • $2.00
  • $3.00
  • $4.00
  • $6.00
Why: ($30K − $18K) / (5,000 − 2,000) = $12K / 3,000 = $4.00/MH. Fixed = $30K − ($4 × 5,000) = $10K.

Practice 4 — Period vs product

A salesperson's commission is classified as:

  • Manufacturing overhead
  • Period (selling) expense
  • Direct labor
  • Direct material
Why: Selling activity = period cost, never inventoried. Manufacturing overhead applies only to factory-floor activities (rent, supervision, utilities, etc.).
Unit 5 · D.2 Overhead

Overhead allocation — plantwide vs departmental

Allocate overhead to products using a base that approximates how each product consumes the underlying resource. The two big choices: which base, and at what level (plantwide or by department)?

§5.1Plantwide vs departmental rates

A plantwide rate uses one base for the whole plant — simple but inaccurate when departments differ. A departmental rate sets a separate rate per department, each with its own appropriate base.

DepartmentBest baseWhy
Machining (capital-intensive)Machine hoursMachines drive depreciation, utilities, maintenance
Assembly (labor-intensive)Direct labor hoursLabor drives most overhead
Procurement-heavyDirect material costMaterial flow drives the OH

§5.2The base should CAUSE the overhead

A plausible base correlates with overhead and ideally drives it. Sales dollars, for instance, rarely drives manufacturing overhead — it's downstream of production. Direct labor hours used to be the universal default; as automation rose, machine hours became more appropriate for many industries.

§5.3Capacity choice for the denominator

  • Theoretical capacity — 100% utilization, no breaks. Unrealistic.
  • Practical capacity — theoretical minus normal downtime. Best for cost management.
  • Normal capacity — average over a few years. Smooths cyclical demand.
  • Master-budget capacity — this year's expected volume.
Recession trap

Using master-budget capacity in a downturn raises the OH rate (less volume to absorb fixed OH), making products look pricier. Price increases drive customers away; demand falls; rate rises further. Practical capacity isolates "cost of unused capacity" as a separate line.

Practice 1 — Allocation base choice

A CNC machining department's overhead is best allocated using:

  • Direct labor hours
  • Sales dollars
  • Machine hours
  • Number of employees
Why: Machine-intensive department → machine hours drive utility, depreciation, maintenance.

Practice 2 — When plantwide fails

A plantwide overhead rate is most likely to distort product cost when:

  • All products use departments equally
  • Departments differ in resource intensity
  • The plant has only one department
  • Direct material is the largest cost
Why: If product X is 90% machined and product Y is 90% assembled, but the plantwide rate is set on labor hours, X is undercosted and Y overcosted. Departmental rates fix it.

Practice 3 — Capacity choice

A company in a cyclical industry wants product costs that don't swing with the cycle. Best denominator:

  • Theoretical
  • Master-budget capacity for the current year
  • Practical capacity
  • Normal capacity
Why: Normal capacity averages activity across the cycle, producing a stable per-unit OH. Master-budget swings with the cycle; theoretical is unrealistic; practical isolates idle-capacity cost separately.

Practice 4 — Predetermined rate computation

Budgeted OH $480K; budgeted MH 24,000. Predetermined rate per MH:

  • $20
  • $24
  • $10
  • $50
Why: $480K / 24,000 = $20/MH. Applied OH then = $20 × actual MH.
Unit 6 · D.3 Variances

Standard costs & the variance framework

A variance is the difference between actual cost and standard cost (or budgeted cost). Variances are the diagnostic engine: they tell you where reality deviated from plan and — crucially — into which components.

§6.1The price/quantity decomposition

For each input (materials, labor, variable OH), the total variance splits into two:

  • Price (or rate) variance — paid more or less per unit of input.
  • Quantity (or efficiency) variance — used more or less of the input than standard.

For fixed overhead, the split is between budget variance (spent more or less than budget) and volume variance (utilized capacity differently than budgeted).

§6.2Sign convention

SignLabelEffect on income
Positive (Actual > Standard)Unfavorable (U)Reduces income
Negative (Actual < Standard)Favorable (F)Increases income

For price/rate: paying MORE per unit is unfavorable. For quantity/efficiency: USING MORE input is unfavorable. (Both interpretations result in the same total cost variance sign.)

§6.3Standard quantity allowed

Critical concept. The standard cost per actual output unit:

Standard quantity allowed = Actual output × Standard input per unit
Standard hours allowed = Actual output × Standard hours per unit

Always compare actual usage against the standard allowed for the actual production — NOT against the original budget standard. This is what makes variance analysis fair to the manager (they're not penalized for producing more or fewer units than planned).

Why decompose?

The TOTAL variance = "actual differs from standard by $X." That alone is uninformative. The decomposition into price and quantity points the investigation: price variance suggests procurement or rate negotiation issues; quantity variance suggests production or yield issues.

Practice 1 — Standard quantity allowed

Standard 4 lb/unit; produced 1,200 units. Standard quantity allowed:

  • 4,800 lbs
  • 3,000 lbs
  • 1,200 lbs
  • 4 lbs
Why: 1,200 × 4 = 4,800 lbs. Standard quantity is computed for actual output, not for the original budget output.

Practice 2 — Sign convention

Actual material cost $50,200; standard for actual production $48,000. Total material variance:

  • $2,200 F
  • $2,200 U
  • $0
  • $48,000 U
Why: Actual ($50,200) − Standard ($48,000) = $2,200. Positive = U (unfavorable). To learn whether it was a price or quantity issue, decompose further.

Practice 3 — Decomposition purpose

Why decompose a total variance into price and quantity components?

  • To make the math look harder
  • For tax compliance
  • To direct investigation toward the right root cause
  • To eliminate the variance
Why: Different sub-variances point to different responsible functions: price variance → procurement; quantity variance → production / yield. Without decomposition, "the variance was $X unfavorable" is unactionable.

Practice 4 — Comparing actual to original budget

Comparing actual costs to the ORIGINAL budgeted costs (not the flexible budget) for variance analysis is:

  • Best practice
  • Required by GAAP
  • Acceptable for fixed costs only
  • Unfair — mixes volume effects with spending efficiency
Why: Actual vs original budget mixes volume (we produced more or fewer units than planned) with efficiency (we were better or worse per unit). Flexible budget at actual volume isolates the latter.
Unit 7 · D.3 Variances

Direct material & direct labor variances

Now apply the price/quantity framework to materials and labor — the two largest controllable cost categories in most manufacturing operations.

§7.1Direct material variances

DM Price Variance = (Actual price − Standard price) × Actual quantity (purchased or used)
DM Quantity Variance = (Actual quantity used − Standard quantity allowed) × Standard price

Note the variance "stayed in the buckets": price variance uses actual quantity (you can only feel the price effect on what you actually bought); quantity variance uses standard price (rate is held constant to isolate the quantity effect).

§7.2Direct labor variances

DL Rate Variance = (Actual rate − Standard rate) × Actual hours
DL Efficiency Variance = (Actual hours − Standard hours allowed) × Standard rate

Same structure as DM — just substitute "rate" for price and "hours" for quantity.

§7.3Common interpretations

Variance patternPossible cause
DM price F + DM quantity UBought cheap material that's harder to work with — more waste
DM price U + DM quantity FBought premium material that yielded better — quality trade-off
DL rate F + DL efficiency UUsed less-skilled (cheaper) labor — slower output
DL rate U + DL efficiency FUsed premium / overtime labor — faster but expensive

Whether the trade-off is favorable depends on totals and product impact (quality, defect rate, customer satisfaction).

Purchase vs use

For DM price variance, "actual quantity" can mean quantity PURCHASED (timing of price recognition) or quantity USED. Most modern systems use purchased quantity — the price effect is recognized when materials arrive, not when consumed. Either is acceptable; just be consistent.

Practice 1 — DM price

Standard $5/lb; actual $4.80/lb; purchased 2,000 lb. DM price variance:

  • $400 F
  • $400 U
  • $0
  • $10,000 F
Why: ($4.80 − $5.00) × 2,000 = -$400 = $400 F. Paid less than standard.

Practice 2 — DM quantity

Standard 3 lb/unit at $5; produced 1,000 units; actually used 3,200 lb. DM quantity variance:

  • $200 F
  • $0
  • $1,000 U
  • $1,000 F
Why: Standard allowed = 1,000 × 3 = 3,000 lb. (3,200 − 3,000) × $5 = $1,000 U.

Practice 3 — DL efficiency

Standard 2 hr/unit at $25; produced 500 units; actual 1,050 hours. DL efficiency variance:

  • $1,500 F
  • $1,250 U
  • $1,250 F
  • $2,500 U
Why: Standard hours allowed = 500 × 2 = 1,000. (1,050 − 1,000) × $25 = $1,250 U. Took 50 hours more than standard.

Practice 4 — Variance interpretation

DM Price F $1,000 and DM Quantity U $1,500. The most likely root cause:

  • Strong supplier negotiation alone
  • Production team wasted material independent of purchasing
  • Both unrelated
  • Bought lower-quality material that wasted more in production — net cost
Why: Classic price/quantity trade-off pattern: cheaper material that's harder to use yielded more waste. Net unfavorable $500 — savings on the wrong dimension. Investigate supplier quality.

Practice 5 — DL rate variance

Standard rate $20/hr; actual rate $22/hr; actual hours 800. DL rate variance:

  • $1,600 U
  • $1,600 F
  • $2,000 U
  • $3,200 U
Why: ($22 − $20) × 800 = $1,600 U. Paid $2/hr more than standard on 800 hours.
Unit 8 · D.3 Variances

Overhead variances — variable spending/efficiency, fixed budget/volume

Variable overhead variances follow the DM/DL pattern. Fixed overhead has its own twist: applied OH uses an estimated rate, so variances split between spending (rate accuracy) and volume (denominator activity).

§8.1Variable OH

VOH Spending = (Actual rate − Standard rate) × Actual hours
VOH Efficiency = (Actual hours − Standard hours allowed) × Standard rate

Identical structure to DL variances, applied to variable overhead. Common driver: machine hours or DLH.

§8.2Fixed OH — two variances, different logic

FOH Budget Variance = Actual FOH − Budgeted FOH
FOH Volume Variance = Budgeted FOH − Applied FOH (= Std rate × Std hours allowed)

Budget variance measures spending performance: did we spend more than we planned? Volume variance measures capacity utilization: did we produce more or fewer units than the level used to set the predetermined rate? Higher actual production = favorable volume variance (more units absorbing the same fixed cost).

§8.3Reconciliation

Total OH variance = Actual OH − Applied OH = Budget variance + Volume variance (for fixed) + Spending + Efficiency (for variable). Each variance answers a different management question.

Volume variance is informational

FOH volume variance reflects how the company allocated its fixed cost across actual output, not how well it controlled costs. A plant running 80% capacity will show unfavorable volume variance even with perfect spending discipline. Don't reflexively treat unfavorable volume as a "problem."

Practice 1 — VOH efficiency

Standard VOH rate $5/DLH; standard hours allowed 2,000; actual hours 2,200. VOH efficiency variance:

  • $1,000 F
  • $1,000 unchanged
  • $1,000 U
  • $11,000 U
Why: (2,200 − 2,000) × $5 = $1,000 U. Took 200 hours more than standard at $5 rate.

Practice 2 — FOH budget variance

Budgeted FOH $40K; actual FOH $43K. Budget variance:

  • $3,000 U
  • $3,000 F
  • $0
  • Cannot be calculated
Why: Actual − Budgeted = $43K − $40K = $3,000 U. Spent more on fixed overhead than planned.

Practice 3 — FOH volume variance source

A favorable fixed overhead VOLUME variance arises when:

  • Actual fixed OH is lower than budgeted
  • Actual rate is lower than standard rate
  • Hours used were less than allowed
  • Actual production exceeded the level used to set the predetermined rate
Why: Volume = Budgeted FOH − Applied FOH. Higher production raises Applied FOH above Budgeted — favorable. The other choices describe budget or efficiency variances, not volume.

Practice 4 — Volume variance limitation

An unfavorable FOH volume variance most often signals:

  • Material spending is out of control
  • Bad-debt expense is rising
  • The plant ran below the capacity used in setting the predetermined rate
  • Workers were less efficient
Why: Volume variance is purely about utilization. Spending and efficiency variances are separate. An 80% capacity plant will show unfavorable volume even with perfect cost discipline.

Practice 5 — VOH spending

Actual VOH rate $4/DLH; standard $3.50/DLH; actual hours 1,500. VOH spending variance:

  • $750 F
  • $750 U
  • $5,250 U
  • $6,000 U
Why: ($4.00 − $3.50) × 1,500 = $0.50 × 1,500 = $750 U. Paid $0.50 more per hour than standard rate.
Unit 9 · D.4 Performance

Profitability analysis — product, BU, customer

Beyond company-level NI, slice profitability finer to drive better decisions. Same lens applies at every level: revenue minus directly attributable cost. The lens choice depends on the decision.

§9.1Three cuts of profit

CutWhat it showsDecisions enabled
Product profitabilityRevenue per SKU − traceable variable + product-specific fixedPricing, product mix, discontinue/keep
Business unit profitabilitySegment revenue − segment-level costCapital allocation, BU bonuses, BU divestiture
Customer profitabilityCustomer revenue − all costs to serve that customerService tiering, customer retention, fire bad customers

§9.2Common patterns

The Pareto pattern is universal:

  • ~80% of profit from ~20% of customers (or products, or business units).
  • ~10-20% of customers may actually LOSE money once full service costs are loaded.
  • "Whale curves" plot cumulative profit by customer rank — typically rises steeply, peaks, then declines as unprofitable customers drag down totals.

§9.3Common analytical traps

  • Allocated overhead distortion — full-cost margins can mislead because allocated overhead may not actually go away if a product is dropped.
  • Joint product over-attribution — selling a luxury item at a "loss" might still be valuable if it drives flagship traffic.
  • Customer acquisition cost amortization — first-year customer "loses money" but earns out over a 5-year retention period.
Action: tier the service model

Once customer profitability is clear, design service tiers matched to value. Premium customers get high-touch; standard customers get self-service. Don't fire unprofitable customers — make them profitable through service economics, or let them go to a lower-cost channel.

Practice 1 — Pareto pattern

A typical customer profitability analysis reveals:

  • A small fraction of customers generates most profit; some are unprofitable to serve
  • All customers contribute roughly equally
  • Customer acquisition cost is irrelevant
  • Service costs are uniform across customers
Why: The 80/20 pattern dominates. Action: tier service to fit profitability, not just revenue.

Practice 2 — Whale curve interpretation

A customer-profitability "whale curve" rising then falling means:

  • The company has steady margins across all customers
  • The curve always rises monotonically
  • Top customers contribute most profit; bottom customers actively destroy profit
  • Customer acquisition is too expensive
Why: The curve rises as profitable customers add to cumulative profit. It peaks when neutral customers (~$0 profit) are reached. It then declines as unprofitable customers (negative profit) drag total down. The "tail" behind the peak represents the value-destroying segment.

Practice 3 — Allocated cost trap

A product line shows a $5K loss based on full cost, including $20K of allocated corporate overhead. The decision to drop it should be based on:

  • Drop — it's losing $5K
  • Keep — it has positive revenue
  • Drop — and reduce all overhead allocations
  • Keep if its contribution margin (revenue − variable cost) covers product-specific fixed costs
Why: Drop only if avoidable cost exceeds revenue. Allocated corporate overhead probably doesn't go away if the product is dropped — it gets reallocated to remaining products. Consider only avoidable costs in drop decisions.

Practice 4 — CLV view

A new customer cost $500 to acquire and earned $100 profit in year 1. Over a 5-year retention, the customer is expected to earn $1,000 total profit. The CFO should view year 1 as:

  • A failure — $400 net loss
  • An investment — amortize acquisition over expected customer lifetime
  • Irrelevant
  • Indistinguishable from a one-time customer
Why: Customer-acquisition cost is an investment that earns out over the customer's lifetime. Year-1 P&L misleads. Customer-lifetime-value (CLV) analysis matches acquisition cost against expected lifetime contribution.
Unit 10 · D.4 Performance

Return on investment (ROI) and responsibility centers

ROI measures profit per dollar of invested capital, putting investments on a common scale. Responsibility centers structure the organization so each manager is held accountable for what they actually control.

§10.1ROI

ROI = Net income (or operating income) ÷ Average investment (or assets)
= Profit margin × Asset turnover (DuPont 2-step)

Used to compare divisions, projects, capital investments. Easy to compute; widely understood.

§10.2ROI's structural weakness

Managers may reject value-creating projects whose return is below their unit's current ROI — accepting drags the average down. Example:

  • Division ROI: 25%. Cost of capital: 12%.
  • New project: ROI 18% (above cost of capital — value-creating).
  • If accepted, division ROI averages down. Manager rejects to protect bonus.
  • The company loses 6% above cost of capital × project size.

§10.3Residual income (RI) — fixes ROI's incentive

RI = Net income − (Required return × Investment)

RI rewards any project earning above the required return, regardless of effect on average ROI. The CMA prefers RI for divisional evaluation; ROI is still ubiquitous because it's intuitive.

§10.4Responsibility centers

TypeManager controlsManager evaluated on
Cost centerCosts onlySpending efficiency, variances
Revenue centerRevenue onlySales targets, mix
Profit centerBoth revenues and costsOperating income
Investment centerRevenues, costs, AND investment baseROI, RI, EVA

Match the metric to the level of control: don't penalize a cost-center manager for revenue shortfalls; don't expect a revenue-center manager to manage capital allocation.

Controllability principle

Hold managers accountable only for what they meaningfully control. Allocating uncontrollable corporate overhead into divisional ROI/RI calculations introduces noise and erodes the trust the metric requires.

Practice 1 — ROI

Operating income $90K; average operating assets $600K. ROI:

  • 10%
  • 15%
  • 20%
  • 6.67%
Why: $90K / $600K = 15%.

Practice 2 — ROI dilution

A division at 20% ROI (cost of capital 10%) considers a project that would earn 14%. Under ROI evaluation, the manager will likely:

  • Accept — 14% > 10% cost of capital
  • Decline — 14% is below the required return
  • Decline — accepting drags the divisional ROI down
  • Be indifferent
Why: 14% < current 20% ROI, so accepting reduces divisional ROI. Bonus tied to ROI gives the manager an incentive to reject — even though 14% > 10% is value-creating. Classic dilution problem; RI fixes it.

Practice 3 — RI calculation

NI $80K; investment $500K; required return 12%. Residual income:

  • $20,000
  • $80,000
  • $500,000
  • −$60,000
Why: RI = $80K − (12% × $500K) = $80K − $60K = $20K. Positive RI = earning above required return.

Practice 4 — Center type

A division manager controls revenues, operating costs, AND can recommend capital investments evaluated against a hurdle rate. Best classified as:

  • Cost center
  • Revenue center
  • Profit center
  • Investment center
Why: Investment center: controls inputs, outputs, AND the asset base. Evaluated on ROI/RI/EVA. Profit center stops at operating income; cost/revenue centers control only their respective dimension.
Unit 11 · D.5 Decisions

Breakeven & CVP analysis

Cost-volume-profit (CVP) analysis links volume, contribution margin, and operating income. The breakeven point is the volume where total revenue equals total cost — zero operating profit. Beyond breakeven, every additional unit's CM falls to OI.

§11.1Core formulas

Contribution margin (CM) = Sales − Variable cost
Unit CM = Selling price − Variable cost per unit
CM ratio = CM ÷ Sales (or Unit CM ÷ Selling price)
Breakeven units = Fixed cost ÷ Unit CM
Breakeven sales $ = Fixed cost ÷ CM ratio
Target profit units = (Fixed + Target profit) ÷ Unit CM

§11.2Margin of safety

Margin of safety ($) = Actual sales − Breakeven sales
Margin of safety (%) = MOS $ ÷ Actual sales

Margin of safety = how much sales can drop before the company breaks even. High MOS = safer; low MOS = vulnerable.

§11.3Operating leverage

Degree of operating leverage = Contribution margin ÷ Operating income

Tells you the % change in OI for a 1% change in sales. High leverage (capital-intensive industries: airlines, telecoms) means small sales changes drive big OI changes — sword that cuts both ways.

§11.4CVP assumptions

  • Costs are linear in the relevant range.
  • Single product (or constant mix for multi-product).
  • Selling price is constant.
  • Inventory is constant (production = sales) — relevant for absorption costing.
Multi-product CVP

For a multi-product company, breakeven uses the weighted-average CM based on the standard sales mix. Change the mix and breakeven changes — even with no change in volumes, prices, or costs.

Practice 1 — Breakeven units

Selling price $50; variable cost $30; fixed $80,000. Breakeven units:

  • 4,000
  • 2,667
  • 1,600
  • 2,000
Why: Unit CM = $50 − $30 = $20. BE = $80K / $20 = 4,000 units.

Practice 2 — CM ratio

Sales $500K; variable costs $300K. CM ratio:

  • 30%
  • 60%
  • 40%
  • 67%
Why: CM = $500K − $300K = $200K. CM ratio = $200K / $500K = 40%.

Practice 3 — Target profit

Same data as Q1; target profit $40,000. Required sales units:

  • 4,000
  • 5,000
  • 2,000
  • 6,000
Why: ($80K + $40K) / $20 = 6,000. Above breakeven (4,000), each additional unit's CM falls to OI.

Practice 4 — Margin of safety

Actual sales $500K; breakeven sales $400K. Margin of safety:

  • $100K, 25%
  • $100K, 20%
  • $400K, 80%
  • $0
Why: MOS $ = $500K − $400K = $100K. MOS % = $100K / $500K = 20%. Sales can drop 20% before the company breaks even.

Practice 5 — Operating leverage

CM $300K; operating income $100K. Degree of operating leverage:

  • 0.33
  • 1.0
  • 3.0
  • $200K
Why: $300K / $100K = 3.0×. A 10% sales change yields ~30% OI change. High leverage industries (airlines, software) tend to show DOL of 3-5+.
Unit 12 · D.5 Decisions

Sunk costs, opportunity costs, marginal analysis

When a manager evaluates an option (special order, make-vs-buy, sell-or-process-further), only the costs and revenues that change with the decision matter. The discipline of identifying these — and ignoring sunk costs — is marginal analysis.

§12.1Three cost concepts

  • Sunk cost — already spent; never relevant to a future decision.
  • Opportunity cost — value of the best foregone alternative; ALWAYS relevant.
  • Avoidable / differential cost — changes between options; what marginal analysis weighs.

§12.2Common decisions

DecisionCompareWatch out for
Special orderIncremental revenue vs incremental variable cost (and any opportunity cost of capacity)Don't anchor on full cost; check capacity
Make-vs-buyAvoidable cost of making vs purchase priceAllocated fixed cost may not actually be avoidable
Sell or process furtherIncremental revenue vs separable processing costJoint cost is sunk and irrelevant
Drop a segmentLost contribution margin vs avoidable fixed costAllocated overhead doesn't disappear
Add a productIncremental revenue vs incremental cost (incl. opportunity cost)Cannibalization of existing products

§12.3Capacity-constrained decisions

When capacity is fully utilized, accepting a special order means giving up some current production. Use contribution margin per unit of constrained resource to rank competing uses:

CM per machine hour (or per labor hour, or per pound of scarce material) = Unit CM ÷ Resource per unit

Make products with the highest CM per scarce resource until capacity is exhausted.

Two common traps

(1) Treating allocated fixed cost as if it would disappear with the decision. Most allocated overhead persists. (2) Ignoring opportunity cost. Idle capacity has no opportunity cost; busy capacity does.

Practice 1 — Sunk cost

Six months ago, the company bought a $500K specialized machine. It is now considering a custom job that would use the machine. The $500K is:

  • Sunk — irrelevant
  • Variable cost of the job
  • Opportunity cost
  • Differential cost
Why: The $500K is gone regardless of the decision. Sunk costs never affect the future-only choice. Relevant: incremental revenue from the job, incremental variable costs, opportunity cost if the machine could otherwise be used profitably.

Practice 2 — Special order with idle capacity

Idle capacity. A customer offers $24/unit for 5,000 units of a product that costs $18 variable + $4 allocated fixed. Accept?

  • Reject — price below full cost
  • Reject — fixed not covered
  • Accept — incremental margin $6/unit × 5,000 = $30,000
  • Reject unless price ≥ $22
Why: Idle capacity → fixed costs unchanged → only variable cost is differential. ($24 − $18) × 5,000 = $30K incremental. Take it. Allocated fixed is irrelevant.

Practice 3 — Make-vs-buy

Currently making a part at $12 variable + $4 allocated fixed (of which $2 is avoidable). Outside vendor offers $13. Decision:

  • Make — total cost is $16 vs $13
  • Buy — saves $3/unit
  • Buy — saves $1/unit
  • Make — saves $1/unit (vs avoidable $14)
Why: Avoidable cost of making = $12 + $2 = $14. Buy at $13 saves $1/unit, BUT the question is asking direction: "make at $14 or buy at $13?" Buy saves $1. Wait — re-read: "Make saves $1/unit (vs avoidable $14)" is comparing $14 making vs $13 buying — buying is cheaper by $1, so MAKING does NOT save. The correct answer is choice 2: BUY saves $1/unit.

Practice 4 — Process further

A joint product can be sold at split-off for $20K, or processed further at $7K cost and sold for $30K. Decision:

  • Process further — $3K incremental margin
  • Sell at split-off — separable cost too high
  • Process further only if joint cost < $3K
  • Sell at split-off — joint cost is sunk
Why: Incremental analysis: ($30K − $20K) − $7K = +$3K margin from further processing. Joint cost is sunk and irrelevant.

Practice 5 — Constrained resource ranking

Product A: CM $10/unit, 0.5 machine hours. Product B: CM $15/unit, 1.0 machine hour. Capacity is the constraint. Make:

  • B — higher CM per unit
  • A — higher CM per machine hour ($20 vs $15)
  • Equal mix
  • Cannot be determined without fixed costs
Why: CM per machine hour: A = $10 / 0.5 = $20; B = $15 / 1 = $15. A wins on the constrained resource. Capacity-constrained decisions rank by CM per unit of scarce resource, not absolute CM.
