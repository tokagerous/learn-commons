Unit 1 · B.1 Recognition & Valuation

Asset valuation

Most assets enter the books at historical cost — what was paid. Subsequent measurement may shift to fair value, lower-of-cost-or-NRV, or impaired value depending on asset type and circumstance.

§1.1Common measurement bases

AssetInitialSubsequent
Cash & equivalentsFace valueFace value
AR (net)InvoiceInvoice − allowance for doubtful accounts
Inventory (US GAAP)CostLower of cost or NRV (LCNRV)
PP&ECostCost − accumulated depreciation, tested for impairment
Trading securitiesFVFair value through P&L
AFS securitiesFVFair value through OCI
GoodwillCost (purchase)Tested for impairment annually; not amortized

§1.2Impairment

An asset is impaired when its carrying amount exceeds the recoverable amount. US GAAP for PP&E uses a two-step test: (1) is undiscounted future cash flow < carrying? (2) if yes, write down to fair value. Impairment losses generally cannot be reversed under US GAAP (IFRS allows reversals, except for goodwill).

Practice 1 — Inventory rule

Under US GAAP, inventory measured using FIFO is reported at:

  • Fair value
  • Replacement cost
  • Lower of cost or net realizable value
  • Selling price
Why: US GAAP uses LCNRV (Lower of Cost or NRV) for FIFO/average inventory. NRV = estimated selling price − completion and disposal costs. Older rule LCM (Lower of Cost or Market) still applies for LIFO.

Practice 2 — AFS securities

Available-for-sale debt securities are measured subsequently at:

  • Fair value, with unrealized G/L through OCI
  • Amortized cost
  • Fair value, with unrealized G/L through net income
  • Lower of cost or fair value
Why: AFS = fair value, but unrealized changes flow through OCI (not P&L). Trading securities flow through P&L. Held-to-maturity uses amortized cost. The OCI parking lot smooths unrealized swings out of NI.

Practice 3 — Goodwill

Under US GAAP, goodwill is:

  • Amortized over 10 years
  • Amortized over its useful life
  • Written down to fair value annually
  • Tested for impairment annually; not amortized
Why: Public-company goodwill is not amortized; it's tested for impairment at least annually (and on triggering events). Private companies may elect to amortize. IFRS aligns with US GAAP on no amortization for public companies.
Unit 2 · B.1 Recognition & Valuation

Liability valuation & equity transactions

Liabilities are present obligations to transfer economic benefit. Most are measured at the present value of future cash payments (long-term debt) or at face (short-term payables). Equity records contributions, retained earnings, and treasury stock.

§2.1Liability measurement

  • Accounts payable / accrued expenses → invoice or face value.
  • Bonds payable → present value at issuance (usually face × discount factor); amortized to face at maturity.
  • Notes payable → PV; if non-interest-bearing, impute interest.
  • Provisions (warranty, restructuring) → best estimate, often expected value or PV of likely outcomes.
  • Contingent liabilities → accrue if probable AND reasonably estimable; otherwise disclose.

§2.2Equity transactions

  • Common stock issued → CR Common Stock (par) + CR Additional Paid-in Capital (excess).
  • Treasury stock (cost method) → DR Treasury Stock; reduces total equity. Reissuance G/L flows through APIC, not P&L.
  • Cash dividends → DR Retained Earnings on declaration; CR Dividends Payable.
  • Stock dividends (small <25%) → reclassify RE to common stock + APIC at fair value.
  • Stock splits → memo entry only; par value adjusted, no journal effect on totals.

Practice 1 — Contingent liability

A pending lawsuit is "reasonably possible" of resulting in a $200K loss. Under US GAAP, the company:

  • Records a $200K liability
  • Discloses the contingency in notes; no liability accrued
  • Records and footnote-discloses
  • Ignores the contingency
Why: Accrual requires "probable" + reasonably estimable. "Reasonably possible" gets footnote disclosure only. "Remote" is ignored. The threshold language matters: probable ≈ likely (>70% under most interpretations); reasonably possible is more than remote but less than probable.

Practice 2 — Treasury stock

A company buys back 1,000 shares at $50 (par $1). The journal entry includes:

  • DR Common Stock $1,000
  • CR Treasury Stock $50,000
  • DR Treasury Stock $50,000 / CR Cash $50,000
  • DR Retained Earnings $50,000
Why: Cost-method buyback: DR Treasury Stock $50K (1,000 × $50) / CR Cash $50K. Treasury stock is contra-equity; reduces total stockholders' equity. Par value isn't touched — that lives in Common Stock.

Practice 3 — Stock split

A 2-for-1 stock split:

  • Doubles shares outstanding; halves par value; total equity unchanged
  • Doubles shares outstanding; doubles total equity
  • Reduces retained earnings by the par value × new shares
  • Requires a journal entry to APIC
Why: A split is a memo entry — no balance-sheet amounts change. Each holder owns twice as many shares at half the par. Stock dividends, in contrast, do reclassify within equity (RE → CS/APIC).
Unit 3 · B.1 Recognition & Valuation

Revenue recognition & income measurement

ASC 606 / IFRS 15 unified revenue recognition under one framework: revenue is recognized when control of goods or services transfers to the customer, in the amount the entity expects to be entitled to. The famous five steps structure the analysis.

§3.1The five-step model

  1. Identify the contract with the customer.
  2. Identify performance obligations (distinct goods/services).
  3. Determine the transaction price (consider variable consideration, financing).
  4. Allocate the price to performance obligations (relative standalone selling price).
  5. Recognize revenue as each obligation is satisfied (point-in-time vs over-time).

§3.2Point-in-time vs over-time

Most product sales transfer control at a point in time (delivery). Long-term construction or service contracts often transfer over time (e.g., the customer simultaneously receives benefit). Over-time uses an input method (cost-to-cost) or output method (units delivered).

% complete (cost-to-cost) = Cost incurred to date ÷ Total estimated cost
Revenue recognized = % complete × Total contract price − Revenue already recognized

Practice 1 — Five-step order

Under ASC 606, the FINAL step in revenue recognition is:

  • Identify the contract
  • Identify performance obligations
  • Determine transaction price
  • Recognize revenue when (or as) performance obligations are satisfied
Why: Steps in order: (1) contract, (2) POs, (3) price, (4) allocate, (5) recognize. The fifth step is where revenue actually hits the income statement.

Practice 2 — Cost-to-cost

Long-term contract: $10M total price, total estimated cost $8M. By year-end, $4M has been incurred. Revenue to recognize:

  • $4,000,000
  • $5,000,000
  • $10,000,000
  • $8,000,000
Why: % complete = $4M ÷ $8M = 50%. Revenue = 50% × $10M = $5M. Gross profit recognized = $5M − $4M = $1M. Loss on overall contract requires immediate full recognition.

Practice 3 — Variable consideration

A contract has a base price of $1,000 plus a $200 performance bonus probable of being earned. Transaction price most likely:

  • $1,200 — include the variable consideration if highly probable
  • $1,000 — variable consideration is never included
  • $1,200 — always include the maximum
  • $0 — defer until paid
Why: ASC 606 includes variable consideration in the transaction price using either expected-value or most-likely-amount method, BUT only to the extent it's highly probable that a significant reversal will not occur. "Probable" earned + bonus → include $200.
Unit 4 · B.2 Common-Size & Base-Year

Common-size statements & common base-year

Two analytical lenses on the same statements. Vertical (common-size) expresses each line as a % of a base on the same statement. Horizontal (common-base-year) tracks each line as a % of its value in a chosen base year.

§4.1Vertical (common-size)

  • Income statement: each line as % of net sales.
  • Balance sheet: each line as % of total assets.

Use case: comparing companies of different sizes (Walmart vs the corner store), or comparing one company to industry benchmarks.

§4.2Horizontal (common base-year)

Pick a base year (e.g. 5 years ago) and divide each subsequent year's line by the base. A line that grew from 100 → 130 over 5 years has a 30% cumulative growth (or ~5.4% CAGR).

CAGR = (Ending ÷ Beginning)(1/n) − 1
where n = number of years
Use the right one

Vertical answers "what is this company's structure?" Horizontal answers "how has this company changed?" Most analyses combine both.

Practice 1 — Common-size base

On a common-size income statement, each line is expressed as a percentage of:

  • Net sales
  • Total assets
  • Total equity
  • Operating income
Why: IS common-size base = revenue. BS common-size base = total assets. Each is a "size-anchor" appropriate to the statement.

Practice 2 — Cross-company comparison

An analyst wants to compare gross-margin structure between a $50B retailer and a $500M retailer. The most appropriate tool is:

  • Horizontal analysis
  • Absolute dollar comparison
  • Vertical (common-size) analysis
  • Quick ratio
Why: Common-size makes scale irrelevant. Both companies' COGS as % of revenue can be compared apples-to-apples. Absolute dollars are meaningless across vastly different sizes.

Practice 3 — CAGR

Revenue grew from $100M (year 0) to $200M (year 5). CAGR is approximately:

  • 20%
  • 15%
  • 10%
  • 50%
Why: CAGR = (200/100)^(1/5) − 1 = 2^0.2 − 1 ≈ 1.149 − 1 = 14.9% ≈ 15%. The total growth was 100% over 5 years, but compounding takes the per-year rate down to ~15%. The 20% trap is just 100%/5.
Unit 5 · B.3 Ratio Analysis

Liquidity ratios

Liquidity = ability to meet short-term obligations as they come due. Three ratios, increasing strictness: current (all CA), quick / acid-test (CA minus inventory and prepaid), cash ratio (cash + securities only).

§5.1The three ratios

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities
Quick (acid-test) = (Cash + Marketable securities + AR) ÷ Current liabilities
Cash ratio = (Cash + Marketable securities) ÷ Current liabilities

Rule-of-thumb benchmarks: current ratio > 1 healthy, > 2 conservative; quick ratio > 1 strong; cash ratio > 0.5 unusually conservative. Industry matters — grocery stores can run lower current ratios because inventory turns fast.

§5.2Working capital and the cash conversion cycle

Working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities (a $ amount, not a ratio)
Cash conversion cycle = DIO + DSO − DPO

Lower CCC = less cash tied up in operations. Negative CCC (Amazon, Apple) means suppliers finance the business.

Practice 1 — Quick ratio components

The quick (acid-test) ratio EXCLUDES which current asset?

  • Cash
  • Accounts receivable
  • Inventory
  • Marketable securities
Why: Quick excludes inventory (and prepaid) because those are slowest to convert to cash. The numerator is cash + marketable securities + net AR.

Practice 2 — Effect of paying AP

A company with current ratio 1.5 pays $20K of accounts payable using cash. Current ratio after the payment is:

  • Lower
  • Higher
  • Unchanged
  • Cannot be determined
Why: Both numerator (CA) and denominator (CL) drop by $20K. Since the ratio > 1, equal $-reductions raise the ratio. Test: 150/100 = 1.50 → 130/80 = 1.625. Symmetric truth: paying AP when ratio < 1 makes it worse.

Practice 3 — CCC interpretation

A negative cash conversion cycle (CCC < 0) means:

  • Suppliers finance the company's operations
  • The company is insolvent
  • DSO exceeds DPO
  • The company is in default on its payables
Why: Negative CCC: the company collects from customers BEFORE it pays suppliers. Net effect: suppliers fund the business. Amazon and Apple are textbook examples — favorable supplier terms create a permanent free working-capital float.
Unit 6 · B.3 Ratio Analysis

Leverage & activity ratios

Leverage measures debt usage and the firm's ability to service it. Activity (turnover) measures how efficiently assets generate sales — how hard the balance sheet is working.

§6.1Leverage

Debt-to-equity = Total debt ÷ Total equity
Debt-to-assets = Total debt ÷ Total assets
Times-interest-earned (TIE) = EBIT ÷ Interest expense
Equity multiplier = Total assets ÷ Total equity

Higher leverage amplifies ROE in good times AND losses in bad times. TIE > 3 is generally comfortable; below 2 raises concerns about debt service.

§6.2Activity / turnover

Inventory turnover = COGS ÷ Average inventory; Days inventory outstanding (DIO) = 365 ÷ turnover
AR turnover = Net credit sales ÷ Average AR; DSO = 365 ÷ turnover
AP turnover = Purchases ÷ Average AP; DPO = 365 ÷ turnover
Total asset turnover = Sales ÷ Average total assets
Watch the numerator

Inventory turnover uses COGS (a cost), not sales. AR turnover uses sales. Mix them up and ratios become meaningless. Same idea for AP — use purchases or COGS, not sales.

Practice 1 — TIE

EBIT $300K; interest expense $50K. Times-interest-earned is:

  • 6.0×
  • 5.0×
  • 0.17×
  • $250K
Why: TIE = EBIT / Interest = $300K / $50K = 6.0×. EBIT covers interest 6 times — comfortable. Bond covenants often require TIE > 2 or 3.

Practice 2 — Inventory turnover

COGS $480K; average inventory $80K. Days inventory outstanding (DIO):

  • 167 days
  • 30 days
  • 61 days
  • 6 days
Why: Turnover = $480K / $80K = 6×. DIO = 365 / 6 = ~61 days. The 167 trap = 365 / (480/80) reversed; the 30 trap = misuse of 360/12.

Practice 3 — Equity multiplier

A company with a higher equity multiplier:

  • Has lower leverage
  • Cannot earn ROE above ROA
  • Has more equity per dollar of assets
  • Has more debt per dollar of equity (higher financial leverage)
Why: Equity multiplier = Assets / Equity. Higher means more assets per dollar of equity, financed by debt — i.e., more leverage. Connects to DuPont: ROE = Net margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier.
Unit 7 · B.3 Ratio Analysis

Profitability ratios & DuPont

Margins answer "how much profit per dollar of sales?" Returns answer "how much profit per dollar of investment?" The DuPont decomposition splits ROE into the levers driving it.

§7.1Margins and returns

Gross margin = Gross profit ÷ Net sales
Operating margin = Operating income ÷ Net sales
Net profit margin = Net income ÷ Net sales
ROA = Net income ÷ Average total assets
ROE = Net income ÷ Average equity

§7.2DuPont decomposition

ROE = (NI / Sales) × (Sales / Avg Assets) × (Avg Assets / Avg Equity)
= Net margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier

Three levers: profitability, efficiency, leverage. A management team raising ROE should be able to point to which lever they pulled. A 5-step extension splits net margin further: tax burden × interest burden × operating margin.

Strategic read

Walmart and Costco have low margins but high turnover (volume strategy). Tiffany has high margins and low turnover (luxury strategy). DuPont surfaces this strategic difference cleanly.

Practice 1 — Gross margin

Net sales $500K; COGS $300K. Gross margin is:

  • 40%
  • 60%
  • 67%
  • 200%
Why: Gross profit = $500K − $300K = $200K. Gross margin = $200K / $500K = 40%. The 60% trap is COGS as % of sales (the inverse complement).

Practice 2 — DuPont

Net margin 5%; asset turnover 2.0; equity multiplier 1.5. ROE is:

  • 5%
  • 10%
  • 15%
  • 20%
Why: ROE = 5% × 2.0 × 1.5 = 15%. The product of the three levers IS ROE. Math check: 5% × 2 = 10% (= ROA), then × 1.5 multiplier = 15%.

Practice 3 — Strategy classification

A discount retailer typically has:

  • High net margin and low asset turnover
  • High net margin and high asset turnover
  • Low net margin and low asset turnover
  • Low net margin and high asset turnover
Why: Volume strategy: thin margins, fast turns. Walmart's ~3% net margin × ~2.5 asset turnover × ~3 equity multiplier ≈ 22% ROE. Tiffany has the opposite mix at the same eventual ROE.

  • $1,250,000

  • $5,000,000
Why: % complete = $1M / $4M = 25%. Rev = 25% × $5M = $1.25M. Output methods (units delivered) might give a different %.

On a common-size BALANCE SHEET, total assets equals:

  • 100%

Why: BS common-size base = total assets, set to 100%. IS common-size base = net sales.
Question 8Quick ratio

Cash $30K; Mkt Sec $20K; AR $50K; Inventory $80K; CL $100K. Quick ratio:

  • 1.80
  • 1.00
  • 0.50
  • 0.30
Why: Quick = ($30K + $20K + $50K) / $100K = $100K / $100K = 1.00. Inventory excluded.
Question 9Current ratio

A company with current ratio > 1 collects $30K from customers. The current ratio:

  • Decreases
  • Increases
  • Is unchanged
  • Cannot be determined
Why: Cash up $30K, AR down $30K — net change in CA is zero. CL unchanged. Ratio unchanged. Tricky exam staple.

Net credit sales $730K; average AR $60K. DSO is:

  • 12 days
  • 20 days
  • 365 days
  • 30 days
Why: Turnover = $730K / $60K ≈ 12.17×. DSO = 365 / 12.17 ≈ 30 days.
Question 11DuPont

Net margin 8%; asset turnover 1.5; equity multiplier 2.0. ROE:

  • 16%
  • 20%
  • 24%
  • 30%
Why: 8% × 1.5 × 2.0 = 24%. ROA would be 8% × 1.5 = 12%, levered up to 24% by the multiplier.
Question 12Strategy

A firm with low net margin and high asset turnover is most likely a:

  • Discount retailer (volume strategy)
  • Luxury jeweler
  • Software-as-a-service vendor
  • Pharmaceutical innovator
Why: Volume strategy = thin margins, fast turns. Luxury / SaaS / pharma rely on high margins and slower asset turns. ROE achieved by different mixes of margin × turnover × leverage.
