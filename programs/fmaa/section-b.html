Unit 1 · B.1 Recognition & Valuation

Asset valuation — cash, AR, and the allowance method

Most assets enter the books at historical cost — what was paid. Subsequent measurement may shift to fair value, lower-of-cost-or-NRV, or impaired value. This unit covers the simplest assets — cash, receivables, and the allowance for doubtful accounts.

§1.1Cash and equivalents

Cash on hand, demand deposits, and instruments convertible to cash within 90 days (Treasury bills, money-market funds). Compensating balances required by lenders are typically classified separately or disclosed.

§1.2Accounts receivable and the allowance

AR is reported at net realizable value — gross AR minus an allowance for amounts management estimates won't be collected.

Net AR = Gross AR − Allowance for Doubtful Accounts

Two methods to estimate the allowance:

  • Percentage of credit sales (income-statement focus) — bad-debt expense = % × credit sales for the period.
  • Aging of receivables (balance-sheet focus) — apply graduated % rates to current/30/60/90+ AR buckets to estimate ending allowance.

§1.3Write-off and recovery

EventJournal entryNet AR effect
Estimate bad debtDR Bad Debt Exp / CR Allowance↓ (allowance ↑)
Specific account written offDR Allowance / CR ARNone (both ↓)
Recovery of written-off accountDR AR / CR Allowance, then DR Cash / CR ARNone initially
Direct vs allowance

Direct write-off (DR Bad Debt Exp / CR AR when an account is determined uncollectible) violates the matching principle and is acceptable only when bad debts are immaterial — never under U.S. GAAP for material amounts.

Practice 1 — Net AR after write-off

Gross AR $400K; allowance $30K. The company writes off a specific $5K account. Net AR after the write-off:

  • $365,000
  • $395,000
  • $370,000 — unchanged
  • $425,000
Why: Both gross AR ($400K → $395K) and allowance ($30K → $25K) drop by $5K. Net = $395K − $25K = $370K, identical to before ($400K − $30K). The bad-debt expense was already recognized when the allowance was set up.

Practice 2 — Bad-debt expense (% of sales)

Credit sales $2M; estimate is 1% will be uncollectible. Bad-debt expense for the period:

  • $20,000
  • $2,000
  • $200,000
  • Cannot be determined without aging schedule
Why: 1% × $2M = $20,000. Percentage-of-sales sets the EXPENSE directly; the allowance balance becomes whatever it becomes. Aging-method, in contrast, sets the ENDING allowance and backs into the expense.

Practice 3 — Aging method

Aging schedule estimates required ending allowance of $50K. Current allowance balance is $12K (credit). Bad-debt expense to record:

  • $50,000
  • $62,000
  • $12,000
  • $38,000
Why: Need to TOP UP the allowance from $12K to $50K — adjust by $38,000. Aging gives the target ENDING balance; the expense is the plug. If allowance had a debit balance (over-written-off), you'd add to bring it up.

Practice 4 — Direct write-off rule

The direct write-off method:

  • Is preferred under U.S. GAAP
  • Violates the matching principle and is acceptable only when bad debts are immaterial
  • Records bad debt expense based on aging
  • Always overstates AR
Why: The matching principle requires expenses recognized in the period the related revenue is earned. Direct write-off recognizes bad debt only when discovered — sometimes years later. Acceptable for tax and immaterial-impact cases; not for GAAP financial reporting when material.
Unit 2 · B.1 Recognition & Valuation

Inventory valuation — FIFO, LIFO, weighted-average, LCNRV

Inventory is recorded at cost when acquired. The cost-flow assumption decides which units' costs flow to COGS when items sell, and which stay on the balance sheet. Then a valuation rule (LCNRV under U.S. GAAP for FIFO/avg) writes inventory down if its market value falls below cost.

§2.1Three cost-flow assumptions

MethodCOGS reflectsEnding inventory reflectsRising prices effect
FIFO (first-in, first-out)Oldest costsNewest costsLower COGS, higher NI, higher ending inv
LIFO (last-in, first-out, U.S. GAAP only)Newest costsOldest costsHigher COGS, lower NI, lower taxes
Weighted-averageAverage costAverage costBetween FIFO and LIFO
Specific identificationEach item's actual costActual costUsed for unique high-value items (cars, art)
Weighted-average cost per unit = Total cost of goods available ÷ Total units available

§2.2LCNRV (U.S. GAAP, FIFO/avg)

If net realizable value (selling price − completion/disposal costs) falls below cost, write inventory down to NRV. The write-down hits COGS or a separate loss account; recovery (later increases) cannot reverse the prior write-down under U.S. GAAP. IFRS allows reversals up to original cost.

LIFO conformity

If a U.S. company uses LIFO for tax, IRS rules require LIFO for financial statements too — the "LIFO conformity rule." This is a major reason LIFO persists despite IFRS prohibiting it.

Practice 1 — FIFO COGS

Beg inventory: 100 units @ $10. Purchase: 200 units @ $12. Sold 250 units. Under FIFO, COGS:

  • $2,800
  • $3,000
  • $2,750
  • $2,500
Why: FIFO sells oldest first: 100 @ $10 = $1,000, then 150 @ $12 = $1,800. COGS = $2,800. Ending = 50 × $12 = $600.

Practice 2 — LIFO COGS

Same data as Practice 1. Under LIFO, COGS:

  • $2,800
  • $3,000
  • $2,900
  • $2,500
Why: LIFO sells newest first: 200 @ $12 = $2,400, then 50 @ $10 = $500. COGS = $2,900. Ending = 50 × $10 = $500. Notice LIFO COGS > FIFO COGS in rising-price periods.

Practice 3 — Weighted-average

Same data. Under weighted-average, COGS (rounded):

  • $2,800
  • $2,833
  • $2,900
  • $2,750
Why: Avg cost = (100 × $10 + 200 × $12) / 300 = $3,400 / 300 = $11.333. COGS = 250 × $11.333 = $2,833. Sits between FIFO ($2,800) and LIFO ($2,900).

Practice 4 — Rising prices effect

In a period of rising prices, FIFO produces:

  • Higher COGS than LIFO
  • Lower ending inventory than LIFO
  • Same NI as LIFO
  • Higher NI than LIFO and higher ending inventory
Why: FIFO uses oldest (cheaper) costs in COGS → lower COGS → higher NI. Ending inventory holds the newer (pricier) costs → higher BS value. The mirror image is LIFO's tax advantage in rising prices: higher COGS → lower taxable income.

Practice 5 — LCNRV

Inventory cost $50K; estimated selling price $48K; estimated disposal costs $3K. Write down to:

  • $45,000 (NRV)
  • $48,000 (selling price)
  • $50,000 (cost — no write-down)
  • $53,000
Why: NRV = $48K − $3K = $45K. NRV ($45K) < cost ($50K), so write down to $45K. Loss = $5K. Under U.S. GAAP this loss cannot reverse if NRV later recovers. IFRS would allow reversal up to original $50K.
Unit 3 · B.1 Recognition & Valuation

PP&E and intangibles — depreciation, impairment, goodwill

Long-lived assets enter the books at historical cost (purchase price + costs to ready for use). Tangible PP&E is depreciated; intangibles are amortized (if finite-lived) or impairment-tested (indefinite-lived, including goodwill).

§3.1Depreciation methods

MethodPer-period chargePattern
Straight-line(Cost − Salvage) ÷ LifeFlat
Units-of-production(Cost − Salvage) × (Units used ÷ Total units)Tracks actual usage
Double-declining balance(2 ÷ Life) × NBVFront-loaded; ignores salvage in calc but stops at salvage
Sum-of-years' digits(Cost − Salvage) × Year fractionFront-loaded but less than DDB

Accumulated depreciation is a contra-asset; book value (NBV) = Cost − Accumulated depreciation. The asset's cost stays at historical cost on the books; accumulated depreciation absorbs the decline.

§3.2Impairment (U.S. GAAP)

Two-step test for PP&E:

  1. Recoverability: are the asset's undiscounted future cash flows ≥ carrying amount? If yes, no impairment.
  2. Measurement: if no, write down to fair value. Loss = Carrying − FV.

Once impaired, the new lower carrying becomes the depreciable base going forward. Reversals are NOT permitted under U.S. GAAP. IFRS uses a one-step test (carrying vs recoverable amount = max(FV − costs to sell, value-in-use)) and allows reversals.

§3.3Intangibles & goodwill

  • Finite-life intangibles (patents, customer lists, software) — amortize over useful life; test for impairment when triggers exist.
  • Indefinite-life intangibles (perpetual licenses, some trademarks) — not amortized; impairment-tested at least annually.
  • Goodwill — arises only from purchase of another business; never internally generated. Not amortized; impairment-tested annually at the reporting-unit level.

Practice 1 — Straight-line

Cost $50K; salvage $5K; life 5 years. Annual SL depreciation:

  • $10,000
  • $11,000
  • $9,000
  • $8,000
Why: ($50K − $5K) / 5 = $9,000/year. The $10K trap is dividing cost ($50K) by life without subtracting salvage.

Practice 2 — DDB Year 1

Same data. DDB Year 1 depreciation:

  • $20,000
  • $18,000
  • $10,000
  • $25,000
Why: DDB rate = 2/5 = 40%. Year 1 = 40% × $50K (cost, NOT cost minus salvage in DDB calc) = $20,000. Year 2 = 40% × ($50K − $20K NBV) = $12K. Stops when NBV reaches salvage.

Practice 3 — Impairment recoverability

PP&E carrying $300K; expected undiscounted future cash flows $310K; fair value $250K. Under U.S. GAAP, the impairment loss is:

  • $50,000
  • $0 — no impairment
  • $60,000
  • $10,000
Why: Step 1 — undiscounted CFs ($310K) ≥ carrying ($300K) → NOT impaired. Step 2 not triggered. Even though FV is below carrying, U.S. GAAP requires the recoverability test first. IFRS would have impaired ($300K − $250K) since IFRS uses a one-step FV-comparison test.

Practice 4 — Goodwill

Under U.S. GAAP, goodwill is:

  • Amortized over 10 years for all companies
  • Amortized over its useful life
  • Always written off in the year acquired
  • Not amortized; impairment-tested at least annually
Why: Public-company goodwill is impairment-tested only — no amortization since 2001 (SFAS 142). Private companies may elect to amortize over up to 10 years (ASU 2014-02). Goodwill is internally never generated; it arises only from business acquisitions where price exceeds FV of identifiable net assets acquired.
Unit 4 · B.1 Recognition & Valuation

Liability valuation — current, long-term, contingent

Liabilities are present obligations to transfer economic benefit. Current = due within one year (or operating cycle if longer). Long-term = beyond one year, measured at present value of future cash flows. Contingent = obligation depends on a future event.

§4.1Current liabilities

  • Accounts payable — at face / invoice value.
  • Accrued expenses (wages, taxes, utilities) — at amount earned/incurred but unpaid.
  • Unearned revenue — cash received before performance.
  • Notes payable, current portion of long-term debt — at PV.

§4.2Long-term liabilities — bonds payable

Bonds are issued at par, premium, or discount depending on coupon vs market rate at issuance:

  • Coupon = market rate: par.
  • Coupon > market: premium (cash > face).
  • Coupon < market: discount (cash < face).

Premium/discount is amortized over the bond's life (effective-interest method preferred under GAAP/IFRS), bringing the carrying amount to face by maturity. Interest expense each period = carrying × market rate at issuance.

§4.3Contingent liabilities

LikelihoodEstimable?Treatment (U.S. GAAP)
ProbableReasonablyAccrue + disclose
ProbableNot estimableDisclose
Reasonably possibleEitherDisclose only
RemoteEitherIgnore (unless guarantees)
Asymmetric treatment

Loss contingencies are recognized conservatively (probable + estimable). Gain contingencies are NOT recognized until realized — conservatism principle.

Practice 1 — Bond pricing

A bond with a 6% coupon is issued in a market demanding 7%. The bond will be issued at:

  • Par
  • Premium
  • Discount
  • Cannot be determined
Why: Coupon (6%) < market (7%) → bond pays less than market expects → buyers demand a discount on the face. Coupon > market would create a premium.

Practice 2 — Contingent liability — probable + estimable

A pending lawsuit is "probable" of resulting in a loss between $200K and $300K, with $250K being most likely. The company:

  • Accrues a $250K liability and discloses the range
  • Discloses only — no accrual
  • Accrues $300K (worst case)
  • Ignores it
Why: Probable + estimable = accrue. When a range exists, accrue the most likely point estimate (or midpoint if no point is more likely). Disclose the range.

Practice 3 — Gain contingency

A company is suing a competitor for $5M, with management estimating a 90% chance of victory. The company should:

  • Accrue a $4.5M receivable
  • Disclose the contingency; do NOT accrue
  • Accrue $5M
  • Recognize $5M as revenue
Why: Gain contingencies are not accrued until realized — conservatism. Disclose only. Compare to loss contingencies which are accrued at probable + estimable. The asymmetry is intentional.

Practice 4 — Unearned revenue classification

A magazine publisher receives $120 in advance for a 12-month subscription. After 3 months, the unearned-revenue balance is:

  • $120
  • $30
  • $0
  • $90
Why: $120 / 12 = $10/month earned. After 3 months: $30 earned (revenue), $90 still unearned (liability). Adjusting entry monthly: DR Unearned Rev $10 / CR Subscription Rev $10.
Unit 5 · B.1 Recognition & Valuation

Equity transactions — issuance, treasury, dividends, splits

Equity records ownership claims: contributed capital (common stock + APIC), retained earnings, and contra-equity (treasury stock, AOCI losses). Transactions among shareholders flow through equity, never through net income.

§5.1Stock issuance

Issue 1,000 shares of $1 par stock at $25 each. Cash = $25,000.

DR Cash $25,000
CR Common Stock (par × shares = $1 × 1,000) $1,000
CR Additional Paid-in Capital ($25,000 − $1,000) $24,000

§5.2Treasury stock (cost method)

Buyback at $30 of 100 shares: DR Treasury Stock $3,000 / CR Cash $3,000. Treasury is contra-equity — reduces total stockholders' equity.

Reissuance at $35: DR Cash $3,500 / CR Treasury $3,000 / CR APIC-Treasury $500. Reissuance at $25: DR Cash $2,500 / DR APIC-Treasury (or RE if APIC depleted) $500 / CR Treasury $3,000. Gains/losses on treasury never hit P&L — they flow through APIC.

§5.3Dividends

TypeDeclaration entryBS effect
Cash dividendDR RE / CR Dividends Payable↓ RE, ↑ liability
Stock dividend (small <25%)DR RE (FV) / CR CS (par) + APIC (excess)Reclassify within equity at FV
Stock dividend (large ≥25%)DR RE (par) / CR CSReclassify within equity at par
Stock splitMemo entry onlyNo journal effect

§5.4Three relevant dividend dates

  • Declaration — board authorizes; liability is recorded.
  • Record — shareholders on this date receive the dividend; no entry.
  • Payment — DR Dividends Payable / CR Cash.

Practice 1 — Stock issuance entry

Issue 5,000 shares of $2 par stock at $20. Credit to APIC:

  • $0
  • $10,000
  • $100,000
  • $90,000
Why: Cash $100K (5,000 × $20) = CS at par $10K (5,000 × $2) + APIC $90K. APIC absorbs everything above par.

Practice 2 — Treasury reissuance loss

Bought 200 shares treasury at $50; reissuing at $40. APIC-Treasury balance is $1,000 (credit). Entry to record reissuance:

  • DR Cash $8K / DR APIC-Treasury $1K / DR RE $1K / CR Treasury $10K
  • DR Cash $8K / DR Treasury $2K / CR APIC $10K
  • DR Cash $8K / DR Loss on Treasury $2K / CR Treasury $10K
  • No entry — treasury reissues at cost
Why: Sale below cost: total loss = ($50 − $40) × 200 = $2K. Hits APIC-Treasury first ($1K), then RE for the remainder ($1K). Treasury credited at cost ($50 × 200 = $10K). Never P&L — equity transactions don't flow through NI.

Practice 3 — Stock dividend (small)

Declared a 5% stock dividend; current shares 1M; FV $30/share; par $1. RE reduction is:

  • $50,000
  • $1,500,000
  • $1,500,000 (50,000 × $30)
  • $30,000
Why: Small (<25%) stock dividend uses fair value. New shares = 5% × 1M = 50,000. RE reduction = 50,000 × $30 = $1.5M, of which $50K goes to CS (par) and $1.45M to APIC.

Practice 4 — Stock split vs dividend

A 3-for-1 stock split on 100,000 $1-par shares results in:

  • 300,000 shares at $1 par; total CS $300K
  • 300,000 shares at $0.33 par; total CS unchanged
  • 100,000 shares at $3 par
  • Reduces RE by FV × new shares
Why: Split adjusts par proportionally. 3-for-1 → par $1 ÷ 3 = $0.33; shares 100K × 3 = 300K. CS total unchanged ($100K → 300K × $0.33 = $100K). Memo entry only — no journal effect on totals. Stock dividends, in contrast, reclassify within equity.
Unit 6 · B.1 Recognition & Valuation

Revenue recognition — ASC 606 / IFRS 15 five steps

Unified under one global framework: revenue is recognized when control of goods or services transfers to the customer, in the amount the entity expects to be entitled to. The famous five-step model structures every analysis.

§6.1The five steps

  1. Identify the contract — has commercial substance, identifiable rights, payment terms, approval, and probable collection.
  2. Identify performance obligations (POs) — distinct goods or services. A bundle is one PO if items are not separable in practice.
  3. Determine the transaction price — including variable consideration (rebates, performance bonuses, refund rights) and any significant financing component.
  4. Allocate price to POs — based on relative standalone selling prices.
  5. Recognize revenue as each PO is satisfied (point-in-time vs over-time).

§6.2Point-in-time vs over-time

Most product sales transfer control at delivery (point-in-time). Long-term construction or services typically transfer over time when the customer simultaneously receives benefit, the entity's work is customer-controlled, or the asset has no alternative use plus right to payment.

% complete (cost-to-cost) = Costs incurred to date ÷ Total estimated costs
Revenue recognized = % complete × Total contract price − Revenue already recognized

§6.3Variable consideration

Estimate using either expected value (probability-weighted) or most-likely amount. Include only to the extent it's highly probable a significant reversal won't occur — the "constraint."

Loss contracts

If the total estimated cost will exceed the contract price, recognize the entire estimated loss IMMEDIATELY — don't spread it over remaining percent-complete. This is conservatism applied to revenue recognition.

Practice 1 — Step order

Step 4 in the ASC 606 framework is:

  • Identify the contract
  • Identify performance obligations
  • Determine the transaction price
  • Allocate the transaction price to performance obligations
Why: Order: contract (1), POs (2), price (3), allocate (4), recognize (5). Step 4 distributes the total transaction price across the POs identified in step 2 using their relative standalone selling prices.

Practice 2 — Cost-to-cost

$8M contract; total estimated cost $6M; cost incurred to date $1.5M. Revenue to recognize so far:

  • $1,500,000
  • $2,000,000
  • $8,000,000
  • $3,500,000
Why: % complete = $1.5M / $6M = 25%. Revenue = 25% × $8M = $2,000,000. Gross profit recognized = $2M − $1.5M = $500K.

Practice 3 — Loss on long-term contract

$5M contract; revised total estimated cost climbs to $6M (loss expected). Cost to date $2M. Treatment under ASC 606:

  • Spread the $1M loss over remaining percent-complete
  • Reverse all revenue recognized so far
  • Recognize the entire $1M expected loss immediately
  • Defer the loss until contract completion
Why: Conservatism — loss contracts require immediate full loss recognition. Continuing percent-complete revenue but accruing the full loss on top puts the financial statements at the right ending position.

Practice 4 — Variable consideration

Contract base $1,000 + $200 bonus probable of being earned. Constraint analysis suggests minimal reversal risk. Transaction price:

  • $1,200 — include the $200 bonus
  • $1,000 — variable consideration excluded
  • $0 — defer until paid
  • $2,000 — gross up
Why: ASC 606 includes variable consideration to the extent a significant reversal is improbable. Probable + minimal reversal risk → include the $200 → transaction price $1,200.
Unit 7 · B.1 Recognition & Valuation

Income measurement — gains, presentation, EPS basics

The income statement reports the period's revenues, expenses, gains, and losses to arrive at net income. Beyond the basics, the FMAA tests gains-vs-revenues classification, single-step vs multi-step format, and a light dose of EPS.

§7.1Revenues vs gains

Revenues arise from the entity's ordinary, ongoing operations — selling products, providing services. Gains arise from peripheral or incidental transactions — selling old PP&E, foreign-exchange gains, gains on debt extinguishment.

Same distinction for expenses (ongoing) vs losses (peripheral). The classification matters: gains/losses go below operating income; revenues/expenses are inside operating income.

§7.2Single-step vs multi-step

FormatStructure
Single-stepAll revenues + gains − all expenses + losses = NI. One subtraction.
Multi-stepRevenue − COGS = Gross profit; − Op exp = Operating income; ± non-op = Pre-tax; − Tax = NI

§7.3EPS basics

Basic EPS = (NI − Preferred dividends) ÷ Weighted-average common shares outstanding

Weighted-average shares: each share is weighted by months outstanding ÷ 12. Issuing shares mid-year contributes only the partial-year weight.

Diluted EPS additionally accounts for convertible securities and options that would dilute basic if exercised — covered in CMA Part 1 in depth; FMAA tests only basic.

Cumulative preferred

For cumulative preferred stock, subtract the period's dividend amount in the EPS numerator EVEN IF NOT DECLARED — the entitlement accrues. For non-cumulative, subtract only if declared.

Practice 1 — Revenues vs gains

A retailer sells inventory for $40K (cost $30K) AND sells a delivery truck used for years for $10K (book value $7K). On the income statement:

  • Both are revenue
  • Both are gains
  • $40K revenue from inventory; $3K gain on truck (selling price − BV)
  • $50K total revenue
Why: Inventory is the retailer's ordinary business → revenue $40K with COGS $30K → gross profit $10K. Truck sale is peripheral → gain $3K = selling price ($10K) − book value ($7K). Don't gross up gain.

Practice 2 — Multi-step structure

In a multi-step income statement, "operating income" is calculated as:

  • Revenue − COGS
  • Revenue − COGS − Operating expenses
  • Revenue − all expenses and losses
  • Pre-tax income − Tax
Why: Order in multi-step: Revenue − COGS = Gross profit; − Operating expenses = Operating income; ± Non-operating items = Pre-tax; − Tax = NI. Each subtotal is informative on its own.

Practice 3 — Basic EPS

NI $1.2M; preferred dividends declared $200K; weighted-average common shares 500,000. Basic EPS:

  • $2.00
  • $2.40
  • $1.60
  • $3.20
Why: ($1.2M − $200K) / 500K = $1M / 500K = $2.00. The $2.40 trap forgets to subtract preferred dividends.

Practice 4 — Cumulative preferred

Company has cumulative preferred with annual dividend $50K; this year the board did NOT declare a preferred dividend. For basic EPS calculation, the preferred dividend deduction is:

  • $0 — not declared
  • Half — half-rate
  • $50K only if dividends in arrears
  • $50K — accrues regardless of declaration
Why: Cumulative preferred entitlement accrues whether or not declared — common shareholders cannot benefit until preferred is paid (current + arrears). For NON-cumulative, the deduction would be $0 if not declared.
Unit 8 · B.2 Common-Size & Base-Year

Common-size statements (vertical analysis)

Vertical (common-size) analysis expresses each line item as a percentage of a "size anchor" on the same statement. Income statement: % of net sales. Balance sheet: % of total assets. Lets you compare companies of vastly different sizes apples-to-apples.

§8.1Income statement common-size

Each line ÷ net sales × 100. Reveals operating structure: Walmart's COGS is ~75% of revenue (low margin); Apple's is ~55% (high margin). Trends over years tell you whether margins are expanding or compressing.

LineYear 1 $Year 1 %Year 2 $Year 2 %
Revenue$1,000100.0%$1,200100.0%
COGS$60060.0%$78065.0%
Gross profit$40040.0%$42035.0%
Operating exp$20020.0%$24020.0%
Operating income$20020.0%$18015.0%

Reading: revenue grew, but COGS grew faster — gross margin compressed 5 points, dragging operating margin down even though OpEx held steady at 20%.

§8.2Balance sheet common-size

Each asset, liability, and equity line ÷ total assets. Reveals capital structure (debt vs equity), asset mix (inventory-heavy vs receivable-heavy), and liquidity profile (current vs long-term assets).

Use it for benchmarking

Common-size makes industry comparisons direct. A 35% inventory ratio is fine for a retailer, alarming for a software company. Industry context matters as much as the percentages.

Practice 1 — IS common-size base

On a common-size income statement, the base for each line is:

  • Net sales (revenue)
  • Total assets
  • Total equity
  • Operating income
Why: IS base = revenue. BS base = total assets. Each statement uses its own size anchor.

Practice 2 — Reading the table

From the table above, what most likely happened between Year 1 and Year 2?

  • Operating expenses grew uncontrollably
  • Revenue declined
  • Gross margin compressed (COGS grew faster than revenue)
  • The company switched to LIFO
Why: COGS % rose 60→65, gross margin fell 40→35. Revenue grew (+20%) but COGS grew more (+30%). OpEx % held at 20% — they're proportional. Margin compression usually reflects input-cost inflation, pricing pressure, or product-mix shift.

Practice 3 — Cross-company comparison

Which is the BEST use case for common-size statements?

  • Tracking absolute dollar growth
  • Calculating total return for shareholders
  • Forecasting next year's revenue
  • Comparing companies of different sizes on a structural basis
Why: Eliminating scale lets you compare margin and capital-structure profiles across firms. Industry leaders publish "average margin" benchmarks — common-size is the only way that comparison works at all.

Practice 4 — BS common-size insight

A balance sheet shows inventory at 5% of assets in Year 1 and 25% in Year 5 (revenue grew 30%). The MOST likely interpretation:

  • Healthy growth in inventory matching revenue growth
  • Possible inventory build-up (slow turnover, obsolescence risk)
  • Switch from FIFO to LIFO
  • Strong supply chain efficiency
Why: Inventory rising as % of assets — and far faster than revenue grew — is a classic warning sign: building inventory faster than selling. Could reflect demand miss, obsolescence, or seasonal anomaly. Cross-check with inventory turnover and days-of-inventory.
Unit 9 · B.2 Common-Size & Base-Year

Common base year & growth metrics (CAGR)

Horizontal (common-base-year) analysis divides each year's value by its base-year value. Tracks how each line has changed, isolating growth or decline. CAGR converts cumulative growth into an annualized compound rate.

§9.1Base-year analysis

Pick a base year (usually the earliest in your span). Express every subsequent year as % of base. Revenue 100 in Year 0 becomes 100, 110, 121, 130 over four years.

YearRevenue $Index (base = 100)

§9.2CAGR — compound annual growth rate

CAGR = (Ending value ÷ Beginning value)(1/n) − 1
where n = number of years

CAGR converts total growth over multiple years into the equivalent steady annual rate. Total growth of 50% over 3 years isn't 16.7% per year — it's about 14.5% (compounded).

§9.3Year-over-year vs CAGR

  • YoY growth: (This year − Last year) ÷ Last year. Period-specific.
  • Cumulative growth: (Latest year − Base year) ÷ Base year.
  • CAGR: smooths the cumulative into per-year, accounting for compounding.
CAGR hides volatility

A revenue series of [100, 200, 50, 200] has a 4-year CAGR of about 26% — the same as a smooth [100, 126, 159, 200] line. CAGR doesn't capture the wild ride between endpoints. Always look at the actual series, not just the rate.

Practice 1 — CAGR calculation

Revenue grew from $50M (year 0) to $80M (year 4). CAGR is closest to:

  • 15%
  • 10%
  • 12.5%
  • 60%
Why: CAGR = ($80M / $50M)^(1/4) − 1 = 1.6^0.25 − 1 ≈ 1.1247 − 1 = ~12.5%. The 15% trap = 60% / 4. The 60% is total cumulative.

Practice 2 — Index reading

From the table above (Year 0 = 100, Year 3 = 150), revenue grew approximately:

  • 50% cumulative; ~14.5% CAGR over 3 years
  • 50% per year
  • 15% per year
  • 16.7% CAGR
Why: Cumulative = 50%. CAGR = 1.5^(1/3) − 1 ≈ 0.1447 = ~14.5%. The 16.7% trap = 50% / 3.

Practice 3 — YoY vs CAGR

A series shows YoY growth of [50%, −20%, 50%, −20%]. CAGR over the 4 years is closest to:

  • 15% (average)
  • 20%
  • 0%
  • ~9.5%
Why: Multiplicative chain: 1.5 × 0.8 × 1.5 × 0.8 = 1.44. CAGR = 1.44^(1/4) − 1 ≈ 9.5%. Simple-average growth (50−20+50−20)/4 = 15% is wrong — geometric, not arithmetic, mean. CAGR always <= arithmetic average when there's volatility.

Practice 4 — Choosing the metric

A long-term investor wants to compare expected annualized returns across stocks with very different volatility. Best metric:

  • Highest YoY growth in any year
  • Arithmetic average growth
  • CAGR (geometric annualized)
  • Total cumulative return
Why: CAGR captures the equivalent steady rate that connects start and end — what an investor actually experiences. Arithmetic average overstates returns when there's volatility (the −20% / +50% example). Cumulative return doesn't normalize for time.
Unit 10 · B.3 Ratio Analysis

Liquidity ratios — current, quick, cash, CCC

Liquidity = ability to meet short-term obligations as they come due. Three ratios in increasing strictness, plus the cash conversion cycle which measures how long working capital is tied up.

§10.1The three ratios

Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities
Quick (acid-test) = (Cash + Marketable securities + AR) ÷ Current liabilities
Cash ratio = (Cash + Marketable securities) ÷ Current liabilities

Rule-of-thumb: current ratio > 1 healthy, > 2 conservative; quick > 1 strong; cash ratio > 0.5 unusually conservative. Industry context matters — grocery stores can run low ratios because inventory turns fast; software firms run high ratios because of low working capital needs.

§10.2Working capital and CCC

Working capital = CA − CL (a $ amount)
Cash conversion cycle (CCC) = DIO + DSO − DPO

Lower CCC = less cash tied up. Negative CCC means suppliers fund the business — Amazon, Apple, Costco are textbook examples.

§10.3Effect of common transactions on the current ratio

TransactionCurrent ratio > 1Current ratio < 1
Pay AP with cashIncreasesDecreases
Collect AR (cash up, AR down)UnchangedUnchanged
Borrow ST (cash up, CL up)DecreasesIncreases
Buy inventory on credit (inv up, AP up)DecreasesIncreases
Why direction depends on starting ratio

Equal $-changes in numerator and denominator move the ratio toward 1. If starting above 1, equal $-changes pull it down toward 1. If below 1, they push it up toward 1.

Practice 1 — Quick ratio

Cash $40K; Mkt Sec $20K; AR $50K; Inventory $80K; Prepaid $10K; CL $100K. Quick ratio:

  • 2.00
  • 1.10
  • 1.00
  • 0.90
Why: Quick = ($40K + $20K + $50K) / $100K = $110K / $100K = 1.10. Inventory and prepaid excluded.

Practice 2 — Effect on current ratio

A firm with current ratio 1.5 collects $50K of accounts receivable. The current ratio:

  • Increases
  • Decreases
  • Stays the same
  • Cannot be determined
Why: Cash up $50K, AR down $50K — both current assets, so total CA unchanged. CL unchanged. Ratio unchanged. Common test trap.

Practice 3 — CCC interpretation

DIO 60 days; DSO 45 days; DPO 75 days. Cash conversion cycle:

  • 30 days
  • 180 days
  • −30 days
  • 0 days
Why: CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO = 60 + 45 − 75 = 30 days. The company holds $1 of working capital tied up in operations for 30 days on average. Lower is better.

Practice 4 — Negative CCC

Negative CCC indicates:

  • The company is insolvent
  • DSO exceeds DPO
  • Inventory is illiquid
  • Suppliers effectively fund the company's operations
Why: Negative CCC means cash arrives from customers BEFORE the supplier needs to be paid. The float funds operations interest-free. Possible only with strong supplier terms (DPO > DIO + DSO). Amazon, Costco, Apple all run negative CCC.

Practice 5 — Cash ratio purpose

The cash ratio (vs current or quick) is most useful for assessing:

  • Survival under a sudden severe disruption — can the firm meet near-term liabilities with cash on hand?
  • Long-term solvency
  • Operational efficiency
  • Profitability
Why: Cash ratio strips out everything except cash and marketable securities — what you can spend right now. Most relevant in stress-test scenarios (pandemic, recession). Quick still relies on AR collection; cash doesn't.
Unit 11 · B.3 Ratio Analysis

Leverage ratios — D/E, TIE, equity multiplier

Leverage = how much of the company is financed with debt vs equity. More debt amplifies returns in good times AND losses in bad times. Three ratio families: capital structure (mix), coverage (ability to service), and the equity multiplier (DuPont's leverage component).

§11.1Capital structure

Debt-to-equity = Total debt ÷ Total equity
Debt-to-assets = Total debt ÷ Total assets
Equity multiplier = Total assets ÷ Total equity (= 1 + D/E)

D/E above 1 means more debt than equity; depending on industry that's normal (banks, utilities, REITs) or a red flag (tech, services). D/A is bounded between 0 and 1 if equity ≥ 0, easier to interpret across industries.

§11.2Coverage

Times-interest-earned (TIE) = EBIT ÷ Interest expense
Cash-coverage = (EBIT + Depreciation) ÷ Interest expense
Fixed-charge coverage = (EBIT + Lease) ÷ (Interest + Lease)

TIE > 3 generally comfortable; below 2 raises concerns about debt service. Cash-coverage adds back non-cash depreciation — closer to actual cash available.

§11.3Why leverage matters

The classic trade-off:

  • Pros — interest is tax-deductible (cheaper than equity); leverage amplifies ROE; doesn't dilute ownership.
  • Cons — required interest payments regardless of profitability; bankruptcy risk; covenants may restrict operations.

Optimal capital structure balances tax shield gains against expected bankruptcy costs.

Defensive metrics

Cyclical and capital-intensive industries (airlines, automakers) benefit from holding leverage low — they can't escape interest payments during downturns. Predictable-cash businesses (utilities) can sustain higher leverage safely.

Practice 1 — D/E from D/A

Debt-to-assets is 0.6. The corresponding debt-to-equity is:

  • 1.5
  • 0.4
  • 1.0
  • 2.5
Why: D/A = 0.6 → Equity/A = 0.4 → D/E = 0.6/0.4 = 1.5. Equity multiplier = A/E = 1 / 0.4 = 2.5. So D/E = EM − 1 = 1.5.

Practice 2 — TIE

EBIT $400K; interest expense $80K. Times-interest-earned:

  • 3.2×
  • 4.0×
  • 5.0×
  • 0.20×
Why: $400K / $80K = 5.0×. Comfortably above the 2-3× covenant minimum.

Practice 3 — Equity multiplier

A company with a higher equity multiplier:

  • Has more equity per dollar of assets
  • Is less risky
  • Has zero leverage
  • Has more assets per dollar of equity (more leverage)
Why: EM = A / E. Higher means each dollar of equity supports more assets, financed by debt. Connects to DuPont: ROE = ROA × EM. EM is just D/E + 1.

Practice 4 — Tax shield

Why is debt financing often considered "cheaper" than equity at low leverage levels?

  • Interest is tax-deductible, lowering after-tax cost
  • Bond investors require lower returns regardless of tax
  • Debt does not need to be repaid
  • Equity holders demand more reporting
Why: Interest deductibility creates a tax shield = Interest × Tax rate. Pre-tax 8% debt at 25% tax rate has effective after-tax cost of 6%. Equity dividends are NOT deductible. The tax shield is the foundation of the trade-off theory of capital structure.
Unit 12 · B.3 Ratio Analysis

Activity ratios — turnover, DIO, DSO, DPO

Activity (turnover) ratios measure how efficiently a company uses its assets to generate sales. Each ratio answers: how many times per year does this asset cycle? Reciprocally: how many days does it take to turn over once?

§12.1The four core turnovers

Inventory turnover = COGS ÷ Avg inventory; DIO = 365 ÷ turnover
AR turnover = Net credit sales ÷ Avg AR; DSO = 365 ÷ turnover
AP turnover = Purchases (or COGS) ÷ Avg AP; DPO = 365 ÷ turnover
Total asset turnover = Sales ÷ Avg total assets

§12.2Critical: get the numerator right

RatioNumeratorWhy
Inventory turnoverCOGS (NOT sales)Inventory is at cost; matching numerator must also be at cost
AR turnoverNet CREDIT salesCash sales never sit in AR; including them inflates turnover
AP turnoverPurchases (or COGS as proxy)AP arises from purchases, not sales

§12.3What turnovers reveal

  • High inventory turnover → fast-moving, well-managed inventory; or stockouts.
  • Low inventory turnover → obsolete or slow-moving stock; or bulk-buying for safety.
  • High DSO (long collection time) → loose credit policies, customer financial trouble, or lax collection.
  • Low DSO → strict credit terms, prompt collections, or losing customers to laxer competitors.
  • High DPO → using suppliers as financing — pro-cash, but may strain supplier relationships.
365 vs 360

Some textbooks and contracts use 360 days for simplification. The IMA's official materials use 365. Pick whichever you're trained on and stay consistent.

Practice 1 — Inventory turnover

COGS $1.2M; average inventory $200K. Inventory turnover:

  • 0.17×
  • 3.0×
  • 6.0×
  • 8.3×
Why: $1.2M / $200K = 6.0×. DIO = 365 / 6 ≈ 61 days.

Practice 2 — DSO

Net credit sales $1.46M; average AR $200K. DSO:

  • 50 days
  • 30 days
  • 14 days
  • 365 days
Why: Turnover = $1.46M / $200K = 7.3×. DSO = 365 / 7.3 = 50 days. Direct formula: 365 × $200K / $1.46M = 50.

Practice 3 — Wrong numerator

Using sales (instead of COGS) in inventory turnover:

  • Has no effect
  • Makes the ratio more conservative
  • Can give different but valid results
  • Inflates the apparent turnover (because sales include the markup)
Why: Sales = COGS + Gross profit. Using sales puts a much larger numerator over the same inventory denominator → inflated apparent turnover. Inventory is at cost; the numerator should match.

Practice 4 — Total asset turnover

Sales $5M; average total assets $2.5M. Total asset turnover:

  • 0.5×
  • 2.0×
  • $2.5M
  • 50%
Why: $5M / $2.5M = 2.0×. Each dollar of assets generated $2 in sales — strong efficiency. Industries vary widely: retail can hit 3-4×; capital-intensive utilities run 0.3-0.5×.

Practice 5 — DSO trend interpretation

DSO has risen from 35 to 60 days over three quarters while sales stayed flat. The most likely interpretation:

  • Strong sales-discipline improvement
  • Switch from credit to cash sales
  • Slower collections — possibly customer financial weakness or relaxed credit terms
  • Routine seasonal pattern
Why: DSO climbing on flat revenue means money is sitting in AR longer. Possible causes: relaxed credit terms (sales pressure), customer financial stress (collection risk), or process degradation (collections team understaffed). Investigate.
Unit 13 · B.3 Ratio Analysis

Profitability ratios & DuPont decomposition

Margins answer "how much profit per dollar of sales?" Returns answer "how much profit per dollar of investment?" The DuPont decomposition splits ROE into three drivers — surfacing whether returns come from margin, efficiency, or leverage.

§13.1Margins

Gross margin = Gross profit ÷ Net sales
Operating margin = Operating income ÷ Net sales
Net profit margin = Net income ÷ Net sales

Each margin tells a different story. Gross margin = pricing power and direct-cost discipline. Operating margin = total operational leverage. Net margin = the bottom-line take-home rate after non-operating costs and taxes.

§13.2Returns

ROA = Net income ÷ Avg total assets
ROE = Net income ÷ Avg equity

ROA measures asset productivity. ROE measures shareholder return. ROE > ROA when there's leverage — the gap shows how much leverage adds.

§13.3DuPont 3-step

ROE = (NI / Sales) × (Sales / Avg Assets) × (Avg Assets / Avg Equity)
= Net margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier
StrategyNet marginAsset turnoverEMROE
Volume retailer (Walmart-style)3%2.5×3.0×22.5%
Luxury (Tiffany-style)10%0.8×2.5×20.0%
SaaS (high margin)20%0.5×1.5×15.0%
Bank (high leverage)15%0.1×10.0×15.0%

Same approximate ROE achieved through wildly different mixes. DuPont surfaces the strategy.

§13.45-step DuPont (advanced)

ROE = (NI / Pre-tax) × (Pre-tax / EBIT) × (EBIT / Sales) × (Sales / Assets) × (Assets / Equity)
= Tax burden × Interest burden × Operating margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier

Splits net margin into three pieces (tax efficiency, interest burden, operating performance). FMAA tests the 3-step; the 5-step is bonus knowledge.

Practice 1 — Gross margin

Net sales $400K; COGS $260K. Gross margin:

  • 35%
  • 65%
  • 54%
  • $140K
Why: Gross profit = $400K − $260K = $140K. Gross margin = $140K / $400K = 35%. The 65% trap is COGS-as-percent-of-sales, the complement.

Practice 2 — DuPont

Net margin 6%; asset turnover 1.5; equity multiplier 2.5. ROE:

  • 9%
  • 15%
  • 22.5%
  • 10%
Why: 6% × 1.5 × 2.5 = 22.5%. Multiplicative chain. ROA would be 6% × 1.5 = 9%. EM lifts ROA to ROE.

Practice 3 — Strategy classification

A company has gross margin 60% and asset turnover 0.4×. The strategy is most likely:

  • High-volume discount
  • Commodity producer
  • Low-margin wholesale
  • Premium / luxury or specialty (high margin, low volume)
Why: 60% gross margin signals significant pricing power; 0.4× turnover signals slow asset cycling. Together: premium / luxury. Volume strategies show the inverse: low margins, fast turnover.

Practice 4 — ROA vs ROE

A company has ROA 8% and ROE 18%. The most likely explanation is:

  • Sales declined
  • The company carries meaningful financial leverage
  • Management is fraudulent
  • The company has high cash reserves
Why: ROE − ROA = leverage effect. 18 / 8 = 2.25 implied equity multiplier — meaningful debt funding. Higher leverage levers ROA into higher ROE (and bigger losses if NI turns negative).

Practice 5 — When ROE rises but ROA falls

A company's ROE rose from 15% to 18% but ROA fell from 12% to 9%. The most likely cause:

  • Net margin improved sharply
  • Asset turnover increased
  • Equity was repurchased — shrinking the denominator and raising leverage
  • Sales declined while assets stayed flat
Why: Falling ROA + rising ROE → leverage is doing the work. Buying back stock with debt cash (or just increased debt) shrinks equity and raises EM. Watch this pattern: financial engineering creating apparent improvement with deteriorating fundamentals.
