Unit 1 · E.1

Ethical decision making — frameworks & pressures

Ethical decisions arise when one course of action serves your interest at the expense of someone else's, or when competing principles point in different directions. Frameworks don't give you the answer — they make the decision visible.

§1.1Three lenses

  • Consequentialist (utilitarian) — what produces the most good for the most people?
  • Deontological (duty-based) — what does my duty require, regardless of outcome?
  • Virtue ethics — what would a person of good character do here?

Most professional ethics — including the IMA Statement — sit closer to the deontological / virtue end: certain things you simply don't do, even when the math seems to favor them.

§1.2Common pressures

  • Performance targets just out of reach.
  • Personal financial pressure (debts, lifestyle, addiction).
  • Authority pressure ("the CFO told me to").
  • Group conformity ("everyone here does it this way").
  • Rationalization ("we'll fix it next quarter").
Recognize early

The fraud cases that ruin careers rarely begin with a single act of dishonesty. They begin with a small accommodation that is rationalized, then escalates. Recognizing pressure as pressure — not as "just business" — is the first defense.

Practice 1 — Framework

"It would be wrong to falsify the report even if the unit needs the bonus, because integrity is a duty I owe regardless of consequence." This reasoning is most consistent with:

  • Utilitarian / consequentialist
  • Cost-benefit analysis
  • Deontological (duty-based) ethics
  • Egoism
Why: Duty regardless of outcome = deontological (Kant, IMA Statement spirit). Utilitarian would weigh the greater good of the unit's bonus against the harm of inaccurate reporting.

Practice 2 — Pressure type

"The CFO told me to record the entry; I shouldn't second-guess." This rationalization is best characterized as:

  • Authority pressure
  • Personal financial pressure
  • Group conformity
  • Performance pressure
Why: Deferring to authority. The Milgram experiments showed how powerful this is. Professional codes (including the IMA Statement) explicitly require resistance to unlawful or unethical instructions, regardless of who gives them.

Practice 3 — First defense

The first and most important defense against gradual ethical drift is:

  • Detailed external audits
  • Whistleblower hotlines
  • Increased segregation of duties
  • Recognizing pressure as pressure rather than rationalizing it as ordinary business
Why: Awareness is upstream of every other control. Audits, hotlines, and SoD are downstream — they catch fraud after it starts. Ethics training that names the rationalizations explicitly ("we'll fix it next quarter") helps people recognize the slope they're on.
Unit 2 · E.1

Types of business fraud

The ACFE classifies occupational fraud into three families: asset misappropriation, financial statement fraud, and corruption. Misappropriation is the most common; FS fraud is the most expensive per case.

§2.1The three families

FamilyExamplesFrequencyTypical loss
Asset misappropriationSkimming, larceny, payroll, expense reimbursement schemes~85%Lower
CorruptionBribery, kickbacks, conflicts of interest, bid-rigging~50%* (overlaps)Medium
Financial statement fraudRevenue inflation, expense deferral, fictitious assets~10%Highest (often $millions)

§2.2Common asset-misappropriation schemes

  • Skimming — cash diverted before it's recorded.
  • Larceny — cash diverted after recording.
  • Lapping — covering one customer's stolen payment with another's, rolling forward.
  • Ghost employees — payroll to fake employees that the perpetrator collects.
  • Fictitious vendors / shell companies — invoices paid for services never rendered.

Practice 1 — Most expensive type

According to the ACFE's "Report to the Nations," the type of occupational fraud with the HIGHEST median loss per case is:

  • Asset misappropriation
  • Financial statement fraud
  • Corruption
  • Cash skimming
Why: FS fraud is the rarest (~10% of cases) but most expensive (median >$500K, often $millions). Misappropriation is most common but lower-dollar per case. Corruption is in the middle.

Practice 2 — Lapping

"Lapping" describes:

  • Inflating revenue at quarter-end
  • Deferring expenses to a future period
  • Crediting one customer's payment with the next customer's, masking a stolen payment
  • Paying a bribe through an intermediary
Why: Lapping is the classic AR cash-skimming scheme. Detect it via independent bank reconciliation, mandatory vacations (the perpetrator can't keep rolling the scheme forward while away), and rotation of duties.

Practice 3 — FS fraud red flag

A red flag commonly associated with financial-statement fraud is:

  • Aggressive earnings targets combined with management's heavy ownership of stock options
  • Large balance of vacation days carried by the AR clerk
  • Frequent rotation of audit-committee members
  • Detailed expense-reimbursement policies
Why: Stock-based comp + aggressive targets create incentive AND opportunity to hit numbers fraudulently. The other choices are mostly ASSET-misappropriation cues or healthy controls.
Unit 3 · E.2

IMA Statement of Ethical Professional Practice

The IMA Statement is the operating code for management accountants. Memorize the four principles and the four standards — both will appear on the exam, often in the form of "which standard does this scenario violate?"

§3.1The four overarching principles

  • Honesty
  • Fairness
  • Objectivity
  • Responsibility

§3.2The four standards (CCCC)

StandardMeansCommon test cue
CompetenceMaintain professional skill; perform duties per laws/regs/standards"Did the work without the necessary expertise"
ConfidentialityDon't disclose info; don't use it for personal gain; ensure subordinates respect it"Tipped a friend on a pending merger"
IntegrityAvoid actual or apparent conflicts; refuse gifts/favors that would influence; communicate unfavorable as well as favorable"Accepted a vendor's gift while sourcing"
CredibilityCommunicate fairly and objectively; disclose all relevant info; report deficiencies"Selectively reported only favorable variances"
Mnemonic

4C: Competence, Confidentiality, Integrity, Credibility (some sources put them in order C-C-I-C; the IMA materials typically list the four under those names). Integrity and Credibility are the most-tested.

Practice 1 — Standard match

An accountant accepts an expensive watch from a vendor whose contract she influences. Most direct violation:

  • Competence
  • Confidentiality
  • Integrity
  • Credibility
Why: Integrity prohibits accepting gifts that would or appear to influence judgment. Even if she "wouldn't really be influenced," the apparent conflict alone violates Integrity.

Practice 2 — Selective reporting

A controller selectively highlights favorable variances in the monthly review and downplays unfavorable ones to keep the bonus pool intact. Standard violated:

  • Competence
  • Confidentiality
  • Integrity
  • Credibility
Why: Credibility specifically requires fair, objective communication and disclosure of all relevant information — favorable AND unfavorable. Selective reporting is a textbook Credibility violation.

Practice 3 — Confidentiality

A staff accountant casually mentions to a college friend that the company is about to acquire a public competitor. Standard violated:

  • Competence
  • Confidentiality
  • Integrity
  • Credibility
Why: Confidentiality forbids disclosing information acquired in the course of work. (And — separately — federal securities law prohibits trading on material non-public information; the friend now has potentially actionable insider knowledge.)
Unit 4 · E.2

The fraud triangle & resolving ethical dilemmas

Donald Cressey's fraud triangle identifies three conditions that together produce fraud: pressure, opportunity, rationalization. Remove any one, and most fraud doesn't happen.

§4.1The triangle

SideExamples
Pressure (incentive)Personal debts, lifestyle, performance targets, fear of job loss
OpportunityWeak controls, override capability, related-party transactions, complex structures
Rationalization"Borrowing, will repay", "everyone does it", "deserved it", "for the good of the company"

The fraud diamond adds a fourth: capability (the perpetrator must actually have the skill and access to execute and conceal the fraud).

§4.2Resolving an ethical dilemma — IMA framework

When an accountant identifies an unethical act or unresolved conflict, the IMA prescribes a sequence:

  1. Discuss with immediate supervisor. If the supervisor IS the issue, skip this step.
  2. Escalate through internal levels — supervisor's manager, audit committee, board — until resolved.
  3. Consult an objective advisor (IMA Ethics Helpline, internal ethics hotline, attorney) about courses of action.
  4. If unresolved, the only remaining option may be resignation — together with a memorandum laying out the issue.
Not the first step

"Notify external authorities" is generally NOT a required first step under the IMA Statement. The framework prioritizes internal escalation. External whistleblowing may be appropriate when internal channels fail or where law requires (e.g., SOX whistleblower protections, securities fraud).

Practice 1 — Triangle

Which is NOT one of the three sides of Cressey's fraud triangle?

  • Pressure
  • Opportunity
  • Concealment
  • Rationalization
Why: The three sides: pressure, opportunity, rationalization. Concealment is part of every fraud but isn't a side. The "fraud diamond" adds capability — also not concealment.

Practice 2 — First step

An accountant discovers her CFO has instructed her to record a clearly improper entry. The IMA Statement most directly suggests her first step is to:

  • Escalate to the next level above the CFO (audit committee or board)
  • Notify the SEC
  • Resign immediately
  • Comply, then report later
Why: First step is normally the immediate supervisor, but if the supervisor IS the issue, escalate ABOVE them. The audit committee — independent of management — is the right next stop. External reporting is later, only if internal channels fail. Comply-then-report is never acceptable.

Practice 3 — Removing one side

A company strengthens internal controls by tightening segregation of duties and adding independent reconciliation. Which side of the fraud triangle is most directly attacked?

  • Pressure
  • Rationalization
  • Capability
  • Opportunity
Why: Strong controls take away the OPPORTUNITY to commit and conceal fraud. Pressure (incentives, personal needs) is harder for the company to address. Rationalization is countered by tone-at-the-top and ethics training, not controls.

"Skimming" specifically refers to:

  • Inflating sales figures
  • Stealing fixed assets
  • Falsifying expense reports
  • Diverting cash BEFORE it is recorded in the books
Why: Skim = take cash before it ever hits the books — no audit trail. Larceny = taking cash that's already recorded.

  • Competence

  • Credibility

  • Confidentiality

A controller omits an unfavorable variance from the management report to avoid an uncomfortable conversation. Standard violated:

  • Competence

  • Integrity

  • Resign

Why: The IMA framework starts with internal discussion and escalates only if unresolved. External authorities are a last resort, not a first step.
