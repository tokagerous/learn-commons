Unit 1 · E.1 Business Ethics

Ethical decision-making frameworks

Ethical decisions arise when one course of action serves your interest at the expense of someone else's, or when competing principles point in different directions. Frameworks don't give you the answer — they make the decision visible, force you to articulate your reasoning, and reveal which considerations you're weighting.

§1.1Three classical lenses

FrameworkQuestionStrengthWeakness
Consequentialist (Utilitarian)What produces the most good for the most people?Considers impact"Ends justify means" — sometimes endorses sacrificing minorities
Deontological (Duty-based)What does my duty require, regardless of outcome?Universalizable; protects rightsCan ignore catastrophic consequences
Virtue ethicsWhat would a person of good character do?Holistic; considers integrityVague when characters disagree
Justice / fairnessWhat is fair to all parties?Strong on distribution and equality"Fair" itself is contested

§1.2Where professional ethics sits

Most professional ethics — including the IMA Statement of Ethical Professional Practice — sits closer to the deontological / virtue end. Certain things you simply don't do, even when the math seems to favor them. "Most good for most people" doesn't justify falsifying financial statements, even if the BU's bonus pool would be larger.

§1.3A practical decision sequence

  1. Recognize the issue — name what's at stake. Is this an ethical decision, or a personal-preference one?
  2. Identify stakeholders — who benefits, who is harmed, what are their legitimate interests?
  3. Consider alternatives — usually more options exist than the binary first frame suggests.
  4. Apply frameworks — what does each lens say? When they agree, decision is easy.
  5. Consider the public test — would I be comfortable if this decision were on the front page?
  6. Decide and act — including what to communicate, to whom, and how.
The newspaper test

Ask: "If this decision and my reasoning were published in tomorrow's Wall Street Journal, would I be comfortable defending it?" If not, that's signal — not proof of wrongdoing, but a flag worth examining. Many ethical dilemmas dissolve under the newspaper test.

Practice 1 — Framework match

"It would be wrong to falsify the report even if the unit needs the bonus, because integrity is a duty I owe regardless of consequence." This reasoning is most consistent with:

  • Utilitarian / consequentialist
  • Cost-benefit analysis
  • Deontological (duty-based) ethics
  • Egoism
Why: Duty regardless of outcome = deontological (Kant, IMA Statement spirit). Utilitarian would weigh the unit's bonus benefit against the harm of inaccurate reporting.

Practice 2 — Newspaper test

The "newspaper test" asks:

  • Would I be comfortable if this decision and my reasoning appeared on tomorrow's front page?
  • Would the SEC approve in writing?
  • Has the decision been made before by competitors?
  • Is the decision technically legal?
Why: The newspaper test is a quick gut-check that surfaces when something feels uncomfortable. Discomfort isn't proof of wrongdoing, but it's signal worth examining. Legal but unethical decisions often fail this test.

Practice 3 — Most professional ethics codes

Most professional ethics codes (including the IMA Statement) sit closest to which framework?

  • Egoism (self-interest)
  • Pure consequentialism
  • Cost-benefit analysis
  • Deontological / virtue ethics
Why: Codes tend to specify duties and prohibited behaviors — deontological in form. Some considerations are absolute regardless of consequence ("you shall not falsify…"). Virtue language ("integrity, honesty, fairness") complements the duties.

Practice 4 — Stakeholder analysis

In ethical decision-making, identifying stakeholders helps because:

  • It satisfies legal disclosure requirements
  • It speeds up the decision
  • It surfaces all parties affected, including those who may be harmed silently
  • It reduces the need for analysis
Why: First-frame decisions often consider only the obvious parties. Identifying the full set of affected stakeholders (employees, suppliers, customers, regulators, community) surfaces hidden harms and broadens the ethical calculus.
Unit 2 · E.1 Business Ethics

Common pressures and rationalizations

Most people who commit fraud don't think of themselves as criminals. Pressures push them to act, then rationalizations let them keep their self-image intact. Understanding both lets you spot risk before it materializes — in others and in yourself.

§2.1The five most common pressures

  • Performance pressure — targets just out of reach; bonus tied to a single metric.
  • Personal financial pressure — debts, lifestyle, addiction, family medical costs.
  • Authority pressure — "the CFO told me to"; structural power asymmetry.
  • Group conformity — "everyone here does this"; tribe over principle.
  • Time pressure — close on Friday, audit on Monday; shortcuts taken under stress.

§2.2The classic rationalizations

RationalizationWhat it really is
"I'm just borrowing — I'll pay it back"Theft (becomes obvious when the discovery happens before repayment)
"I deserve this; the company underpaid me"Compensating yourself outside the legitimate process
"Everyone does it"Conformity used to suspend personal judgment
"It's only this once"Almost never true; first time leads to second time
"For the good of the company"Conflating personal benefit with company benefit
"We'll fix it next quarter"Aggressive accounting that compounds; cannot self-correct

§2.3The slippery slope

Almost no fraud begins with a major theft. It begins with a small accommodation — fudging an expense report, "borrowing" cash for a weekend, postponing an inconvenient adjustment. Each accommodation is rationalized; each makes the next easier; the slope drops gradually until the fall is catastrophic.

This is why ethical training emphasizes EARLY pattern recognition: it's much easier to walk back from the first $100 than the first $100,000.

Self-defense

Recognizing pressure as pressure — not as "just business" — is the first defense. When you find yourself rationalizing, that's a signal: the rationalization is doing the work that integrity should be doing.

Practice 1 — Authority pressure

"The CFO told me to record the entry; I shouldn't second-guess." This is best characterized as:

  • Authority pressure
  • Personal financial pressure
  • Group conformity
  • Time pressure
Why: Deferring to authority — Milgram-style. Professional codes (including the IMA Statement) explicitly require resistance to unlawful or unethical instructions, regardless of who gives them.

Practice 2 — First defense

The first and most important defense against gradual ethical drift is:

  • Detailed external audits
  • Whistleblower hotlines
  • Increased segregation of duties
  • Recognizing pressure as pressure rather than rationalizing it as ordinary business
Why: Awareness is upstream of every other control. Audits, hotlines, and SoD catch fraud after it starts. Ethics training that names the rationalizations explicitly ("we'll fix it next quarter") helps people recognize the slope they're on.

Practice 3 — Slippery slope mechanic

The "slippery slope" of fraud most directly describes:

  • The legal requirement for whistleblower protection
  • How small initial frauds always escalate to major ones automatically
  • How small accommodations, each rationalized, make subsequent ones easier and more likely
  • A specific anti-fraud control framework
Why: Each rationalized step doesn't trigger the alarm — but it makes the next step's alarm threshold a little higher. Over time, behavior that would have been unthinkable becomes routine. Understanding this mechanic helps catch yourself early.

Practice 4 — Common rationalizations

"I'm just borrowing — I'll pay it back" most often functions as:

  • A legitimate loan arrangement
  • A rationalization that conceals theft until repayment doesn't happen in time
  • A protected employment arrangement
  • An ethical hedging strategy
Why: The "loan" is unauthorized. If discovered before repayment, it's theft. The rationalization works only as long as no one looks. Almost every embezzlement case starts with this self-narrative.
Unit 3 · E.1 Business Ethics

Types of business fraud (ACFE taxonomy)

The Association of Certified Fraud Examiners (ACFE) classifies occupational fraud — fraud committed by employees against employers — into three families. The proportions and dollar magnitudes are remarkably stable across global studies.

§3.1The fraud tree

FamilyExamplesFrequencyMedian loss
Asset misappropriationSkimming, larceny, payroll, expense reimbursement, billing schemes, inventory theft~85% of casesLower (median ~$100K)
CorruptionBribery, kickbacks, conflicts of interest, bid-rigging, illegal gratuities~50% (often overlaps misappropriation)Medium (~$200K)
Financial statement fraudRevenue inflation, expense deferral, fictitious assets, hidden liabilities~10% of casesHighest ($500K+, often $millions)

§3.2Asset-misappropriation schemes

  • Skimming — cash diverted BEFORE recording. No paper trail; hardest to detect via books.
  • Cash larceny — cash diverted AFTER recording. Creates a discrepancy.
  • Lapping — covering one customer's stolen payment with another's payment, rolling forward indefinitely.
  • Ghost employees — payroll to fake employees that the perpetrator collects.
  • Fictitious vendors / shell companies — invoices paid for services never rendered.
  • Expense reimbursement fraud — fictitious or inflated expense claims.
  • Inventory theft — physical removal of goods.

§3.3FS fraud schemes

  • Revenue inflation — channel-stuffing, bill-and-hold, fictitious sales, premature recognition.
  • Expense deferral or capitalization — capitalizing what should be expensed (WorldCom).
  • Hidden or understated liabilities — off-balance-sheet entities (Enron's SPEs).
  • Improper asset valuation — overstating receivables, inventory, goodwill not impaired when it should be.
  • Improper disclosures — omitting related-party transactions, contingencies.
Why FS fraud is the most expensive

FS fraud is rare per case but enormous in dollar terms because it's nearly always perpetrated by senior management with broad access to override controls. The 2002 WorldCom case ($11B) and Enron ($74B in lost market cap) are the textbook examples that prompted SOX.

Practice 1 — Most expensive type

According to the ACFE, the type of occupational fraud with the HIGHEST median loss per case is:

  • Asset misappropriation
  • Financial statement fraud
  • Corruption
  • Cash skimming
Why: FS fraud is the rarest (~10%) but most expensive — often $millions per case, perpetrated by senior management. Misappropriation is most common but lower per case. Corruption is in the middle.

Practice 2 — Lapping

"Lapping" describes:

  • Inflating revenue at quarter-end
  • Capitalizing what should be expensed
  • Crediting one customer's payment with the next customer's payment, masking a stolen payment
  • Paying a bribe through an intermediary
Why: Lapping is the classic AR cash-skimming scheme. Detect it via independent bank reconciliation, mandatory vacations (the perpetrator can't keep the scheme rolling forward while away), and rotation of duties.

Practice 3 — Skimming vs larceny

The key distinction between skimming and cash larceny is:

  • Skimming uses checks; larceny uses cash
  • Larceny is less common
  • Skimming requires collusion
  • Skimming diverts cash BEFORE recording; larceny diverts cash AFTER recording
Why: Skimming = takes cash before it ever hits the books — no audit trail. Larceny = takes cash already recorded — creates a detectable discrepancy. Skimming is harder to detect but limited to point-of-receipt; larceny is broader but easier to catch.

Practice 4 — FS fraud red flag

A red flag commonly associated with financial-statement fraud is:

  • Aggressive earnings targets combined with management's heavy ownership of stock options
  • Large balance of vacation days carried by the AR clerk
  • Frequent rotation of audit-committee members
  • Detailed expense-reimbursement policies
Why: Stock-based comp + aggressive targets create incentive AND opportunity to hit numbers fraudulently. Large unused vacation days suggests the AR clerk doesn't want anyone covering their work — a misappropriation cue.

Practice 5 — Channel stuffing

Shipping product to distributors near quarter-end with side agreements allowing return is most likely:

  • Asset misappropriation
  • Bribery
  • Channel stuffing — premature revenue recognition (FS fraud)
  • Embezzlement
Why: Channel stuffing inflates current-period revenue while the side agreement effectively makes the "sale" a consignment. Revenue should not be recognized when control hasn't transferred. ASC 606 explicitly addresses this — return rights and side agreements affect transaction price determination.
Unit 4 · E.1 Business Ethics

Detection schemes and red flags

If fraud cannot always be prevented, the next best thing is fast detection — and detection requires both controls and the awareness to recognize anomalies as anomalies.

§4.1How fraud is detected (ACFE data)

Detection methodApproximate share
Tip (especially from employees)~40-45%
Internal audit~15%
Management review~12%
By accident~5-7%
External audit~3-4%
Document examination, account reconciliation, monitoring toolsbalance

Tips are by far the dominant source. This is why whistleblower hotlines (anonymous, well-publicized, with anti-retaliation protections) are among the most effective anti-fraud controls — they more than triple the tip rate.

§4.2Personal red flags

The ACFE notes that ~85% of perpetrators displayed at least one of these behavioral red flags before discovery:

  • Living beyond their means (sudden lifestyle upgrade unsupported by salary)
  • Financial difficulties (debts, divorce, addiction)
  • Unusually close relationships with vendors or customers
  • Control issues — refusal to share duties, unwillingness to take vacation
  • Recent personal stress (illness, family problems)
  • Defensive about questions or audits

§4.3Process red flags

  • Accounting irregularities — frequent year-end adjustments, unusual journal entries.
  • Unexplained variances repeatedly favorable for one manager.
  • Missing or altered documents.
  • Unusual related-party transactions.
  • Aggressive accounting positions justified with elaborate technical arguments.
  • Sudden growth or margin expansion that competitors aren't matching.

§4.4Effective anti-fraud controls

The ACFE measures which controls reduce median loss and detection time. Top performers:

  1. Mandatory employee vacations / job rotation (huge effect — many ongoing schemes need constant maintenance)
  2. Surprise audits
  3. Hotline / reporting mechanism with anti-retaliation
  4. Dedicated fraud-risk assessment
  5. Formal anti-fraud policy + training
  6. Strong tone at the top
Vacations as fraud detection

"Mandatory two consecutive weeks" is more than HR policy — it's a fraud control. Schemes like lapping require daily attention to roll forward; the perpetrator can't sustain them on vacation. Force vacation, rotate someone in, and the scheme breaks.

Practice 1 — Top detection method

According to ACFE data, the single largest source of fraud detection is:

  • Tips (especially from employees)
  • External audit
  • By accident
  • Forensic accounting investigations
Why: ~40-45% of fraud cases are detected via tips. Whistleblower hotlines dramatically improve this because anonymity removes retaliation fear. External audits detect <5%.

Practice 2 — Mandatory vacation purpose

A "mandatory two-week consecutive vacation" policy primarily serves as:

  • HR morale benefit
  • Cost reduction
  • Fraud detection — schemes that need daily attention often surface during the vacation
  • Tax efficiency
Why: Schemes like lapping, ghost employees, and fictitious vendors typically need ongoing maintenance. A two-week absence with someone else covering the work often reveals the scheme.

Practice 3 — Behavioral red flags

All of the following are typical behavioral red flags EXCEPT:

  • Living beyond means
  • Refusing to take vacation
  • Defensive about audit questions
  • Taking lunch at the same time every day
Why: Lunchtime regularity is unrelated. The other three are well-documented in ACFE data — sustained patterns associated with active fraud schemes.

Practice 4 — Hotline effectiveness

A whistleblower hotline most directly increases fraud detection by:

  • Eliminating the need for internal audit
  • Providing an anonymous, anti-retaliation channel that more than triples the tip rate
  • Replacing external audit
  • Reducing the cost of compliance training
Why: Tips are the #1 detection source. Anonymity + anti-retaliation removes barriers that suppress reporting. Companies with hotlines see substantially higher tip rates and shorter detection windows.
Unit 5 · E.2 Ethics for Accountants

IMA Statement of Ethical Professional Practice

The IMA Statement is the operating code for management accountants. Memorize the four overarching principles AND the four standards — both will appear on the exam, often in the form of "which standard does this scenario violate?"

§5.1The four overarching principles

  • Honesty
  • Fairness
  • Objectivity
  • Responsibility

The principles set the broad ethical aspiration. The standards specify operational requirements.

§5.2The four standards (CCIC)

StandardMeansCommon test cue
CompetenceMaintain professional skill; perform duties per laws/regs/standards; recognize and communicate limitations"Did the work without the necessary expertise"
ConfidentialityDon't disclose info; don't use it for personal/others' gain; ensure subordinates respect it"Tipped a friend on a pending merger"
IntegrityAvoid actual or apparent conflicts; refuse gifts/favors that would influence; communicate unfavorable information as well as favorable"Accepted a vendor's gift while sourcing"
CredibilityCommunicate fairly and objectively; disclose all relevant info; report internal-control deficiencies; comply with relevant laws"Selectively reported only favorable variances"

§5.3Common scenarios and which standard they violate

ScenarioStandard violated
Accountant accepts a luxury watch from a vendorIntegrity
Tipped a friend about pending acquisitionConfidentiality
Selectively reports favorable variances; omits unfavorableCredibility
Took on tax engagement without sufficient knowledgeCompetence
Did not disclose a known internal-control weaknessCredibility (or Integrity)
Failed to maintain CPE as requiredCompetence
Apparent conflict of interest with a relative's businessIntegrity
"Apparent" matters

Integrity prohibits not just actual conflicts but APPARENT conflicts. Even if a gift wouldn't actually sway the accountant, accepting it creates the appearance of impropriety — and that itself is the violation.

Practice 1 — Standard match (gift)

An accountant accepts an expensive watch from a vendor whose contract she influences. Most direct violation:

  • Competence
  • Confidentiality
  • Integrity
  • Credibility
Why: Integrity prohibits accepting gifts that would or APPEAR TO influence judgment. Even if she wouldn't really be influenced, the apparent conflict alone violates Integrity.

Practice 2 — Selective reporting

A controller selectively highlights favorable variances and downplays unfavorable ones to keep the bonus pool intact. Standard violated:

  • Competence
  • Confidentiality
  • Integrity
  • Credibility
Why: Credibility requires fair, objective communication and disclosure of all relevant information — favorable AND unfavorable. Selective reporting is a textbook Credibility violation.

Practice 3 — Tipping

A staff accountant casually mentions to a college friend that the company is about to acquire a public competitor. Standard violated:

  • Competence
  • Confidentiality
  • Integrity
  • Credibility
Why: Confidentiality forbids disclosing information acquired in the course of work. Separately, federal securities law prohibits trading on material non-public information; the friend now has potentially actionable insider knowledge.

Practice 4 — Competence

An accountant takes on a complex international tax engagement despite knowing the rules are outside their training. The most direct standard violation is:

  • Competence — must maintain professional skill and recognize/communicate limitations
  • Confidentiality
  • Credibility
  • Integrity
Why: Competence requires recognizing the limits of your own expertise and either declining the work, partnering with someone qualified, or seeking sufficient training before proceeding.

Practice 5 — Apparent conflict

An accountant's brother owns a company that is one of three vendors bidding on a contract. The accountant is on the bid-evaluation committee. The most ethical action:

  • Recuse only if the brother's bid wins
  • Continue serving — disclose at year-end
  • Recuse from the bid-evaluation committee and disclose the relationship to management
  • Recommend the brother's bid since his prices are better
Why: Apparent conflict alone is a violation. Recuse and disclose, regardless of confidence in personal objectivity. Other committee members can evaluate without the conflict.

Practice 6 — Failure to disclose ICFR weakness

An accountant discovers a material weakness in internal control over financial reporting but doesn't escalate or document. Standard violated:

  • Competence
  • Confidentiality
  • Integrity
  • Credibility
Why: Credibility specifically requires reporting "internal-control deficiencies" — the IMA standards explicitly enumerate this duty. Failing to disclose the weakness violates Credibility (and may also implicate Integrity if motivation is to avoid management discomfort).
Unit 6 · E.2 Ethics for Accountants

Fraud triangle / diamond

Donald Cressey's fraud triangle identifies three conditions that together produce fraud: pressure, opportunity, rationalization. Remove any one, and most fraud doesn't happen.

§6.1The three sides

SideExamplesWho can address it
Pressure (incentive / motivation)Personal debts, lifestyle, performance targets, fear of job loss, family medical expensesMostly personal; company can avoid creating performance pressure that invites fraud
OpportunityWeak controls, override capability, related-party transactions, complex structures, lack of audit visibilityThe company — internal controls, segregation, oversight
Rationalization"Borrowing, will repay", "everyone does it", "deserved it", "for the good of the company"Tone at the top, ethics training, hiring

Internal control mostly attacks opportunity. Tone at the top and culture mostly attack rationalization. Pressure is hardest to address structurally — most you can do is avoid creating performance pressure that invites it (single-metric bonuses, impossible targets).

§6.2The fraud diamond — adding capability

Wolfe and Hermanson (2004) extended the triangle into the fraud diamond by adding capability: the perpetrator must actually have the skill, position, and access to execute and conceal the fraud.

Capability matters because pressure + opportunity + rationalization don't guarantee fraud — the person also needs:

  • Position (authority to override controls or initiate fraudulent transactions)
  • Brain (technical understanding to construct a workable scheme)
  • Confidence (ability to deal with stress over time)
  • Ability to lie effectively

Some fraudsters fit a profile: smart, technical, calm under pressure, with broad system access. Recognizing this combination as a risk factor — without paranoia — informs control design (require backups, mandatory vacations, periodic role rotation).

§6.3Removing one side

The triangle is multiplicative: missing any side and fraud is unlikely. This is why anti-fraud strategy is layered:

  • Tone at the top + ethics culture → reduces rationalization opportunities.
  • Strong controls (SoD, monitoring, audits) → reduces opportunity.
  • Reasonable performance targets, employee assistance programs, balanced compensation → reduces pressure.
Pressure can come from the top

When senior management creates extreme performance pressure (Enron, Wells Fargo cross-selling), the resulting fraud often involves multiple employees who didn't initiate the pressure. The institutional cause is upstream of the individual perpetrator.

Practice 1 — Triangle sides

Which is NOT one of the three sides of Cressey's fraud triangle?

  • Pressure
  • Opportunity
  • Concealment
  • Rationalization
Why: The three sides: pressure, opportunity, rationalization. Concealment is part of every fraud but isn't a triangle side. The fraud diamond adds capability — also not concealment.

Practice 2 — Removing one side

A company strengthens internal controls by tightening segregation of duties and adding independent reconciliation. Which side of the fraud triangle is most directly attacked?

  • Pressure
  • Rationalization
  • Capability
  • Opportunity
Why: Strong controls take away the OPPORTUNITY to commit and conceal fraud. Pressure (incentives, personal needs) is harder for the company to address. Rationalization is countered by tone-at-the-top and ethics training, not controls.

Practice 3 — Tone at the top

A strong "tone at the top" most directly attacks which side of the fraud triangle?

  • Rationalization
  • Opportunity
  • Capability
  • Pressure
Why: Tone at the top sets cultural expectations that delegitimize the rationalizations ("everyone does it," "for the good of the company"). When the boss visibly lives the values, the rationalization mechanism breaks down.

Practice 4 — Diamond addition

The fraud diamond extends the triangle by adding:

  • Concealment
  • Capability — skill, position, and access required to execute the scheme
  • Compensation pressure
  • External economic factors
Why: Wolfe and Hermanson noted that pressure + opportunity + rationalization aren't sufficient — the perpetrator also needs the practical capability to execute and sustain the fraud. Capability includes position, technical skill, confidence, and the ability to lie effectively.
Unit 7 · E.2 Ethics for Accountants

Resolving ethical dilemmas — the IMA framework

When an accountant identifies an unethical act, an unresolved conflict, or pressure to violate the IMA Statement, the framework prescribes a sequence — internal first, external as a last resort.

§7.1The standard sequence

  1. Discuss with the immediate supervisor. Many issues dissolve here — the supervisor wasn't aware, was misinformed, or hadn't thought it through.
  2. If the supervisor IS the issue, skip them. Go to the next level (their manager, audit committee, board chair).
  3. Escalate through internal levels — supervisor's manager, audit committee, board — until resolved.
  4. Consult an objective advisor — IMA Ethics Helpline, internal ethics hotline, attorney — about your courses of action and your legal exposure.
  5. If the issue is unresolved, the only remaining option may be resignation — together with an informative memorandum to the appropriate level laying out the issue and your concerns.

§7.2What about external whistleblowing?

External reporting (to regulators, law enforcement) is generally NOT a required first step under the IMA Statement. The framework prioritizes internal escalation. External whistleblowing may be appropriate when:

  • Internal channels have failed or have been compromised.
  • Law requires reporting (e.g., specific violations triggering mandatory disclosure).
  • SOX whistleblower protections apply (publicly traded companies).
  • The fraud is so substantial that internal channels are likely to suppress it.

§7.3Whistleblower protections

Statute / mechanismProtection / incentive
Sarbanes-Oxley §806Anti-retaliation for FS-fraud whistleblowers in public companies
SEC Office of the Whistleblower10-30% of penalties > $1M; anti-retaliation
Dodd-FrankStrengthened SEC whistleblower program
False Claims Act (qui tam)Whistleblower share of government recovery (federal contracts)

§7.4Common mistakes

  • Going public too early — burns bridges, may forfeit whistleblower protections that require giving the company a chance.
  • Documenting in personal email or social media — destroys confidentiality and may waive privilege.
  • Confronting the perpetrator personally — destroys evidence, alerts them.
  • Resigning silently — leaves the issue unresolved and may forfeit subsequent legal recourse.
Document everything

From the first uneasy moment, keep a contemporaneous (date-stamped) personal log of conversations, instructions, and your own communications. Save copies on neutral media. Don't rely on company systems alone — they can be revoked.

Practice 1 — First step (normal)

An accountant uncertain about an entry's propriety should typically FIRST:

  • Discuss with the immediate supervisor
  • File a report with the SEC
  • Resign
  • Post on social media
Why: The IMA framework starts with internal discussion. External authorities are a last resort, not a first step. Many issues dissolve at the supervisor level when the supervisor wasn't aware.

Practice 2 — When supervisor IS the issue

An accountant discovers her CFO has instructed her to record a clearly improper entry. The IMA Statement most directly suggests her first step is to:

  • Escalate to the next level above the CFO (audit committee or board)
  • Notify the SEC
  • Resign immediately
  • Comply, then report later
Why: First step is normally the immediate supervisor, but if the supervisor IS the issue, escalate ABOVE them. The audit committee — independent of management — is the right next stop. External reporting is later, only if internal channels fail.

Practice 3 — IMA Ethics Helpline

The IMA Ethics Helpline is most useful for:

  • Reporting fraud to law enforcement
  • Filing SEC complaints
  • Discussing ethical dilemmas with an objective advisor before deciding next steps
  • Negotiating settlement amounts
Why: The helpline provides confidential consultation — sounding board for an accountant facing a dilemma. Doesn't replace legal counsel and isn't a reporting channel to authorities. Helps you think through your options.

Practice 4 — Resignation as last resort

If internal escalation fails to resolve a major ethical dilemma, the IMA Statement's last-resort recommendation is:

  • Continue working and stay silent
  • Always file with the SEC immediately
  • Confront the perpetrator personally
  • Resign with an informative memorandum to the appropriate level
Why: Resignation is the final available step under the IMA Statement when internal channels are exhausted. The memorandum documents the issue, preserves the record, and gives the appropriate body a chance to act. External whistleblowing may also be appropriate, but isn't always required.

Practice 5 — SOX whistleblower

SOX §806 specifically protects:

  • All whistleblowers in any organization
  • Whistleblowers in public companies who report financial-statement fraud, against retaliation
  • Tax fraud whistleblowers only
  • Government contractor whistleblowers
Why: SOX §806 = anti-retaliation for FS-fraud reporting in PUBLIC companies. The False Claims Act / qui tam covers government-contractor fraud separately. Dodd-Frank further strengthens SEC whistleblower programs with bounty incentives.

An accountant uses non-public sales-pipeline data to recommend stock to a friend. Standard violated:

Question 8Integrity

  • Credibility

A controller omits an unfavorable variance from the report to avoid an uncomfortable conversation. Standard violated:

  • Competence
  • Confidentiality
  • Integrity
  • Credibility

The three sides of the fraud triangle are:

  • Pressure, Concealment, Capability
  • Pressure, Capability, Rationalization
  • Pressure, Opportunity, Rationalization
  • Opportunity, Capability, Concealment

  • File a report with the SEC
  • Resign
  • Post publicly
