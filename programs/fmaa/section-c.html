& Budgeting.

Unit 1 · C.1 Budgeting Concepts

Why budget — performance goals & process characteristics

Budgets serve four purposes: plan (set direction), coordinate (align functions), communicate (translate strategy into operating numbers), and control (provide a benchmark for variance analysis).

§1.1Goals: stretch but achievable

Targets too easy = no motivation. Too hard = demoralization or gaming. Effective targets are challenging but attainable — typically requiring above-average effort but reachable by competent execution.

§1.2Top-down vs bottom-up

  • Top-down (imposed): management sets targets, departments execute. Fast, aligned with strategy, but risks unrealistic numbers and low buy-in.
  • Bottom-up (participative): each manager builds their numbers, rolled up. Higher buy-in, better local information, but slow and prone to budget slack.
  • Hybrid: management sets targets and capital allocation; teams build operational details to fit. Most common.
Slack

Budget slack = padding — managers who set their own numbers tend to under-promise, building in a cushion. Detect it via benchmarking, peer review, and zero-based questioning of base assumptions.

Practice 1 — Budget purposes

Which is NOT a primary purpose of a budget?

  • Planning
  • Coordination
  • Tax minimization
  • Performance evaluation
Why: Tax minimization is a separate exercise. The four canonical budget purposes: plan, coordinate, communicate, control (evaluate). Budgets influence decisions that have tax effects, but tax planning isn't their main job.

Practice 2 — Top-down vs bottom-up

A primary disadvantage of bottom-up (participative) budgeting is:

  • Risk of budget slack from managers padding their numbers
  • Lack of coordination across departments
  • Slow strategic alignment
  • All of these
Why: Slack (sandbagging) is the classic bottom-up problem. The other downsides also exist (slower process, harder coordination), but slack is the singular issue most often cited. Hybrid approaches mitigate it.

Practice 3 — Effective targets

A budget target should be:

  • Easily achievable to maintain morale
  • Challenging but attainable with competent effort
  • Theoretically optimal regardless of feasibility
  • Set by accounting independent of operations
Why: Stretch-but-attainable maximizes motivational pull. Easy targets create complacency; impossible targets get gamed. Operations input is essential — accounting can't set realistic operational targets alone.
Unit 2 · C.2 Methodologies

Master budget — the integrated annual plan

The master budget is the comprehensive annual plan: a sequence of interlocking budgets feeding from the sales forecast through to projected financial statements. The pieces connect — change the sales forecast and every downstream budget shifts.

§2.1The flow

  1. Sales budget (the starting driver) — units × price.
  2. Production budget — sales + ending inventory − beginning inventory.
  3. Direct materials budget — production × per-unit need + ending RM − beginning RM.
  4. Direct labor budget — production × per-unit hours × rate.
  5. Manufacturing overhead budget — variable per-unit + fixed.
  6. Selling & admin budget.
  7. Cash budget — receipts − disbursements.
  8. Pro-forma IS & BS.
Production = Sales units + Desired End FG − Beg FG
RM purchases = (Production × per-unit need) + Desired End RM − Beg RM

§2.2Why sales is the keystone

The sales forecast is the most uncertain input — and it drives everything else. A small forecasting error compounds through production, materials, labor, and cash. Most variance analyses begin by asking how much of the gap traces back to a sales-volume miss.

Practice 1 — Production budget

Sales forecast 12,000 units; desired ending FG 1,500; beginning FG 1,000. Production budget:

  • 12,500 units
  • 14,500 units
  • 11,500 units
  • 10,500 units
Why: Production = Sales + End FG − Beg FG = 12,000 + 1,500 − 1,000 = 12,500. Build for what you'll sell PLUS the inventory you want to end with, MINUS what's already on hand.

Practice 2 — RM purchases

Production needs 50,000 lbs of material this period. Desired end RM 8,000 lbs; beginning RM 5,000 lbs. Required purchases:

  • 50,000 lbs
  • 47,000 lbs
  • 53,000 lbs
  • 63,000 lbs
Why: Purchases = Need + End RM − Beg RM = 50K + 8K − 5K = 53,000 lbs. Same equation pattern as production: cover usage + desired ending − beginning.

Practice 3 — Master-budget keystone

The first (driving) budget in a master budget is typically the:

  • Production budget
  • Cash budget
  • Capital budget
  • Sales budget
Why: Sales drives everything downstream. Production is built to support sales; materials and labor are built to support production; cash is built from receipts and disbursements driven by all the above.
Unit 3 · C.2 Methodologies

Flexible budget — adjust to actual volume

A static budget is locked at one expected volume. A flexible budget adjusts variable costs to the volume that actually occurred — separating volume effects from spending efficiency.

§3.1The mechanic

Flexible-budget formula for each line: Fixed cost + (Variable rate × Actual volume). Comparing actual to flexible budget reveals spending/efficiency variances; comparing flexible to static reveals volume variance.

Static budgetFlexible budgetActual
Volume10,000 units9,500 units9,500 units
Variable cost ($3/unit)$30,000$28,500$29,000
Fixed cost$50,000$50,000$51,000

Flex-vs-actual variance: $500 unfavorable spending (variable) + $1,000 unfavorable spending (fixed) = $1,500 U. Static-vs-flex: $1,500 favorable volume variance for variable costs.

Why both

Comparing actual to STATIC mixes volume and efficiency effects. The flexible budget cleanly separates them — fundamental to fair performance evaluation.

Practice 1 — Flex calculation

Variable cost rate $4/unit; fixed $20K. Flex budget at 5,000 units:

  • $20,000
  • $40,000
  • $25,000
  • $45,000
Why: $20K fixed + ($4 × 5,000) = $20K + $20K = $40,000. The variable component flexes; fixed doesn't.

Practice 2 — Variance attribution

Comparing the flexible budget to actual results isolates:

  • Spending and efficiency variances
  • Volume variance only
  • Strategic variances
  • Pricing variances only
Why: Flex-to-actual = spending/efficiency (volume already matches). Static-to-flex = pure volume effect. The "two-step" (static → flex → actual) split is the foundation of CMA Section C variance analysis.

Practice 3 — Fixed cost in flex

In a flexible budget, fixed costs:

  • Increase proportionally with volume
  • Vary linearly with sales dollars
  • Stay constant within the relevant range
  • Are excluded
Why: Fixed = fixed by definition. The flex budget keeps fixed costs unchanged and adjusts only the variable portion to actual volume.
Unit 4 · C.2 Methodologies

Rolling budget & zero-based budget

Two alternatives to the traditional once-a-year process. Rolling (continuous) budgets always look 12 months ahead; zero-based budgets (ZBB) rebuild every line from scratch each cycle.

§4.1Rolling budget

As each month closes, drop it and add a new month at the end of the horizon. Always 12 months of forward visibility. Pros: continuously fresh, encourages ongoing planning. Cons: more work; requires good systems.

§4.2Zero-based budget

Each manager justifies every line from $0 — no historical baseline accepted. Reveals slack, surfaces unjustified spending, but is expensive to operate and exhausting if done annually. Often run on a 3-5 year cycle, with incremental budgeting in between.

ApproachBest when
Traditional (incremental)Stable environment; budgets refined annually from prior year
RollingVolatile environment; need continuous re-forecast
Zero-basedCost reduction needed; suspect of accumulated slack
Activity-basedWant budgets tied to drivers, not historical accounts

Practice 1 — Rolling budget feature

A rolling (continuous) budget:

  • Is rebuilt from $0 each year
  • Covers a 5-year horizon
  • Always maintains a fixed forward horizon (e.g. 12 months)
  • Is used only by non-profits
Why: The defining feature: as a period closes, a new period is added at the end of the horizon, so the forward view stays constant. ZBB rebuilds; rolling re-extends.

Practice 2 — ZBB advantage

A primary advantage of zero-based budgeting is:

  • Speed and simplicity
  • Forces justification of every spending line, surfacing slack
  • Eliminates need for management review
  • Lower preparation cost than incremental budgeting
Why: ZBB starts from a blank slate — every dollar must be justified. Cost reduction efforts often deploy ZBB precisely for this reason. The trade-off: it's labor-intensive and tiring.

Practice 3 — Activity-based budgeting

Activity-based budgeting differs from traditional budgeting by:

  • Eliminating fixed-cost categories
  • Building budgets only from prior-year actuals
  • Using a rolling horizon
  • Tying budgets to activity drivers (orders, setups, customers) rather than GL accounts
Why: Activity-based budgeting mirrors ABC: budget by activity volume × cost per activity. More closely linked to operational reality than account-by-account incrementing. Often paired with ABC for end-to-end activity-driven costing/budgeting.
Unit 5 · C.3 Annual Plan

Operational budgets — sales, production, materials, labor

Operational budgets translate the sales forecast into action: how many units to produce, how much material to buy, how much labor to hire, how much overhead to apply.

§5.1The cascade

We saw the equations in Unit 2; here's how they fit together as policy:

  • Production budget reflects an inventory policy (e.g. "carry 1 month of forward sales as ending FG").
  • Materials budget reflects a vendor relationship and lead time (e.g. "order to maintain 2 weeks of safety stock").
  • Labor budget reflects hiring/firing constraints, union agreements, training cycles.
  • OH budget reflects predetermined rates from Section D — variable rate × planned activity + budgeted fixed.

§5.2Stable production with seasonal sales

If sales are seasonal but production must be steady (capital-intensive plant, skilled labor), the production budget produces evenly and inventory absorbs the swing — building before peak and drawing down during peak. The trade-off: carrying-cost vs scaling-cost.

Practice 1 — Inventory policy effect

A company decides to RAISE its desired ending FG inventory from 500 to 1,500 units. Holding sales forecast constant, the production budget will:

  • Increase by 1,000 units
  • Decrease by 1,000 units
  • Stay the same
  • Decrease to half
Why: Production = Sales + End FG − Beg FG. Raising End FG by 1,000 raises production by 1,000. Carrying more inventory has cost — usually justified by smoother production or uncertainty buffers.

Practice 2 — Direct labor budget

Production budget 8,000 units; standard labor 0.5 hours/unit; rate $20/hr. Direct labor budget:

  • $160,000
  • $40,000
  • $80,000
  • $320,000
Why: Hours = 8,000 × 0.5 = 4,000. Cost = 4,000 × $20 = $80,000.

Practice 3 — Stable production

A toy maker with strong Q4 holiday sales but year-round labor force most likely uses:

  • Just-in-time production with no inventory
  • Seasonal layoffs and re-hiring
  • Production that exactly matches monthly sales
  • Stable production with inventory build before Q4
Why: Stable production + inventory absorbs the seasonality. Seasonal layoffs are expensive and damage workforce morale; matching monthly production needs surge capacity. Most large seasonal manufacturers smooth production this way.
Unit 6 · C.3 Annual Plan

Financial & capital budgets

After operations are budgeted, two more layers complete the master plan: the financial budget (cash budget + pro-forma BS) and the capital budget (long-term asset additions, deeper than the annual cycle).

§6.1Cash budget

Receipts (collections from sales given AR aging) − Disbursements (payments for materials given AP aging, labor, overhead, S&A, taxes, capex). A negative running balance triggers borrowing; surpluses can be invested or used to reduce debt.

Ending cash = Beginning cash + Receipts − Disbursements ± Financing
Minimum cash balance often required (e.g. compensating balance, working-capital cushion)

§6.2Capital budget

Long-term decisions — equipment purchases, facility expansion, acquisitions. Evaluated separately because they cross multiple periods and use NPV / IRR / payback (CMA Part 2 territory in depth). The annual capital budget reflects which approved projects spend cash this year.

Connection

Capital decisions feed the master plan multiple ways: depreciation hits the IS, interest on financing debt hits the IS, asset additions hit the BS, and cash outlays hit the cash budget.

Practice 1 — Cash budget components

A cash budget primarily includes:

  • Net income from the income statement
  • Cash receipts and disbursements based on AR/AP aging and other timing
  • Depreciation expense
  • Equity issuances at fair value
Why: Cash budget tracks actual cash movement, not accrual-basis NI. Depreciation never appears (non-cash). Equity issuances appear at the cash received, not at FV of shares (which equals cash for new issuances).

Practice 2 — Receipt timing

Sales of $100K in March, of which 20% collected in March, 60% in April, 20% in May. April cash receipt from March sales:

  • $20,000
  • $80,000
  • $100,000
  • $60,000
Why: 60% of $100K = $60,000. Cash budgets translate sales (the IS recognition month) into cash receipts spread across collection months per the AR aging policy.

Practice 3 — Capital vs operating

A capital budget primarily addresses:

  • Multi-period investments in long-term assets
  • Daily working-capital management
  • Annual income forecasting
  • Marketing campaign allocation
Why: Capital = capex / long-lived asset additions evaluated over multiple periods (NPV, IRR, payback). Annual operating budgets handle the year-by-year cash effects of approved capital projects.
