& Budgeting.

Unit 1 · C.1 Budgeting Concepts

Why budget — purposes, performance goals, participation

Budgets serve four purposes: plan (set direction), coordinate (align functions), communicate (translate strategy into operating numbers), and control (provide a benchmark for variance analysis). When any of those purposes is neglected, the budget becomes friction rather than fuel.

§1.1Effective performance goals

Targets too easy → no motivation. Too hard → demoralization or gaming. Effective targets are challenging but attainable — typically requiring above-average effort but reachable by competent execution.

Target typeUse caseRisk
Easy / sandbaggedRare — sometimes during downturns to maintain moraleManager complacency, missed opportunity
Stretch / challengingMost operational budgetsBest motivation; some gaming
Ideal / theoreticalEngineering benchmark; never the bonus targetDemoralization if used for evaluation

§1.2Top-down vs bottom-up vs hybrid

  • Top-down (imposed) — management sets numbers; departments execute. Fast, strategically aligned, but risks unrealistic targets and low buy-in.
  • Bottom-up (participative) — managers build their own numbers; rolled up. Higher buy-in, better local information, but slow and prone to budget slack.
  • Hybrid — management sets financial targets and capital allocation; teams build operational details to fit. Most common.

§1.3Budget slack and how to detect it

Slack = padding. When managers know their bonuses depend on hitting budget, they have a structural incentive to under-promise. Detection tools:

  • Year-over-year variance pattern (consistent favorable variances suggest sandbagging).
  • Benchmarking against peers.
  • Zero-based questioning of base assumptions.
  • Separating planning targets from compensation targets.
The Goodhart trap

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Tying compensation tightly to a single budget number creates incentives to hit that number at the expense of unmeasured outcomes (quality, customer satisfaction, long-term investment).

Practice 1 — Budget purposes

Which is NOT one of the primary purposes of a budget?

  • Planning
  • Coordination
  • Tax minimization
  • Performance evaluation (control)
Why: Tax minimization is a separate exercise (it influences planning decisions, but isn't a budget purpose). The four canonical purposes: plan, coordinate, communicate, control.

Practice 2 — Slack source

Budget slack is most associated with:

  • Bottom-up (participative) budgeting
  • Top-down imposed budgeting
  • Zero-based budgeting
  • Activity-based budgeting
Why: Managers setting their own targets tend to under-promise. Top-down avoids slack but risks unrealistic targets. ZBB and ABB challenge incrementalism. Hybrid approaches mitigate slack while keeping operational input.

Practice 3 — Effective targets

An effective budget target is:

  • Easily achievable to maintain morale
  • Challenging but attainable with competent effort
  • Theoretically optimal regardless of feasibility
  • Set by accounting alone
Why: Stretch-but-attainable maximizes motivational pull. Easy targets create complacency; impossible targets get gamed (or quit). Operations input is essential — accounting can't set realistic operational targets alone.

Practice 4 — Goodhart's law

Tying executive compensation tightly to a single budget number risks:

  • Lowering tax exposure
  • Improving customer satisfaction
  • Increasing innovation investment
  • Encouraging gaming and sacrificing unmeasured outcomes (Goodhart's law)
Why: Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Hitting the budget might involve cutting customer support, deferring maintenance, or aggressive accounting. Multi-metric scorecards and longer-horizon comp mitigate this.
Unit 2 · C.1 Budgeting Concepts

Characteristics of a successful budgeting process

Beyond the targets themselves, the process determines whether a budget motivates or merely consumes time. Successful processes share a small set of structural features.

§2.1What makes a budget process work

  • Top management commitment — visible CEO/CFO support; budget enforcement is taken seriously.
  • Clear linkage to strategy — financial targets cascade from strategic plan, not built in isolation.
  • Realistic and challenging — calibrated to be motivating, not demoralizing.
  • Participation — those held accountable have a voice in setting numbers.
  • Timeliness — budget completed before the period starts; variance reports follow promptly.
  • Information system — actuals captured at the same level of detail as the budget.
  • Flexibility — re-forecast when conditions change materially.
  • Performance feedback — variance discussions focus on root cause and learning, not just blame.

§2.2Common pitfalls

PitfallSymptomFix
Last year + inflationSame numbers every year, same problemsZBB, driver-based planning
Annual cycle in volatile environmentPlan obsolete by Q2Rolling forecast
Single number, single useTarget tightly tied to comp; gamingSeparate planning, forecasting, comp targets
Late variance reportingQuarterly, after the damageMonthly close discipline; flash reports

§2.3The budget committee and calendar

Larger organizations form a budget committee (CFO, business-unit heads, treasurer) that sets assumptions (sales price, FX, key inflation rates) used uniformly across departments. The budget calendar typically starts 3-6 months before the fiscal year — high-level direction in month 1, detailed builds in months 2-4, consolidation and revisions in months 5-6.

Practice 1 — Critical success factor

The most often-cited critical factor for a successful budget process is:

  • Visible top-management commitment
  • Detailed line-item analysis
  • Strict adherence to historical patterns
  • Long preparation cycle
Why: Without sustained CEO/CFO commitment, the budget becomes paperwork. Detailed analysis matters but can't substitute for leadership signal. Long cycles often signal dysfunction rather than rigor.

Practice 2 — Single-target gaming

A company finds managers consistently hit budget but underperform on customer satisfaction. The likely fix:

  • Tighten the budget further
  • Replace bottom-up with top-down
  • Use a balanced scorecard with multiple metrics
  • Eliminate variance reporting
Why: Single-metric incentives invite gaming of unmeasured dimensions. A balanced scorecard adds non-financial measures (CSAT, quality, retention, innovation) and weighs them in evaluation. Tightening or flipping the process doesn't address the root cause.

Practice 3 — When traditional budgets fail

In a fast-moving industry where Q1's plan is often obsolete by Q2, the most appropriate adaptation is:

  • Skip budgeting entirely
  • Lengthen the budget cycle to two years
  • Stay strict with the annual plan to maintain discipline
  • Adopt a rolling forecast
Why: Rolling forecasts always look 12 months ahead and re-forecast each period. Annual budgets become reference points rather than rigid targets. Many large companies (Statoil, Borealis) abandoned traditional annual budgets in favor of rolling forecasts plus separate strategic plans.

Practice 4 — Budget calendar

A standard corporate budget calendar typically begins:

  • The first day of the fiscal year
  • 3-6 months before the fiscal year
  • The day after Year-end close
  • Whenever convenient
Why: Most large companies start in Q3 of the prior year (e.g., July for a calendar-year fiscal). High-level guidance in month 1, detailed builds in months 2-4, consolidation and revisions in months 5-6, board approval before year-end.
Unit 3 · C.2 Methodologies

Master budget — the integrated annual plan

The master budget is the comprehensive annual plan: a sequence of interlocking budgets feeding from the sales forecast through to projected financial statements. Change one assumption — usually the sales forecast — and every downstream budget shifts.

§3.1The flow

  1. Sales budget (the keystone) — units × price.
  2. Production budget — sales + ending FG − beginning FG.
  3. Direct materials budget — production × per-unit need + ending RM − beginning RM.
  4. Direct labor budget — production × per-unit hours × rate.
  5. Manufacturing overhead budget — variable per-unit + fixed.
  6. Selling & admin budget.
  7. Cash budget — receipts − disbursements.
  8. Pro-forma IS & BS.

Operational budgets (1-6) feed into the financial budgets (7) which roll up into pro-forma statements (8).

§3.2The sales budget

Sales = Units forecast × Price. Sources:

  • Sales force estimates — bottom-up; risks slack.
  • Market analysis — top-down; risks ignoring local intelligence.
  • Statistical methods (regression, seasonality) — math-driven; risks missing structural change.
  • In practice: blend of all three.

§3.3Why sales is the keystone

The sales forecast is the most uncertain input — and it drives everything else. A 10% error in sales propagates into production, materials, labor, and cash. Most variance analyses begin by asking how much of the gap traces back to a sales-volume miss.

Sensitivity analysis

Mature finance teams build the master budget as a flexible model that can be re-run with high/base/low sales scenarios. The base case becomes the budget; high/low cases inform contingency planning.

Practice 1 — Master-budget keystone

The first (driving) budget in a master budget is typically the:

  • Production budget
  • Cash budget
  • Capital budget
  • Sales budget
Why: Sales drives everything downstream. Production is built to support sales; materials and labor support production; cash flows from receipts and disbursements driven by all the above.

Practice 2 — Sales budget calculation

Forecast 25,000 units × $40 average price. Sales budget:

  • $1,000,000
  • $25,000,000
  • $40,000
  • $625
Why: 25,000 × $40 = $1,000,000.

Practice 3 — Source mix

A robust sales forecast typically blends:

  • Only sales-force estimates
  • Only statistical regression
  • Sales-force estimates, market analysis, and statistical methods
  • Whatever the CFO prefers that quarter
Why: Each source has biases — sales-force can sandbag; market analysis can miss local detail; statistics can miss structural change. Blending them, with a calibration discussion to reconcile, produces the most defensible forecast.

Practice 4 — Sensitivity

Re-running the master budget with high/base/low sales scenarios is most useful for:

  • Contingency planning and identifying which downstream budgets are most sensitive to sales
  • Reducing the actual budget
  • Avoiding budget revisions
  • Eliminating cash forecasts
Why: Scenarios surface what would change in production, headcount, cash needs if reality deviates from base case. The base becomes the budget; high/low feed contingency response plans (when to add a shift, when to delay a hire).
Unit 4 · C.2 Methodologies

Operational budget mechanics — production, materials, labor

After sales is set, three downstream calculations cascade. Production = sales + ending FG − beginning FG. Materials = production usage + ending RM − beginning RM. Labor = production × per-unit hours × rate.

§4.1Production budget

Production = Sales units + Desired End FG − Beginning FG

Reflects an inventory policy ("carry 1 month of forward sales as ending FG"). Higher End FG raises production; lower End FG can reduce production below sales (if drawing down inventory).

§4.2Direct materials budget

RM purchases = (Production × need per unit) + Desired End RM − Beginning RM

Reflects vendor relationships, lead times, and safety-stock policy. Same arithmetic as production but applied to materials.

§4.3Direct labor budget

Labor cost = Production × Standard hours per unit × Rate per hour

Reflects hiring/firing constraints, overtime policy, and union agreements. Constrained labor (hard to ramp up/down) often pushes companies toward stable production with seasonal inventory rather than chasing demand.

§4.4Stable production with seasonal sales

If sales are seasonal but production must be steady (capital-intensive plant, skilled labor), the production budget produces evenly and inventory absorbs the swing — building before peak and drawing down during peak. Trade-off: carrying cost (warehousing, capital tied up in inventory) vs scaling cost (overtime, hiring/firing, equipment surge).

Matching inventory effect

Production exceeding sales = inventory build (cash outflow). Production below sales = inventory draw-down (cash conserved). The cash budget reflects this through changes in working capital.

Practice 1 — Production budget

Sales forecast 30,000; desired end FG 4,500; beginning FG 5,000. Production:

  • 29,500
  • 30,500
  • 39,500
  • 21,500
Why: 30,000 + 4,500 − 5,000 = 29,500. Inventory drawdown (end < beginning) reduces required production below sales.

Practice 2 — Effect of raising End FG

Holding sales forecast constant, doubling the desired ending FG inventory will:

  • Halve the production budget
  • Cut materials needs in half
  • Increase production by the change in ending FG
  • Have no effect
Why: Production = Sales + End FG − Beg FG. Raising End FG raises production by the same amount. More inventory has carrying cost — usually justified by smoother production or uncertainty buffers.

Practice 3 — RM purchases

Production needs 100,000 lbs of material. Desired end RM 20,000 lbs; beginning RM 15,000 lbs. Required purchases:

  • 100,000 lbs
  • 85,000 lbs
  • 120,000 lbs
  • 105,000 lbs
Why: 100K + 20K − 15K = 105,000 lbs. Same equation pattern as production: usage + desired ending − beginning.

Practice 4 — Labor cost

Production 8,000 units; standard 0.5 hr/unit; rate $25/hr. Direct labor cost:

  • $200,000
  • $100,000
  • $50,000
  • $400,000
Why: 8,000 × 0.5 × $25 = 4,000 × $25 = $100,000.

Practice 5 — Stable production decision

A toy maker with strong Q4 holiday sales but year-round skilled labor most likely:

  • Builds inventory steadily through Q1-Q3 to absorb peak demand
  • Lays off workers Q1-Q3 and re-hires for Q4
  • Produces only in November and December
  • Outsources all manufacturing
Why: Stable production through the year + inventory build is the standard pattern when labor is hard to ramp. Trades carrying cost for steady employment, capacity utilization, and quality consistency.
Unit 5 · C.2 Methodologies

Flexible budget — adjust to actual volume

A static budget is locked at one expected volume. A flexible budget adjusts variable costs to the volume that actually occurred — separating volume effects from spending efficiency. Foundation of fair performance evaluation.

§5.1The mechanic

Flexible-budget formula for each line: Fixed cost + (Variable rate × Actual volume). Then compare:

  • Static vs Flex = volume variance.
  • Flex vs Actual = spending / efficiency variance.
StaticFlexActual
Volume10,000 units9,500 units9,500 units
Variable cost ($3/unit)$30,000$28,500$29,000
Fixed cost$50,000$50,000$51,000
Total$80,000$78,500$80,000

Reading the table:

  • Static-vs-flex: $80K − $78.5K = $1,500 favorable VOLUME variance for variable costs (we produced 500 fewer units, saving $1,500).
  • Flex-vs-actual: $80K − $78.5K = $1,500 unfavorable SPENDING variance ($500 unfavorable on variable + $1,000 unfavorable on fixed).

§5.2Why this matters

Comparing actual to STATIC mixes volume and efficiency effects. A manager who produced fewer units (perhaps because of a market slowdown beyond their control) might appear to "underspend" — but they were more efficient or wasteful per unit produced is a separate question. The flexible budget separates the two cleanly.

Bridge to variances

Section D goes deep on price/efficiency/volume variances. The flex budget framework is the foundation: standard cost expectations are flexed to actual volume, then decomposed into rate and quantity effects.

Practice 1 — Flex calculation

Variable cost $4/unit; fixed $20K. Flex budget at 6,500 units:

  • $26,000
  • $46,000
  • $24,000
  • $66,500
Why: $20K + ($4 × 6,500) = $20K + $26K = $46,000.

Practice 2 — Variance attribution

Comparing the FLEXIBLE budget to ACTUAL results isolates:

  • Spending and efficiency variances
  • Volume variance only
  • Strategic variances
  • Pricing variances only
Why: Flex-to-actual = spending/efficiency (volume already matches). Static-to-flex = pure volume effect. The "two-step" split is the foundation of variance analysis.

Practice 3 — Fixed cost in flex

In a flexible budget, fixed costs:

  • Increase proportionally with volume
  • Vary linearly with sales
  • Stay constant within the relevant range
  • Are excluded entirely
Why: Fixed = fixed by definition. The flex budget keeps fixed costs unchanged and adjusts only the variable portion to actual volume.

Practice 4 — Reading variances

Static budget total cost $100K; flexible budget at actual volume $90K; actual cost $94K. The unfavorable spending/efficiency variance is:

  • $10,000 favorable
  • $6,000 favorable
  • $10,000 unfavorable
  • $4,000 unfavorable
Why: Flex vs actual: $94K − $90K = $4K U (spending). Static vs flex: $100K − $90K = $10K favorable volume variance (lower volume than budgeted reduced expected cost).
Unit 6 · C.2 Methodologies

Rolling (continuous) budget

A rolling budget always maintains a fixed forward horizon (most commonly 12 months). As each period closes, drop it and add a new period at the end — so the forward visibility stays constant.

§6.1Mechanic

Imagine a 12-month rolling budget kicked off in January 2024 covering Jan-Dec 2024. As January 2024 closes, drop January and add January 2025 — now the budget covers Feb 2024 through Jan 2025. Continue monthly.

§6.2Pros and cons

ProCon
Continuous forward visibilityMore work — re-forecasting every period
Encourages ongoing planning rather than once-a-yearNeeds strong data systems
Naturally re-prices stale assumptionsCan blur the "annual budget" benchmark for evaluation
Adapts to volatile environmentsSome managers find rolling-target movement disorienting

§6.3Rolling forecasts vs rolling budgets

A subtle distinction: a rolling FORECAST is a periodic projection of likely outcomes, not necessarily a commitment. A rolling BUDGET is a committed plan. Many companies adopt rolling forecasts (12-18 months, for management visibility) alongside an annual budget (the formal commitment used for compensation).

Beyond Budgeting

The "Beyond Budgeting" movement (Borealis, Statoil) abandoned annual budgets entirely in favor of rolling forecasts plus separate strategic targets and relative-performance compensation. Niche but influential.

Practice 1 — Rolling budget definition

A 12-month rolling budget:

  • Always maintains 12 months of forward horizon by adding a new period as one closes
  • Is rebuilt from $0 every year
  • Covers a 24-month strategic span
  • Locks in fixed-cost lines for 12 months
Why: Rolling = always look forward 12 months. ZBB rebuilds; annual budgets stay fixed; rolling re-extends.

Practice 2 — Best fit

Rolling budgets are most beneficial in environments where:

  • Operations are highly stable and predictable
  • Senior management resists planning
  • Variances are calculated annually
  • Conditions change rapidly and the annual plan goes stale
Why: Volatility justifies the extra effort of continuous re-forecasting. In stable industries, the marginal value over annual budgeting is small relative to the cost.

Practice 3 — Forecast vs budget

A rolling FORECAST differs from a rolling BUDGET by being:

  • Computed less frequently
  • Always 24 months long
  • A projection rather than a committed plan
  • Used only by non-profits
Why: Forecast = "what we expect to happen." Budget = "what we commit to make happen." Many large companies maintain both: rolling forecasts for management visibility, annual budgets for board accountability and bonus targets.

Practice 4 — Cost of rolling

A primary disadvantage of rolling budgets is:

  • Reduced forward visibility
  • Higher administrative cost — re-forecasting every period
  • Inability to integrate with strategic planning
  • They cannot be compared to actuals
Why: Rolling needs more frequent updates and good systems. Done by hand in spreadsheets, the cost can outweigh the benefit. Modern EPM tools dramatically reduce the friction.
Unit 7 · C.2 Methodologies

Zero-based & activity-based budgeting

Two alternatives to incremental ("last year + 5%") budgeting. Zero-based budgeting (ZBB) rebuilds every line from $0 each cycle. Activity-based budgeting (ABB) ties spending to operational drivers rather than GL accounts.

§7.1Zero-based budgeting

Each manager justifies every dollar from a $0 baseline — no historical claim accepted. Surfaces slack, surfaces unjustified spending, but is expensive to operate. Often deployed on a 3-5 year cycle, with incremental budgeting in between, or focused on specific cost categories (overhead, S&A) where slack accumulates.

Decision packages: each activity is broken into discrete packages (current level, reduced level, enhanced level), ranked by management, then funded down the priority list until the budget is exhausted.

§7.2Activity-based budgeting

Mirrors ABC (activity-based costing): identify activities and drivers, then budget = activity volume × cost per activity. Connects financial planning to operational reality:

  • Order-processing budget = forecast orders × cost per order.
  • Customer-service budget = forecast calls × cost per call.
  • Engineering-change budget = forecast changes × cost per change.

When activity volumes change, budgets recalculate naturally — closer to a flex-budget mindset.

§7.3Choosing a methodology

ApproachBest when
Incremental (traditional)Stable environment; budgets refined annually from prior year
RollingVolatile environment; continuous re-forecast
Zero-basedCost-reduction needed; suspect of accumulated slack
Activity-basedWant budgets tied to drivers rather than GL accounts
Master / staticStable annual cycle, single budget for all uses
ZBB fatigue

Annual ZBB is exhausting. Most companies that adopt ZBB use it for a one- or two-year reset, then revert to incremental with periodic ZBB refreshes. Ongoing ZBB risks "ZBB theater" — managers learning to defend last year's number with new arguments.

Practice 1 — ZBB advantage

A primary advantage of zero-based budgeting is:

  • Speed and simplicity
  • Forces fresh justification of every spending line, surfacing accumulated slack
  • Eliminates need for management review
  • Lower preparation cost than incremental
Why: ZBB starts from a blank slate — every dollar must be justified. Cost-reduction efforts often deploy ZBB precisely for this reason. The trade-off: it's labor-intensive.

Practice 2 — ABB

Activity-based budgeting differs from traditional budgeting by:

  • Eliminating fixed-cost categories
  • Building budgets only from prior-year actuals
  • Using a rolling horizon
  • Tying budgets to activity drivers (orders, setups, customers) rather than GL accounts
Why: ABB mirrors ABC: budget by activity volume × cost per activity. More closely linked to operational reality than account-by-account incrementing.

Practice 3 — Decision packages

In ZBB, decision packages are:

  • Sealed envelopes opened at year-end
  • Outsourcing contracts
  • Discrete activity bundles ranked and funded down the priority list
  • Software licenses
Why: ZBB decision packages express each activity at multiple service levels (reduced, current, enhanced), let management rank them, and fund top-down until the budget is exhausted. Below-the-line packages get cut.

Practice 4 — When ZBB falters

A risk of using ZBB every year indefinitely is:

  • "ZBB theater" — managers learning to defend last year's number with new arguments rather than genuinely re-evaluating
  • Inability to detect cost increases
  • Reduced flexibility
  • Conflicts with U.S. GAAP
Why: Habituation undermines ZBB's core mechanic — fresh re-evaluation. Periodic ZBB resets (every 3-5 years), or category-focused ZBB (overhead only), preserve the discipline without exhausting the organization.
Unit 8 · C.3 Annual Plan

Cash budget construction

After operations are budgeted (Units 4-5), translate them into cash flow. The cash budget tracks receipts and disbursements month-by-month, signals when borrowing or investment is needed, and is the master budget's most important "reality check" — accrual-basis NI without a cash plan can mask a coming liquidity crisis.

§8.1Structure

Ending cash = Beginning cash + Receipts − Disbursements ± Financing

Common categories:

  • Receipts: cash sales, AR collections (per aging), interest, other income.
  • Disbursements: AP payments (per terms), labor, overhead cash, S&A, taxes, capex, dividends, debt service.
  • Financing: short-term borrowing if balance falls below minimum; debt repayment / investment if surplus.

§8.2Translating sales into receipts

Most sales are credit sales; cash arrives later per the AR aging policy. Example: 30% in month of sale, 60% next month, 10% second month.

Sales MarMar receipt (30%)Apr receipt (60%)May receipt (10%)
$200K$60K$120K$20K

§8.3Disbursements and minimum balance

Companies typically have a minimum cash balance requirement (compensating balance with bank, working-capital cushion, regulatory floor). When projected cash falls below the minimum, borrowing kicks in. When surplus accumulates, debt is paid down or invested.

Non-cash items

Depreciation NEVER appears in a cash budget. Bad-debt expense is a non-cash accrual — affects NI but not cash. Only actual cash movements appear.

Practice 1 — Cash budget exclusions

Which item does NOT appear in a cash budget?

  • Customer cash collections
  • Tax payments
  • Depreciation expense
  • Capital expenditures
Why: Depreciation is non-cash. Bad debt provision is also non-cash. Only items affecting actual cash flow belong.

Practice 2 — Receipt timing

Sales $300K in May. Collection: 25% same month, 65% next month, 10% second month. June receipts from May sales:

  • $75,000
  • $195,000
  • $225,000
  • $300,000
Why: 65% × $300K = $195,000. The remaining $30K (10%) arrives in July.

Practice 3 — Multi-month aggregation

Sales: Jan $100K, Feb $200K, Mar $300K. Same 25/65/10 collection pattern. March cash receipts total:

  • $215,000
  • $300,000
  • $165,000
  • $75,000
Why: March receipts = 25% × March sales + 65% × Feb sales + 10% × Jan sales = 0.25×$300K + 0.65×$200K + 0.10×$100K = $75K + $130K + $10K = $215K.

Practice 4 — Minimum cash balance

Projected ending cash $40K; minimum required balance $100K. Most likely action:

  • Pay down existing debt
  • Invest surplus in marketable securities
  • No action needed
  • Borrow $60K (or more) to maintain the minimum
Why: Cash budget signals a $60K shortfall vs the $100K minimum. Borrow at least $60K (often slightly more for safety). Surplus actions only apply when projected cash exceeds the minimum.

Practice 5 — Source of cash budget data

The cash budget primarily draws cash inflows and outflows from:

  • Pro-forma income statement
  • Pro-forma balance sheet
  • Operational budgets adjusted for AR/AP timing
  • Capital budget alone
Why: Sales budget + AR aging → receipts. RM/labor/OH budgets + AP terms → disbursements. Pro-forma IS uses accrual NI; cash budget needs cash-basis flows. The capital budget feeds capex disbursements.
Unit 9 · C.3 Annual Plan

Pro-forma financial statements

After operational and cash budgets are set, the master budget closes with pro-forma (projected) financial statements. The pro-forma IS comes from operational budgets; the pro-forma BS combines beginning balances with projected changes; the pro-forma cash flow reconciles them.

§9.1Pro-forma income statement

Built from the operational budgets:

  • Sales (from sales budget)
  • − COGS (from production + materials + labor + variable OH at cost per unit × units sold)
  • = Gross profit
  • − Selling & admin (from S&A budget)
  • − Fixed manufacturing OH (from OH budget)
  • = Operating income
  • ± Non-operating (interest expense from debt schedule, etc.)
  • − Income tax
  • = Net income

§9.2Pro-forma balance sheet

For each line item, the projected ending balance is:

Ending balance = Beginning balance + Increases − Decreases

Examples:

  • Cash — from cash budget ending balance.
  • AR — from receipts schedule (sales − collections + opening AR).
  • Inventory — from production budget ending FG and RM.
  • PP&E (net) — opening + capex − depreciation.
  • AP — opening + purchases − payments.
  • Long-term debt — opening + new borrowings − repayments.
  • Retained earnings — opening + NI − dividends.

§9.3Pro-forma cash flow

Reconciles NI (accrual) to change in cash. Three sections (operating, investing, financing) per ASC 230 / IAS 7. The indirect method starts from NI and adjusts:

  • Add back non-cash expenses (depreciation, amortization, bad-debt expense)
  • Subtract changes in operating assets (AR up = cash down)
  • Add changes in operating liabilities (AP up = cash up)
  • Subtract investing cash outflows (capex)
  • Add financing cash inflows (new debt, equity issuance) minus outflows (debt repayment, dividends)
The reconciliation

The cash budget (Unit 8) and the pro-forma cash-flow statement should agree. If they don't, find the timing error — usually in the AR / AP collection patterns or the depreciation roll-forward.

Practice 1 — Pro-forma BS components

The ending balance of accounts receivable on a pro-forma balance sheet equals:

  • Sales − COGS
  • Beginning AR + Sales − Cash collections
  • Cash budget ending balance
  • Net income
Why: AR roll-forward: opening + new credit sales − collections during the period. Pulled from the sales budget and the AR collection schedule.

Practice 2 — Retained earnings

Beginning RE $500K; pro-forma NI $300K; planned cash dividends $80K. Ending RE:

  • $220K
  • $300K
  • $880K
  • $720K
Why: $500K + $300K − $80K = $720K. Standard retained-earnings roll-forward.

Practice 3 — Indirect cash flow

Under the indirect method, an INCREASE in accounts receivable during the period:

  • Increases cash from operations
  • Has no effect on the cash flow statement
  • Decreases cash from operations
  • Appears in financing activities
Why: Higher AR means more sales recognized than cash collected — cash didn't increase as much as NI. Subtract the AR increase. (CA up → cash down. CL up → cash up.)

Practice 4 — Reconciliation check

If the cash-budget ending balance disagrees with the pro-forma BS cash line, the error most likely lies in:

  • Timing assumptions (collections, payments) or depreciation roll-forward
  • The income tax rate
  • The sales price assumption
  • The fixed-cost lines
Why: Cash budget and pro-forma cash flow are two views of the same flows. Disagreement nearly always traces to inconsistent timing patterns (AR collection % differs between schedules) or non-cash items mishandled (depreciation, bad-debt provision).
Unit 10 · C.3 Annual Plan

Capital expenditures budget

Capital decisions — equipment, buildings, software systems, acquisitions — span multiple years and use long-horizon evaluation methods. The annual capital budget is the slice of approved capex that hits cash this year, plus depreciation impacts on the IS.

§10.1The capital budget vs the operating cycle

§10.2Evaluation methods (intro)

Full coverage in CMA Part 2. For FMAA, recognize the basics:

  • Net Present Value (NPV) — sum of discounted future cash flows minus initial investment. Accept if NPV > 0.
  • Internal Rate of Return (IRR) — discount rate that makes NPV = 0. Accept if IRR > required return.
  • Payback period — years until cumulative cash flows repay the investment. Simple but ignores time value and post-payback flows.

§10.3How capital decisions feed the master plan

An approved capital project flows through multiple budgets:

  • Cash budget — capex spending in the year.
  • Pro-forma BS — increase in PP&E.
  • Pro-forma IS — depreciation expense over future periods; interest expense if financed with debt.
  • Operational budgets — efficiency gains or new revenue from the new capacity.
Don't confuse

The annual capital budget is the year's cash outflow for approved projects. The capital plan / strategic capex schedule is the multi-year forward-look. The two work together but answer different questions.

Practice 1 — Capital vs operating

A capital budget primarily addresses:

  • Multi-period investments in long-term assets
  • Daily working-capital management
  • Annual income forecasting
  • Marketing campaigns
Why: Capital = capex evaluated over multiple periods (NPV, IRR, payback). Annual operating budgets handle the year-by-year cash effects of approved capital projects.

Practice 2 — NPV decision

A project has NPV of −$200K at the company's required return. Decision:

  • Accept — NPV is on a different scale
  • Accept if IRR > 0
  • Reject — negative NPV destroys value
  • Defer indefinitely
Why: NPV < 0 → project doesn't earn the required return → reject (assuming no strategic option value not captured in the cash flows). Accept if NPV > 0; tie-break with IRR for mutually exclusive projects.

Practice 3 — Payback weakness

A weakness of the payback method as a capital-budgeting tool is that it:

  • Is too complex for non-finance managers
  • Cannot be calculated for new investments
  • Ignores the cost of debt
  • Ignores the time value of money and cash flows after the payback period
Why: Two big issues: time value (a $1 in year 5 ≠ $1 today) and post-payback ignored (a project that pays back in 3 years and runs another 10 vs one that pays back in 3 and dies — payback says equal). NPV/IRR fix both.

Practice 4 — Capital project IS impact

Approved capex of $5M financed with debt. The pro-forma INCOME STATEMENT impact in year 1 (assuming 10-year SL depreciation, 5% interest):

  • $5M expense
  • $500K depreciation + $250K interest = $750K total expense
  • $0 — capex is balance sheet only
  • $5M depreciation
Why: $5M / 10 years = $500K depreciation. $5M × 5% = $250K interest. The full $5M doesn't expense in year 1 — that's the whole point of capitalizing rather than expensing.
