Unit 01 · A.1 · Foundations

The Language of Accounting

Before you can record a single transaction, you need vocabulary. Accounting describes what an entity owns, owes, and has earned through a small set of carefully defined terms — and a few large rule-makers (FASB, IASB) decide what counts as proper usage.

§1.1What accounting actually does

Accounting is the process of identifying, recording, and communicating economic events of a business to people who must make decisions about it. It produces information; the information is consumed by managers (internal users) and by investors, lenders, regulators, and tax authorities (external users).

BranchAudienceOutputRules
Financial accountingExternal usersGeneral-purpose statements (BS, IS, SCF, equity)GAAP / IFRS — public, audited
Managerial accountingInternal — managersBudgets, variance reports, cost analysisWhatever helps the decision; no external rules
Tax accountingTax authoritiesTax returnsIRC and state rules — different from GAAP
AuditingExternal usersOpinion on whether statements fairly presentGAAS / PCAOB / ISA

§1.2Foundational assumptions

  • Economic entity — the business is a separate accounting unit from its owners. Personal expenses do not belong in business books.
  • Going concern — assume the entity will operate long enough to use its assets and pay its liabilities. Justifies historical cost over liquidation values.
  • Monetary unit — record only items measurable in money, in a single stable currency.
  • Periodicity — split continuous economic activity into reporting periods (month, quarter, year). Forces estimates because period boundaries cut through ongoing events.
  • Accrual basis — recognise revenues when earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of cash timing.

§1.3Cash basis vs. accrual basis

Customer prepays for serviceRevenue when receivedLiability (unearned revenue) until earned

GAAP / IFRS = accrual

The cash basis is permitted only for very small businesses or for personal taxes. All FMAA / CMA / CPA examination content assumes accrual unless stated otherwise.

§1.4Matching, revenue recognition, historical cost, full disclosure

  • Revenue recognition — recognise revenue when the performance obligation is satisfied (control of goods/services transfers to the customer). ASC 606 / IFRS 15.
  • Matching (expense recognition) — recognise expenses in the same period as the revenues they helped produce.
  • Historical cost — generally record assets at the cost paid; some classes (financial instruments, biological assets) move to fair value subsequently.
  • Full disclosure — include in the notes anything material to the reader's decision.

§1.6Qualitative characteristics of useful information

  • Fundamental: relevance (predictive, confirmatory, material) and faithful representation (complete, neutral, free from material error).
  • Enhancing: comparability, verifiability, timeliness, understandability.
  • Cost constraint: information must be worth more than it costs to produce.

Unit 02 · A.2 · Bookkeeping Cycle

Accounting Equation & Double-Entry Foundations

Every transaction touches at least two accounts in equal-and-opposite ways. The fundamental equation — Assets = Liabilities + Equity — must hold after every entry, no exceptions. Master this and the rest of the cycle is mechanical.

§2.1The accounting equation

The equation
Assets = Liabilities + Owners' Equity
Expanded equation
Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings + (Revenues − Expenses) − Dividends

§2.2The five element families

ElementExamplesNormal balanceStatement
AssetsCash, A/R, inventory, PP&EDebitBalance sheet
LiabilitiesA/P, notes payable, unearned revCreditBalance sheet
Equity (capital)Common stock, retained earningsCreditBalance sheet / equity
RevenuesSales, service revenue, interest incomeCreditIncome statement → RE
ExpensesCOGS, rent, salaries, depreciationDebitIncome statement → RE

§2.3The debits-and-credits rules

Mnemonic: DEAD CLER — Debits increase Expenses, Assets, and Dividends; Credits increase Liabilities, Equity, and Revenues. Sum DR = Sum CR for every entry; the equation holds after every transaction.

§2.5Contra accounts

A contra account has the opposite normal balance of its companion: it offsets a balance that would otherwise be over-stated. Common examples:

Unit 03 · A.2 · Bookkeeping Cycle

Source Documents & Analyzing Transactions

Every journal entry begins with a piece of paper (or electronic record) — a sales invoice, a vendor bill, a check stub, a cash-register tape. Source documents establish that the transaction happened and supply the data the entry needs.

§3.1What counts as a transaction

An accounting transaction is an economic event that changes at least one of: assets, liabilities, equity, revenues, or expenses. It must be measurable in money and (usually) involve an external party. Hiring an employee is not a transaction (no obligation yet). Paying that employee at month-end is.

§3.2Common source documents

Vendor billPurchase on creditDR Inventory or Expense · CR A/P

§3.3Three steps to analyze any transaction

  1. Identify the accounts touched and their element family.
  2. Decide which direction each touched account moves — increase or decrease.
  3. Translate to debit/credit using DEAD CLER and confirm Σ DR = Σ CR.
A reliable mental script

"What did we get? What did we give up? What promise was made or paid? Translate to debit/credit." Apply this and you will rarely book in the wrong direction.

§3.5Internal vs. external transactions

  • External — exchanges with parties outside the entity (customers, vendors, lenders, employees).
  • Internal — economic events within the entity (supplies consumed, equipment depreciation, write-down of obsolete inventory). Captured at period end via adjusting entries (Units 7–9).

§3.6Materiality

Strict accounting recognises every transaction. Materiality permits abbreviated treatment (e.g., expensing a $30 stapler instead of capitalising it). Materiality is judged from the user's perspective: would the omission or misstatement change a reasonable decision?

Unit 04 · A.2 · Bookkeeping Cycle

Recording Journal Entries

The general journal is the chronological "book of original entry." Every transaction lands here first — date, accounts, amounts, brief explanation — before being posted to the ledger.

§4.1Anatomy of a journal entry

  • Date — when the transaction occurred
  • Account titles — debit accounts on the first line(s), credit accounts indented below
  • Amounts — debit column on left, credit column on right
  • Posting reference (PR) — added when posted to the ledger
  • Brief explanation — narrative naming the source document or counterparty
Date     Account                           DR        CR
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Mar 12   Inventory                       2,500
            Accounts Payable                          2,500
         (Purchase of merchandise on account, Inv. #4582)

§4.2Simple vs. compound entries

A simple entry has one debit and one credit. A compound entry has multiple debits and/or credits. Sum DR = Sum CR is still required. Example compound:

DR  Equipment              5,000
    CR  Cash                            1,000
    CR  Notes Payable                   4,000

§4.5Special journals

Real systems break the general journal into special journals for high-volume routine transactions, with the GJ used only for non-routine items:

Special journalRecords
Sales journalAll credit sales (DR A/R · CR Sales)
Purchases journalAll credit purchases of inventory
Cash receipts journalAll cash inflows

§4.6Common journal-entry errors

  • Reversed direction — recording a debit as a credit and vice versa.
  • Transposition — writing $4,500 as $5,400. Difference is divisible by 9.
  • Slide — writing $4,500 as $450 or $45,000. Difference is divisible by 9 (and a power of 10).
  • Wrong account — debiting Repairs Expense instead of Equipment when capitalising. Trial balance unaffected; financial statements wrong.

Unit 05 · A.2 · Bookkeeping Cycle

Posting to the Ledger (T-Accounts)

After the journal records each transaction chronologically, the ledger groups them by account. The "T-account" is the accountant's whiteboard — debits on the left, credits on the right, balance computed as the difference.

§5.1Journal vs. ledger

—JournalLedger
Organised byDate (chronologically)Account
PurposeRecord transactionsCompute balances
Order of useFirst (book of original entry)Second (book of final entry)
RevealsWhat happened on March 12The current cash balance

§5.2The T-account format

                       Cash
        ─────────────────────────────────
        Debit (left)       Credit (right)
        ─────────────────────────────────
  3/1   40,000             3,000   3/2 (rent)
  3/8    9,000             4,000   3/12 (A/P)
  3/18   2,500             1,500   3/25 (div)
        ─────────────────────────────────
  Bal   43,000

The balance is the difference between debit and credit totals, written on the side that has the larger total — the normal balance side for that account family.

§5.3Posting steps

  1. Locate the account in the ledger.
  2. Record the date.
  3. Enter the journal page reference (audit trail).
  4. Write the debit (or credit) amount in the appropriate column.
  5. Update the running balance.

§5.4Subsidiary ledgers and control accounts

For high-cardinality accounts (A/R with 1,000 customers, A/P with 200 vendors, inventory with 50,000 SKUs), companies maintain a subsidiary ledger with one account per customer/vendor/SKU. The general ledger keeps a single control account whose balance equals the sum of the subsidiary balances.

Accounts ReceivableOne account per customerStatement of customer balances; collections
Accounts PayableOne account per vendorAging by vendor; payment scheduling
InventoryOne account per SKUStock counts; reorder triggers
PP&EOne account per assetDepreciation schedule per asset

Unit 06 · A.2 · Bookkeeping Cycle

Preparing the Trial Balance & Locating Errors

A trial balance is a list of every account and its balance, columns of debits and credits side-by-side. The columns must match. If they don't, there is a math or posting error to find before period-end statements can be trusted.

§6.1What a trial balance proves (and doesn't)

  • Proves: total debits equal total credits — the equation has not been visibly violated.
  • Does NOT prove: that all transactions are recorded, that the right accounts were used, or that all accounts have correct balances. Errors that affect both sides equally are invisible.

  1. Re-add the columns.
  2. Compute the discrepancy. Test for 9 (transposition/slide) and 2 (wrong-side).
  3. Scan for unusual balances.
  4. Trace back: trial balance → ledger → journal → source document.
  5. Reconcile control account against subsidiary ledger.

Example: a $500 cash sale was recorded as DR Cash 500 / CR Service Revenue 500 when it should have been DR Cash 500 / CR Sales 500. Correcting entry: DR Service Revenue 500 / CR Sales 500.

Depreciation Methods & Changes in Estimate

Long-lived assets give up service over time. Depreciation allocates their cost across the periods that benefit, recognised through a recurring adjusting entry. Useful life and salvage are estimates — and estimates can change.

§9.1Why depreciate

The matching principle requires that the cost of using a long-lived asset be expensed in the periods in which the asset helps generate revenue. Depreciation is a cost-allocation process, not a valuation process.

§9.2Depreciable cost = Cost − Salvage value

TermDefinition
Cost (capitalised basis)Purchase price + freight + installation + sales tax + any cost to ready the asset for use
Salvage / residual valueEstimated proceeds at end of useful life
Useful lifeEstimated period the asset will be used (years, units, hours)
Depreciable baseCost − Salvage value
Net book value (NBV)Cost − Accumulated depreciation

§9.3Three classic methods

Straight-line
Annual Depreciation = (Cost − Salvage) ÷ Useful life (years)
Units-of-production
Per unit = (Cost − Salvage) ÷ Total estimated units · Period = Per unit × Units this period
Double-declining balance (DDB)
Annual Depreciation = NBV at start of year × (2 ÷ Useful life)
Salvage ignored in calc; cap last year so NBV = Salvage.

§9.4Worked example — same asset, three methods

Machine $50,000, salvage $5,000, life 5 years (or 90,000 units).

YearStraight-lineUnits-of-productionDouble-declining (40%)
1$9,000$10,000 (20,000 units)$20,000 (50,000 × 40%)
2$9,000$12,500 (25,000 units)$12,000
3$9,000$11,000 (22,000 units)$7,200
4$9,000$8,000 (16,000 units)$4,320

Same total depreciation; only the timing differs.

§9.5The recurring adjusting entry

DR  Depreciation Expense    9,000
    CR  Accumulated Depreciation        9,000

The asset itself is never credited (it remains at original cost). Accumulated depreciation, a contra-asset, grows. NBV = Cost − Accumulated Depreciation.

§9.6Changes in estimate — prospective only

  1. Compute current NBV.
  2. Compute remaining depreciable base = NBV − new salvage.
  3. Allocate over remaining useful life.

Worked: After 3 years SL of $50,000 asset, Acc Dep $27,000, NBV $23,000. Estimate revised to 7 total years and salvage $3,000. Remaining depreciable = $23,000 − $3,000 = $20,000. Remaining life = 4 years. New annual = $5,000.

Prospective only

Changes in estimate are NEVER applied retroactively. ASC 250 / IAS 8.

§9.7Disposal of an asset

Three steps: (1) update depreciation to disposal date; (2) remove cost and accumulated depreciation; (3) recognise gain/loss = Cash − NBV.

Sell machine for $8,000 cash. Cost $50,000, Acc Dep $40,000 → NBV $10,000.

DR  Accumulated Depreciation  40,000
DR  Loss on Disposal           2,000

The Worksheet & Adjusted Trial Balance

A worksheet is a multi-column scratch pad that lays out the unadjusted trial balance, the adjustments, and the adjusted trial balance side by side, then sorts each line into the income statement or balance sheet. It is informal — never a financial statement — but it makes month-end painless.

§10.1The classic 10-column worksheet

Cols 1-2Cols 3-4Cols 5-6Cols 7-8Cols 9-10
Unadjusted TBAdjustmentsAdjusted TBIncome StatementBalance Sheet
DR / CRDR / CRDR / CRDR / CRDR / CR

§10.2How to use the worksheet

  1. Enter unadjusted trial balance balances.
  2. Enter all period-end adjustments. Each adjustment is keyed (a), (b), … so you can audit.
  3. Cross-foot to compute the adjusted trial balance: unadjusted ± adjustments.
  4. Carry each line to the income statement (revenues + expenses) or balance sheet (assets, liabilities, equity, dividends).
  5. The IS column difference is net income/loss. Carry that single number into the BS column on the side that makes it balance — credit side for net income, debit side for net loss.

§10.3Statements built from the adjusted trial balance

  1. Income Statement — revenues − expenses = net income. From IS columns.
  2. Statement of Retained Earnings — Beg RE + NI − Dividends = End RE.
  3. Balance Sheet — Assets = Liabilities + (Common Stock + End RE).
  4. Statement of Cash Flows — built separately (Unit 15).

§10.4Common adjustments listed on the worksheet

  • Wage adjustment: DR Wage Exp + CR Wages Payable.
  • Depreciation: DR Depreciation Exp + CR Acc Dep.
  • Insurance, supplies, rent expirations: DR Expense + CR Prepaid.
  • Unearned revenue earned: DR Unearned Liability + CR Revenue.
  • Interest accruals.
  • Tax accrual: often last because it depends on adjusted pre-tax income.

§10.5Net income / loss on the worksheet

If Σ revenues > Σ expenses, the difference is net income. Add it to the IS debit column (to balance) and to the BS credit column (because it increases retained earnings). For a net loss, reverse — add to IS credit and BS debit.

§10.6Worksheet ≠ ledger

The worksheet helps you plan; the journal/ledger record the actual entries. Adjustments worked out on the worksheet must still be journalized and posted to the ledger.

Closing the Books

Income-statement accounts (revenues, expenses) and the dividends account are temporary — they collect a single period's activity and reset to zero before the next period begins. Closing entries do that resetting and roll the period's net income into retained earnings.

§11.1Permanent vs. temporary accounts

TypeExamplesAt year end
Permanent (real)Cash, A/R, Inventory, Equipment, A/P, N/P, Common Stock, Retained EarningsCarry forward
Temporary (nominal)Sales, Service Revenue, COGS, Wage Expense, Rent, Depreciation, DividendsClosed to zero

§11.2Income Summary — a transit account

Most textbooks use a temporary holding account called Income Summary (or Profit & Loss) that receives all revenues and expenses, then transfers the net result to Retained Earnings. Some companies skip Income Summary and close directly to RE — both are correct.

§11.3The four closing entries

  1. Close revenues — DR each revenue · CR Income Summary.
  2. Close expenses — CR each expense · DR Income Summary.
  3. Close Income Summary to Retained Earnings — DR Income Summary · CR Retained Earnings (for net income; reverse for net loss).
  4. Close Dividends to Retained Earnings — DR Retained Earnings · CR Dividends.

§11.4Worked example

Revenues: Sales $9,000 + Service $1,250 = $10,250. Expenses: COGS $4,000, Rent $3,000, Wage $1,800, Insurance $200, Depr $333 = $9,333. Dividends $1,500.

§11.5Why we close

  • Income statement reflects ONE period only — beginning balance must be zero.
  • Income flows to retained earnings — RE is the link between income statement and balance sheet.
  • Dividends are NOT an expense — they reduce RE directly, not net income.
  • Closing produces the post-closing trial balance (Unit 12).

§11.6Common closing pitfalls

  • Forgetting to close Dividends — Dividends balance carries into the next year.
  • Closing Dividends to Income Summary — wrong. Dividends close directly to Retained Earnings.
  • Closing Accumulated Depreciation — never. It's a permanent contra-asset.
  • Closing Common Stock or Retained Earnings — never.

§12.2The full accounting cycle — eight steps

  1. Analyze transactions from source documents.
  2. Journalize transactions in the general journal.
  3. Post journal entries to the general ledger.
  4. Prepare an unadjusted trial balance.
  5. Make adjusting entries (accruals, deferrals, depreciation, estimates).
  6. Prepare an adjusted trial balance.
  7. Prepare financial statements.
  8. Close temporary accounts and prepare the post-closing trial balance.

Optional step 9: reversing entries (Unit 13) to simplify the next period's bookkeeping.

§12.3Why the post-closing TB matters

  • Audit trail — proves the closing entries didn't break the equation.
  • Opening balances — gives the starting balances for the new period's permanent accounts.
  • Internal control — a final mathematical check before next period begins.

§12.4What if the post-closing TB doesn't balance?

Same procedure as the unadjusted TB (Unit 6): re-add columns; check divisibility by 9 (transposition) and 2 (wrong-side); trace back through closing journal entries. The most common mistake is forgetting one of the four closing entries (especially Dividends).

§12.5Reverting permanent vs. temporary balances

After closing, every permanent account has a non-zero (or zero) balance that becomes its opening balance for next period. Every temporary account has a balance of $0 ready to capture next period's activity.

§12.6The new period

On Jan 1, the period reopens. New transactions are journalized and posted. The cycle repeats — same eight steps, same discipline.

Unit 13 · A.2 · Bookkeeping Cycle

Reversing Entries — An Optional Step

Reversing entries are optional bookkeeping shortcuts made on the first day of the new period that "undo" certain accruals from the prior period. They do not change net income — they just simplify the next period's recording so the bookkeeper does not have to remember to split a future cash payment between accrual and current expense.

§13.1The shortcut

Recall: at year end, you accrued $4,500 of wages with the entry DR Wage Expense 4,500 / CR Wages Payable 4,500. On Jan 7, the company pays $9,000 in total wages — $4,500 of which was already expensed last period.

Without a reversing entry, the Jan 7 cash payment requires a careful split:

DR  Wages Payable        4,500    (clearing the accrual)
DR  Wage Expense         4,500    (current period's portion)
    CR  Cash                              9,000

With a reversing entry on Jan 1, the bookkeeper just records the cash payment normally:

Jan 1 — Reversing entry:
DR  Wages Payable         4,500
    CR  Wage Expense                    4,500   (creates a temporary credit balance in expense)

Jan 7 — Normal cash entry:
DR  Wage Expense          9,000
    CR  Cash                              9,000

Net Wage Expense after both entries = $9,000 − $4,500 = $4,500 ✓ (matches the period's true wages)

§13.2Which adjustments can be reversed

Adjustment typeReverse?Why / why not
Accrued expense (wages, interest)YesFuture cash payment will be entered with full DR Expense, simplifying recording
Accrued revenue (unbilled service)YesFuture cash receipt will be DR Cash · CR Revenue at full amount
Deferred items recorded as asset/liability (Prepaid, Unearned)NoThe prepaid asset / unearned liability balance is still meaningful and will be amortised normally next period
DepreciationNoAcc Dep is permanent; reversing would invalidate the carrying amount
Bad-debt allowanceNoAllowance is a permanent contra-asset

§13.3Worked example — accrued interest revenue

Dec 31 accrual: DR Interest Receivable 1,000 / CR Interest Revenue 1,000.

Jan 1 — Reversing:
DR  Interest Revenue       1,000
    CR  Interest Receivable             1,000

Apr 30 — Cash collection ($3,000 total interest, 4 months of which is current Jan-Apr):
DR  Cash                   3,000
    CR  Interest Revenue                 3,000

Year-2 Interest Revenue = $3,000 − $1,000 = $2,000 ✓
(matches Jan 1–Apr 30 interest only — the prior $1,000 was earned in year 1)

§13.4Reversing is optional — both methods are GAAP-compliant

The choice is purely operational. Companies with high transaction volume often reverse to keep the bookkeeping simple and consistent. Smaller companies may skip reversing and instead train bookkeepers to split future cash receipts/payments.

§13.5Why reversing entries don't change net income

The reversing entry creates a temporary credit (or debit) balance in an expense (or revenue) account on Jan 1. When the cash event lands later in the period, the full cash amount is debited (or credited) to the same account. The net balance equals only the current period's actual incurred amount — exactly what should be on the income statement.

§13.6Common pitfalls

  • Reversing a deferral — wrong. Prepaid and unearned balances are real continuing items.
  • Reversing depreciation — wrong. Acc Dep is permanent.
  • Forgetting to reverse — not wrong, just less convenient. Watch the cash entry's split.

Unit 15 · A.4 · Cash Flows & Controls

The Statement of Cash Flows — Three Activities

Net income tells you whether the company was profitable; the cash-flow statement tells you whether it was solvent. Cash is reconciled in three buckets — operating, investing, financing — and the total reconciles to the change in cash on the balance sheet.

§15.1Why we need the SCF

Accrual net income includes non-cash items (depreciation), recognises revenue without cash receipt (A/R growth), and excludes financing/investing flows that don't run through the income statement. The SCF answers: did operations actually produce cash this period? How much was raised from lenders/owners, and how much was spent on long-term assets?

§15.2The three sections

ActivityWhat it capturesExamples (inflows / outflows)
OperatingCash effects of the income statement+Cash from customers, +Interest received, −Cash to suppliers, −Wages paid, −Tax paid
InvestingBuying / selling long-term assets−Buy PP&E, +Sell PP&E, −Buy investments, +Receive principal on a note receivable
FinancingTransactions with debtholders and shareholders+Issue stock, +Borrow (bonds, notes), −Repay debt, −Pay dividends, −Buy treasury stock

Sum of the three = change in cash. Add to opening cash → ending cash → must tie to balance-sheet cash.

§15.3Direct vs. indirect operating method

  • Direct method — list cash receipts and cash payments by category (cash from customers, cash paid to suppliers, etc.). More intuitive but requires extra data.
  • Indirect method — start with net income and adjust for non-cash items and working-capital changes. Far more common in U.S. practice.

Unit 16 · A.4 · Cash Flows & Controls

Safeguarding Cash & Internal Controls (COSO)

Cash is the most fungible — and therefore most vulnerable — asset on the books. Internal controls are the policies, procedures, and structures management designs to safeguard assets, ensure reliable reporting, comply with laws, and run operations efficiently.

§16.1The four objectives of internal control

  1. Safeguard assets — prevent and detect theft, loss, misuse.
  2. Ensure reliable financial reporting — accuracy and integrity of the books.
  3. Promote operational efficiency — controls also support effective and efficient operations.
  4. Encourage compliance with laws and regulations.

§16.2The COSO framework — five components

ComponentWhat it covers
Control environment"Tone at the top." Integrity, ethics, governance, organisational structure.
Risk assessmentIdentifying and analyzing risks to the company's objectives.
Control activitiesPolicies and procedures: authorisations, reconciliations, segregation of duties.
Information and communicationHow the entity captures and shares information needed to run controls.
Monitoring activitiesOngoing evaluations and separate evaluations of the control system.

Mnemonic: "CRIME" — Control environment, Risk assessment, Information & communication, Monitoring, control activitiEs.

§16.3The five activity-level controls

  • Authorisation — only proper personnel can initiate transactions (purchase orders, payroll, refunds).
  • Segregation of duties — separate three roles: custody (handling assets), recording (booking entries), authorisation (approving). One person doing all three has both means and opportunity.
  • Documentation — every transaction backed by a source document; all documents pre-numbered.
  • Physical controls — locks, safes, vaults, access cards, surveillance, password systems.
  • Independent reconciliation / verification — bank rec, A/R confirmations, inventory counts.

§16.5Bank reconciliation — the workhorse cash control

Reconciles the cash-per-books to cash-per-bank statement at month-end. Adjustments to either side until they agree:

± Bank errors                             ±

Items adjusting the BOOK side (interest, service charges, NSF, errors) require journal entries. Items adjusting the BANK side (deposits in transit, outstanding checks) clear themselves over time.

§16.6Inherent limitations of internal control

  • Cost vs. benefit — control cost should not exceed expected loss.
  • Human error — fatigue, mistakes, misunderstanding.
  • Collusion — segregation of duties is defeated when employees conspire.
  • Management override — the strongest control set can be neutralised at the top.
  • Changes in conditions — controls designed for one risk environment may not catch new ones.
