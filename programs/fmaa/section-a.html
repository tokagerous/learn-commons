Unit 01 · A.1 · Foundations

The Language of Accounting

Before you can record a single transaction, you need vocabulary. Accounting describes what an entity owns, owes, and has earned through a small set of carefully defined terms — and a few large rule-makers (FASB, IASB) decide what counts as proper usage.

§1.1What accounting actually does

Accounting is the process of identifying, recording, and communicating economic events of a business to people who must make decisions about it. It produces information; the information is consumed by managers (internal users) and by investors, lenders, regulators, and tax authorities (external users).

BranchAudienceOutputRules
Financial accountingExternal usersGeneral-purpose statements (BS, IS, SCF, equity)GAAP / IFRS — public, audited
Managerial accountingInternal — managersBudgets, variance reports, cost analysisWhatever helps the decision; no external rules
Tax accountingTax authoritiesTax returnsIRC and state rules — different from GAAP
AuditingExternal usersOpinion on whether statements fairly presentGAAS / PCAOB / ISA

§1.2Foundational assumptions

  • Economic entity — the business is a separate accounting unit from its owners. Personal expenses do not belong in business books.
  • Going concern — assume the entity will operate long enough to use its assets and pay its liabilities. Justifies historical cost over liquidation values.
  • Monetary unit — record only items measurable in money, in a single stable currency.
  • Periodicity — split continuous economic activity into reporting periods (month, quarter, year). Forces estimates because period boundaries cut through ongoing events.
  • Accrual basis — recognise revenues when earned and expenses when incurred, regardless of cash timing.

§1.3Cash basis vs. accrual basis

EventCash basis recognitionAccrual basis recognition
Sell on account, customer pays next monthWhen cash arrivesWhen the sale is made (revenue earned)
Receive utility bill in Dec, pay in JanJanuary (when paid)December (expense incurred)
Customer prepays for serviceRevenue when receivedLiability (unearned revenue) until earned
Buy 12-month insurance Sep 1Expense when paidAsset (prepaid), expense over 12 months
GAAP / IFRS = accrual

The cash basis is permitted only for very small businesses or for personal taxes. All FMAA / CMA / CPA examination content assumes accrual unless stated otherwise.

§1.4Matching, revenue recognition, historical cost, full disclosure

  • Revenue recognition — recognise revenue when the performance obligation is satisfied (control of goods/services transfers to the customer). ASC 606 / IFRS 15.
  • Matching (expense recognition) — recognise expenses in the same period as the revenues they helped produce.
  • Historical cost — generally record assets at the cost paid; some classes (financial instruments, biological assets) move to fair value subsequently.
  • Full disclosure — include in the notes anything material to the reader's decision.

§1.5Who sets the rules

§1.6Qualitative characteristics of useful information

  • Fundamental: relevance (predictive, confirmatory, material) and faithful representation (complete, neutral, free from material error).
  • Enhancing: comparability, verifiability, timeliness, understandability.
  • Cost constraint: information must be worth more than it costs to produce.

Unit 02 · A.2 · Bookkeeping Cycle

Accounting Equation & Double-Entry Foundations

Every transaction touches at least two accounts in equal-and-opposite ways. The fundamental equation — Assets = Liabilities + Equity — must hold after every entry, no exceptions. Master this and the rest of the cycle is mechanical.

§2.1The accounting equation

The equation
Assets = Liabilities + Owners' Equity
Expanded equation
Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings + (Revenues − Expenses) − Dividends

§2.2The five element families

ElementExamplesNormal balanceStatement
AssetsCash, A/R, inventory, PP&EDebitBalance sheet
LiabilitiesA/P, notes payable, unearned revCreditBalance sheet
Equity (capital)Common stock, retained earningsCreditBalance sheet / equity
RevenuesSales, service revenue, interest incomeCreditIncome statement → RE
ExpensesCOGS, rent, salaries, depreciationDebitIncome statement → RE

§2.3The debits-and-credits rules

Mnemonic: DEAD CLER — Debits increase Expenses, Assets, and Dividends; Credits increase Liabilities, Equity, and Revenues. Sum DR = Sum CR for every entry; the equation holds after every transaction.

§2.4Eight basic transaction patterns

TransactionAsset effectLiability effectEquity effect
Owner invests cash+ Cash—+ Common stock
Buy equipment for cash+ Equipment, − Cash——
Buy inventory on account+ Inventory+ A/P—
Sell goods for cash+ Cash, − Inventory—+ Revenue, − COGS expense
Pay employees− Cash—− Salary expense
Receive on account+ Cash, − A/R——
Pay supplier− Cash− A/P—
Declare and pay dividend− Cash—− Dividends (RE ↓)

§2.5Contra accounts

A contra account has the opposite normal balance of its companion: it offsets a balance that would otherwise be over-stated. Common examples:

Contra accountCompanionNet presentation
Accumulated depreciation (CR)Equipment / Buildings (DR)Net book value of PP&E
Allowance for doubtful accounts (CR)Accounts receivable (DR)Net realisable A/R
Sales returns and allowances (DR)Sales (CR)Net sales
Treasury stock (DR)Common stock & APIC (CR)Total contributed capital

Check your understanding

A company purchases supplies on account for $1,200. The transaction:

  • Increases cash and decreases liabilities
  • Increases assets and increases equity
  • Increases assets and increases liabilities
  • Decreases equity only
Why: Supplies (asset) ↑ $1,200; Accounts payable (liability) ↑ $1,200. Equity is unaffected. The equation stays in balance because both sides went up by $1,200.

Which account has a normal CREDIT balance?

  • Salary expense
  • Dividends declared
  • Unearned service revenue
Why: Unearned service revenue is a liability (the company owes services it has been paid for) — liabilities are credit-balance. Equipment, expenses, and dividends are all debit-balance.

  • Total assets decrease; liabilities decrease

"Accumulated depreciation" is a:

  • Expense with a debit normal balance

Why: Accumulated depreciation reduces the carrying amount of the related PP&E asset; it has a credit balance to offset the debit-balance asset. Net = book value reported on the balance sheet.
Unit 03 · A.2 · Bookkeeping Cycle

Source Documents & Analyzing Transactions

Every journal entry begins with a piece of paper (or electronic record) — a sales invoice, a vendor bill, a check stub, a cash-register tape. Source documents establish that the transaction happened and supply the data the entry needs.

§3.1What counts as a transaction

An accounting transaction is an economic event that changes at least one of: assets, liabilities, equity, revenues, or expenses. It must be measurable in money and (usually) involve an external party. Hiring an employee is not a transaction (no obligation yet). Paying that employee at month-end is.

§3.2Common source documents

DocumentTriggered byTypical entry
Sales invoiceCustomer purchase on creditDR A/R · CR Revenue (and DR COGS · CR Inventory if perpetual)
Cash register tapeRetail cash saleDR Cash · CR Revenue
Vendor billPurchase on creditDR Inventory or Expense · CR A/P
Check stub / wireCash disbursementDR A/P or Expense · CR Cash
Bank deposit slipCash receivedDR Cash · CR Revenue or A/R
Time cards / payroll registerEmployee work performedDR Wage Expense · CR Wages Payable / Cash
Promissory noteBorrowing or lendingDR Cash · CR Notes Payable (issuer)

§3.3Three steps to analyze any transaction

  1. Identify the accounts touched and their element family.
  2. Decide which direction each touched account moves — increase or decrease.
  3. Translate to debit/credit using DEAD CLER and confirm Σ DR = Σ CR.
A reliable mental script

"What did we get? What did we give up? What promise was made or paid? Translate to debit/credit." Apply this and you will rarely book in the wrong direction.

§3.4Worked examples

§3.5Internal vs. external transactions

  • External — exchanges with parties outside the entity (customers, vendors, lenders, employees).
  • Internal — economic events within the entity (supplies consumed, equipment depreciation, write-down of obsolete inventory). Captured at period end via adjusting entries (Units 7–9).

§3.6Materiality

Strict accounting recognises every transaction. Materiality permits abbreviated treatment (e.g., expensing a $30 stapler instead of capitalising it). Materiality is judged from the user's perspective: would the omission or misstatement change a reasonable decision?

Check your understanding

Which of the following is NOT a recordable accounting transaction?

  • Borrowing $20,000 from the bank
  • Signing a contract to hire a new accountant starting next month
  • Paying employees their weekly wages
  • Buying $400 of office supplies on credit
Why: Hiring is a future commitment with no current asset/liability change. The bookkeeping system records the transaction when wages are earned or paid, not when the employee is hired.

Check your understanding — Practice 2

A company buys land for $200,000, paying $40,000 cash and signing a 10-year mortgage for the remainder. The transaction:

  • Decreases assets by $40,000 only
  • Decreases assets by $200,000 and increases liabilities by $160,000
  • Increases assets by $160,000 net and increases liabilities by $160,000
  • Has no effect on the equation
Why: Land ↑ $200,000; Cash ↓ $40,000; Mortgage Payable ↑ $160,000. Net asset change = +$200,000 − $40,000 = +$160,000, matched by +$160,000 liabilities.

Check your understanding — Practice 3

A vendor invoice for $2,500 of inventory with payment terms 2/10, n/30 is the source document for:

  • DR Cash · CR Sales
  • DR Sales · CR A/R
  • DR A/R · CR Sales
  • DR Inventory · CR Accounts Payable
Why: A vendor received invoice means the company is buying. Inventory (asset) increases, and the obligation to pay the vendor (A/P) increases. The 2/10, n/30 discount is recorded only if/when payment is made within 10 days.

Check your understanding — Practice 4

Receiving $9,000 cash today for services to be provided over the next 6 months should be initially recorded as:

  • DR Cash $9,000 · CR Unearned Service Revenue $9,000
  • DR Cash $9,000 · CR Service Revenue $9,000
  • DR Service Revenue $9,000 · CR Cash $9,000
  • No entry until services are performed
Why: Receipt of cash for unperformed services creates a liability (the obligation to deliver service). Revenue is recognised over the 6 months as the service is provided.

Check your understanding — Practice 5

Internal vs. external transactions differ because:

  • Internal transactions don't require journal entries
  • External transactions are always cash; internal are always credit
  • External transactions involve a party outside the entity; internal transactions are events within the entity (e.g., supplies used, depreciation)
  • Internal transactions affect only the income statement
Why: Both types require journal entries. The distinction is whether an outside party is involved. Internal entries — supplies expense, depreciation — are typically posted at period end through adjusting entries.

Check your understanding — Practice 6

Owner brings a personal computer worth $2,000 into the business with no intention of being repaid. The correct entry is:

  • DR Cash 2,000 · CR Common Stock 2,000
  • DR Owner's Drawings 2,000 · CR Equipment 2,000
  • No entry — owner's personal items don't enter the books
  • DR Equipment 2,000 · CR Common Stock (or Owner's Capital) 2,000
Why: Once contributed to the business, the laptop is a business asset. The owner has invested non-cash equity. Cash is NOT debited (no cash changed hands).
Unit 04 · A.2 · Bookkeeping Cycle

Recording Journal Entries

The general journal is the chronological "book of original entry." Every transaction lands here first — date, accounts, amounts, brief explanation — before being posted to the ledger.

§4.1Anatomy of a journal entry

  • Date — when the transaction occurred
  • Account titles — debit accounts on the first line(s), credit accounts indented below
  • Amounts — debit column on left, credit column on right
  • Posting reference (PR) — added when posted to the ledger
  • Brief explanation — narrative naming the source document or counterparty
Date     Account                           DR        CR
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Mar 12   Inventory                       2,500
            Accounts Payable                          2,500
         (Purchase of merchandise on account, Inv. #4582)

§4.2Simple vs. compound entries

A simple entry has one debit and one credit. A compound entry has multiple debits and/or credits. Sum DR = Sum CR is still required. Example compound:

DR  Equipment              5,000
    CR  Cash                            1,000
    CR  Notes Payable                   4,000

§4.5Special journals

Real systems break the general journal into special journals for high-volume routine transactions, with the GJ used only for non-routine items:

Special journalRecords
Sales journalAll credit sales (DR A/R · CR Sales)
Purchases journalAll credit purchases of inventory
Cash receipts journalAll cash inflows
Cash disbursements journalAll cash outflows
General journalAdjusting, closing, reversing, correcting, and unusual entries

§4.6Common journal-entry errors

  • Reversed direction — recording a debit as a credit and vice versa.
  • Transposition — writing $4,500 as $5,400. Difference is divisible by 9.
  • Slide — writing $4,500 as $450 or $45,000. Difference is divisible by 9 (and a power of 10).
  • Wrong account — debiting Repairs Expense instead of Equipment when capitalising. Trial balance unaffected; financial statements wrong.

Unit 05 · A.2 · Bookkeeping Cycle

Posting to the Ledger (T-Accounts)

After the journal records each transaction chronologically, the ledger groups them by account. The "T-account" is the accountant's whiteboard — debits on the left, credits on the right, balance computed as the difference.

§5.1Journal vs. ledger

—JournalLedger
Organised byDate (chronologically)Account
PurposeRecord transactionsCompute balances
Order of useFirst (book of original entry)Second (book of final entry)
RevealsWhat happened on March 12The current cash balance

§5.2The T-account format

                       Cash
        ─────────────────────────────────
        Debit (left)       Credit (right)
        ─────────────────────────────────
  3/1   40,000             3,000   3/2 (rent)
  3/8    9,000             4,000   3/12 (A/P)
  3/18   2,500             1,500   3/25 (div)
        ─────────────────────────────────
  Bal   43,000

The balance is the difference between debit and credit totals, written on the side that has the larger total — the normal balance side for that account family.

§5.3Posting steps

  1. Locate the account in the ledger.
  2. Record the date.
  3. Enter the journal page reference (audit trail).
  4. Write the debit (or credit) amount in the appropriate column.
  5. Update the running balance.

§5.4Subsidiary ledgers and control accounts

For high-cardinality accounts (A/R with 1,000 customers, A/P with 200 vendors, inventory with 50,000 SKUs), companies maintain a subsidiary ledger with one account per customer/vendor/SKU. The general ledger keeps a single control account whose balance equals the sum of the subsidiary balances.

Control accountSubsidiary ledgerPurpose
Accounts ReceivableOne account per customerStatement of customer balances; collections
Accounts PayableOne account per vendorAging by vendor; payment scheduling
InventoryOne account per SKUStock counts; reorder triggers
PP&EOne account per assetDepreciation schedule per asset

Unit 06 · A.2 · Bookkeeping Cycle

Preparing the Trial Balance & Locating Errors

A trial balance is a list of every account and its balance, columns of debits and credits side-by-side. The columns must match. If they don't, there is a math or posting error to find before period-end statements can be trusted.

§6.1What a trial balance proves (and doesn't)

  • Proves: total debits equal total credits — the equation has not been visibly violated.
  • Does NOT prove: that all transactions are recorded, that the right accounts were used, or that all accounts have correct balances. Errors that affect both sides equally are invisible.

  • Adjusted trial balance — after adjusting entries (Units 7–9). Used to prepare the financial statements.
  • Post-closing trial balance — after closing entries (Unit 11). Only permanent (BS) accounts remain.

§6.3Errors a trial balance WILL catch

Error typeEffectDetection clue
Math error in totalsColumns don't matchRe-add columns
One side of an entry omittedColumns differ by the missing amountLook for entries with only DR or only CR
Wrong amount on one sideColumns differ by the discrepancyDifference / 2 may match a single posting
One side posted twiceColumns differ by the doubled amountSearch for repeated posting

If the difference is divisible by 9, suspect transposition or slide. If divisible by 2, suspect a posting on the wrong side.

§6.4Errors a trial balance WILL NOT catch

  • Omission of an entire transaction — both sides simply not recorded.
  • Recording the same entry twice — both sides up by the same amount.
  • Wrong account used — e.g., DR Repairs Expense instead of DR Equipment.

§6.5How to find errors

  1. Re-add the columns.
  2. Compute the discrepancy. Test for 9 (transposition/slide) and 2 (wrong-side).
  3. Scan for unusual balances.
  4. Trace back: trial balance → ledger → journal → source document.
  5. Reconcile control account against subsidiary ledger.

§6.6Correcting errors

Make a correcting entry. Two equally common forms: (a) reverse the bad entry then book the right one, or (b) one combined entry that achieves the same net effect. Example: a $500 cash sale was recorded as DR Cash 500 / CR Service Revenue 500 when it should have been DR Cash 500 / CR Sales 500. Correcting entry: DR Service Revenue 500 / CR Sales 500.

Check your understanding

A trial balance shows total debits of $146,200 and total credits of $146,000. The $200 difference is divisible by 2. The MOST likely cause is:

  • A transposition
  • An omitted journal entry
  • Forgotten depreciation
  • A $100 posting that hit the wrong side (e.g., debited instead of credited)
Why: When an amount lands on the wrong side, it appears on one side instead of the other — the difference is double the amount. $200 / 2 = $100; look for a $100 posting that was reversed.

  • Check whether $360 is divisible by 9 (transposition or slide test) or 2 (wrong-side test)
  • Adjust retained earnings by $360
Why: $360 ÷ 9 = $40 ✓ — strongly suggests a transposition (e.g., posted $84 as $48). The 9-test is the first thing to try.

Check your understanding — Practice 4

A correcting entry differs from a regular adjusting entry because:

  • It is always the largest entry of the period
  • It fixes a recording error rather than recognising the passage of time or accruing an expense
  • It cannot involve cash
  • It must be reversed at the start of the next period
Why: Adjusting entries (depreciation, accruals, deferrals) recognise economic events at period end. Correcting entries fix bookkeeping mistakes.
