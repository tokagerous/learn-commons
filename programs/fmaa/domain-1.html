Unit 01 · Domain 1

The Four Financial Statements

Every business produces four primary financial statements. Each answers a different question about the company.

§1.1The four reports

StatementQuestion it answersTime view
Balance SheetWhat does the company own and owe right now?Snapshot at a point in time (e.g., Dec 31)
Income StatementHow much profit did the company earn over the period?Period (e.g., year, quarter)
Statement of Cash FlowsWhere did cash come from and where did it go?Period
Statement of EquityHow did the owners' stake in the company change?Period

Together, the four reports articulate — they tell a connected story:

  • The income statement's net income flows into the equity statement (retained earnings).
  • The cash flow statement's ending cash ties to the balance sheet's cash account.
  • Equity changes in the equity statement land in the balance sheet's equity section.

§1.2The balance sheet — three sections

SectionWhat's there
AssetsWhat the company owns or controls (cash, AR, inventory, equipment, buildings, intangibles)
LiabilitiesWhat the company owes (AP, wages payable, debt)
EquityThe owners' residual claim (common stock, APIC, retained earnings)

§1.3The income statement — three subtotals

Multi-step format, common for exam questions:

  • Sales − COGS = Gross Profit — how much was left after the cost of producing/buying what was sold
  • Gross Profit − Operating Expenses = Operating Income — recurring profit from operations
  • Operating Income ± Other Items − Tax = Net Income — bottom line

Check your understanding

Which financial statement reports the company's financial position at a SPECIFIC POINT IN TIME (a snapshot)?

  • Income statement
  • Statement of cash flows
  • Balance sheet
  • Statement of equity
Why: The balance sheet is dated AS OF a specific date (e.g., "December 31, 2026"). The income statement, SCF, and equity statement cover a PERIOD (e.g., "for the year ended December 31, 2026"). Snapshot vs. period is the most-tested distinction.

Check your understanding — Practice 2

Sales $400K; COGS $250K; Operating expenses $80K; Interest $10K; Tax $20K. What is OPERATING income?

  • $150,000
  • $70,000
  • $60,000
  • $40,000
Why: Gross profit = Sales − COGS = $400K − $250K = $150K. Operating income = Gross profit − Operating expenses = $150K − $80K = $70K. Interest and tax sit BELOW operating income (non-operating). Net income = $70K − $10K − $20K = $40K.

Check your understanding — Practice 3

The four financial statements articulate. Which connection is correct?

  • Sales on the income statement equals cash on the balance sheet
  • Operating expenses on the income statement equals dividends declared
  • Cost of goods sold equals total liabilities
  • Net income on the income statement flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet (via the statement of equity)
Why: The income statement's bottom line (net income) flows to retained earnings: Beginning RE + NI − Dividends = Ending RE. Ending RE then appears on the balance sheet as part of equity. Articulation is the principle that the four statements are interconnected, not independent.
Unit 02 · Domain 1

The Accounting Equation: A = L + E

Every business activity preserves one fundamental identity. Once you internalise it, debits and credits become almost automatic.

§2.1The equation

The fundamental equation
Assets = Liabilities + Equity

This is a balance-sheet identity that ALWAYS holds. Total resources (assets) equal claims on those resources (liabilities + equity). Every transaction either:

  • Changes both sides equally (e.g., borrow cash: assets up, liabilities up by the same amount), OR
  • Reshuffles within one side (e.g., buy inventory with cash: cash down, inventory up — total assets unchanged)

§2.2The expanded equation

Equity itself can be expanded:

Assets = Liabilities + (Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings)
    = Liabilities + (Contributed Capital + Net Income − Dividends)
    = Liabilities + (Contributed Capital + Revenues − Expenses − Dividends)

This shows how revenues (which increase NI, which increases RE, which increases equity) and expenses/dividends (which decrease equity) all flow through one identity.

Check your understanding

A company has total assets of $800K and total liabilities of $300K. Total equity is:

  • $1,100,000
  • $500,000
  • $300,000
  • Cannot be determined without more info
Why: A = L + E → E = A − L = $800K − $300K = $500K. The equation is a definitional identity; no extra information needed.

Check your understanding — Practice 2

A company purchases $50,000 of inventory on credit (account payable). The immediate impact on the accounting equation is:

  • Assets up $50K; Equity up $50K
  • Assets unchanged
  • Assets up $50K (inventory); Liabilities up $50K (AP); Equity unchanged
  • Liabilities up $50K; Equity down $50K
Why: Inventory increases by $50K (asset up); AP increases by $50K (liability up). Equity unchanged because no income or owner-related event happened. Both sides of the equation move by $50K, preserving the identity.

Check your understanding — Practice 3

The board declares a $20,000 cash dividend. The immediate impact on the accounting equation is:

  • Liabilities up $20K (dividends payable); Equity down $20K (RE)
  • Cash down $20K immediately
  • No change until dividend is paid
  • Equity up $20K
Why: Declaration creates an obligation (Dividends Payable, a current liability) and reduces RE (equity). Cash doesn't move until the actual payment date. The DECLARATION date triggers the entry; the payment date just moves cash and clears the payable. Three dividend dates: declaration, record (who gets paid), payment.
Unit 03 · Domain 1

Accrual vs. Cash Basis

When do you recognise revenue — when you do the work, or when the cash hits the bank? The answer depends on which basis your accounting uses, and the answer matters a lot.

§3.1The split

Cash basisAccrual basis
Revenue recognised whenCash is receivedEarned (work performed, goods delivered)
Expense recognised whenCash is paidIncurred (matched to revenue)
Required by GAAP/IFRS?NoYes
Used bySome small businesses, individualsAll real companies (per GAAP/IFRS)

The intuition: accrual matches the economic activity to the period it occurred. Cash basis can mislead — a company that delivers $10M in December but doesn't collect until February would look broke under cash basis but profitable under accrual.

§3.2The matching principle

Accrual's central rule: match expenses to the revenue they helped generate. If a sale generates $1M in December, the corresponding COGS is also recognised in December — even if the supplier wasn't paid until February.

§3.3Conversions you should be able to do

Revenue (accrual) = Cash collected + Δ AR − Δ Unearned Revenue
Expense (accrual) = Cash paid + Δ Accrued Expenses − Δ Prepaid Expenses

Check your understanding

A consulting firm finishes a $50,000 project in December but doesn't get paid until February. Under ACCRUAL basis, when is the $50K revenue recognised?

  • February (when cash is received)
  • December (when the work is completed and earned)
  • Allocated half to each year
  • Whenever the company chooses
Why: Accrual recognises revenue when EARNED — when the performance obligation is satisfied — not when cash arrives. December (work completed) is the right answer. The unpaid amount sits in Accounts Receivable until February when cash arrives.

Check your understanding — Practice 2

A magazine publisher collects $1,200 cash on October 1 for a 12-month subscription. By December 31, what amount of REVENUE has been earned?

  • $300 (3 months: Oct, Nov, Dec)
  • $1,200 (full amount on receipt)
  • $900 (the unearned portion)
  • $0 (until subscription ends)
Why: $1,200 ÷ 12 = $100/month. Revenue earned for Oct, Nov, Dec = $300. Remaining $900 sits as Unearned Revenue (a current liability) until earned in subsequent months. Cash basis would recognise the full $1,200 on October 1 — wrong under GAAP.

Check your understanding — Practice 3

Cash collected from customers $400K. Beginning AR $50K. Ending AR $70K. What is REVENUE under accrual basis?

  • $380K
  • $400K
  • $420K — Cash + Δ AR
  • $450K
Why: Revenue (accrual) = Cash collected + Δ AR = $400K + ($70K − $50K) = $420K. AR went up $20K, meaning $20K of additional sales were made but not yet collected. Recognise them as revenue under accrual.
Unit 04 · Domain 1

Debits, Credits, and the Accounting Cycle

The bookkeeping language. Debit and credit aren't "good" and "bad" — they're directions in a two-sided entry system that keeps the books balanced.

§4.1The DEAD CLIC mnemonic

Every account has a "normal balance" — the side it usually sits on:

DEAD (Debit normal)CLIC (Credit normal)
DividendsCapital (Equity)
ExpensesLiabilities
AssetsIncome (Revenue)

To increase a DEAD account → debit it. To increase a CLIC account → credit it. To decrease either, do the opposite. Every transaction has equal total debits and credits.

§4.2Walkthrough — buying inventory on credit

$50,000 of inventory purchased on credit (account payable):

DR Inventory (asset)     50,000
    CR Accounts Payable (liability)   50,000

Inventory is an asset (DEAD) — increased by debit. AP is a liability (CLIC) — increased by credit. Both sides equal $50,000. Books balance.

§4.3The accounting cycle

  1. Identify transactions — what happened?
  2. Journal entries — record in the journal (debits and credits)
  3. Post to ledger — accumulate amounts in each account
  4. Trial balance — sanity check that DR = CR
  5. Adjusting entries — accruals, deferrals, depreciation, allowances
  6. Adjusted trial balance
  7. Financial statements
  8. Closing entries — zero out temporary accounts (revenues, expenses, dividends) into RE
  9. Post-closing trial balance — only permanent accounts remain

Check your understanding

A company pays $24,000 cash for two years of insurance coverage. The journal entry on the date of payment is:

  • DR Prepaid Insurance $24,000 / CR Cash $24,000
  • DR Insurance Expense $24,000 / CR Cash $24,000
  • DR Cash $24,000 / CR Prepaid Insurance $24,000
  • DR Insurance Expense $12,000 / DR Prepaid Insurance $12,000 / CR Cash $24,000
Why: The coverage hasn't been used yet — it's a future benefit, an asset called Prepaid Insurance. Each month after, an adjusting entry (DR Insurance Expense / CR Prepaid Insurance) gradually moves the asset to expense.

Check your understanding — Practice 2

Which of the following accounts has a CREDIT normal balance?

  • Inventory
  • Salaries Expense
  • Common Stock
  • Cash
Why: Common Stock is equity (CLIC: Capital) → credit normal balance. Inventory and Cash are assets (DEAD: Assets) → debit. Salaries Expense is an expense (DEAD: Expenses) → debit.

Check your understanding — Practice 3

Closing entries in the accounting cycle are made to:

  • Adjust accruals at year-end
  • Discover errors in the trial balance
  • Zero out temporary accounts (revenues, expenses, dividends) into Retained Earnings
  • Roll forward inventory to next year
Why: Closing entries reset temporary accounts to zero so the next period starts fresh. Revenues and gains close into RE (credit RE). Expenses and losses close out of RE (debit RE). Dividends close out of RE. Permanent accounts (assets, liabilities, equity components other than the temporary ones) carry forward.
