Unit 01 · C.1 · Variance Analysis

Cost & Variance Fundamentals

Variance analysis decomposes the difference between standard and actual into pieces with operating meaning — price, efficiency, volume — so the right manager investigates the right thing.

§1.1The standard-cost system

Under standard costing, every unit of output carries a budgeted (standard) cost based on the standard quantity of inputs at the standard price. Actual cost differs from standard for two reasons:

  • Price/rate variance — paid more (or less) per input unit than standard
  • Quantity/efficiency variance — used more (or less) input than standard for output produced

§1.2The flexible-budget anchor

Always recast at actual output

Variance analysis compares actual costs to the flexible budget at actual output, not to the static budget. The flexible budget allows what input should have been at the volume actually produced.

§1.3The three-way decomposition

LayerComparesWhat it tells you
Static-budget varianceActual results vs. static budgetTotal gap from plan; mixes everything
Sales-volume varianceStatic budget vs. flexible budget at actual volumeEffect of selling more or fewer units than planned
Flexible-budget varianceFlexible budget vs. actual resultsPure price + efficiency effects (manager-controllable)

§1.4F / U sign convention

  • For costs: actual < standard = Favourable; actual > standard = Unfavourable
  • For revenues: actual > budget = F; actual < budget = U
  • For income: actual > budget = F

§1.5Determining the level of activity for standards

Capacity choiceDefinitionEffect on standard fixed OH rate
Theoretical capacityMaximum output, zero downtimeLowest rate (highest denominator) — under-applies in practice
Practical capacityTheoretical minus normal downtime, breaks, maintenanceUsed for product costing in many firms
Normal capacityAverage over a multi-year cycleSmoothed; some firms use for inventory valuation
Expected (master-budget) capacityForecast for the upcoming yearMost common for budgeting; rate fluctuates with forecast

§1.6Why investigate variances

  • Materiality — investigate if >X% or >$Y of standard
  • Persistence — same-direction variances over multiple periods signal a systemic issue (or a wrong standard)
  • Controllability — focus on variances the manager can act on; ignore allocations and pure noise
  • Cost vs. benefit — investigation has a cost; only run it when expected savings exceed it

Check your understanding

A manager's monthly variance report should compare actual cost to:

  • The static-budget cost set at year-start
  • Last year's actual for the same month
  • The flexible budget recast at actual output volume
  • The most recent rolling forecast
Why: The flexible budget at actual volume isolates price/efficiency variances from volume variance. Static budget mixes everything; last year's actual ignores standard updates.

Unit 02 · C.1 · Variance Analysis

Direct Material Variances

Two pieces: did we pay the right price, and did we use the right quantity? Both are computed at actual output level.

§2.1The two DM variances — at a glance

Direct material price variance
DM Price Variance = (AP − SP) × AQ purchased or used

where AP = actual price paid per unit of material, SP = standard price per unit, AQ = actual quantity (purchased OR used — see §2.3).

Direct material quantity (usage) variance
DM Quantity Variance = (AQ used − SQ) × SP

where SQ = standard quantity allowed for actual output = (units produced × DM std per unit).

§2.2The diagram — three columns

§2.3Price variance — purchased vs. used

Two timing conventions exist:

  • Computed at purchase (most common, more useful) — AQ = quantity purchased. Captures the price variance at the earliest possible point so purchasing can act. Inventory carried at standard.
  • Computed at use — AQ = quantity used in production. Defers recognition until materials are issued. Less timely.

§2.4Worked example

Standard: 3 lbs/unit at $4/lb. Production: 1,000 units (so SQ = 3,000 lbs). Actual: bought and used 3,200 lbs at $4.20/lb.

  • Actual cost = 3,200 × $4.20 = $13,440
  • SP × AQ = $4 × 3,200 = $12,800
  • SP × SQ (flexible-budget standard) = $4 × 3,000 = $12,000
  • DM Price Variance = ($4.20 − $4.00) × 3,200 = $0.20 × 3,200 = $640 U (paid more)
  • DM Quantity Variance = (3,200 − 3,000) × $4.00 = 200 × $4 = $800 U (used more)
  • Total flexible-budget variance = $640 U + $800 U = $1,440 U ✓ (matches $13,440 − $12,000)

§2.5Common causes

VarianceCommon causes (F or U)Investigate with
Price (U)Inflation, rush orders, bulk-discount missed, supply shortage, supplier changePurchasing manager
Price (F)Bulk discount achieved, lower-grade material substituted (may worsen quantity variance), favourable contractPurchasing manager — verify quality not compromised
Quantity (U)Wasteful operations, faulty equipment, untrained workers, lower-grade material (links to F price)Production supervisor
Quantity (F)Improved process, higher-grade material (may worsen price), more skilled labourProduction supervisor
Watch the linkage

Price and quantity variances often interact. Buying cheaper material (F price) may use more of it (U quantity); buying premium material (U price) may produce less waste (F quantity). The same purchase decision drives both. Don't praise purchasing without checking production.

Unit 03 · C.1 · Variance Analysis

Direct Labour Variances

Same two-piece logic as direct materials — rate (price) and efficiency (quantity) — applied to labour hours and wage rates.

§3.1The formulas

DL Rate Variance = (AR − SR) × AH
DL Efficiency Variance = (AH − SH) × SR

AR = actual rate, SR = standard rate, AH = actual hours worked, SH = standard hours allowed = (units × std hours per unit).

§3.2Worked example

Standard: 0.5 hours/unit at $20/hour. Production: 2,000 units (SH = 1,000 hours). Actual: worked 1,050 hours at $19.50/hour.

  • Actual cost = 1,050 × $19.50 = $20,475
  • SR × AH = $20 × 1,050 = $21,000
  • SR × SH = $20 × 1,000 = $20,000 (flexible-budget standard)
  • DL Rate Variance = ($19.50 − $20) × 1,050 = −$525 → $525 F
  • DL Efficiency Variance = (1,050 − 1,000) × $20 = $1,000 U
  • Total = $525 F + $1,000 U = $475 U ✓

§3.3Common causes

VarianceCommon causesInvestigate with
Rate (U)Higher-grade workers used; overtime premium; wage increase; mix of skill grades shifted upwardHR and production scheduling
Rate (F)Lower-grade workers (may worsen efficiency); union concessionHR — verify quality not compromised
Efficiency (U)Untrained workers, faulty equipment, low-quality inputs, poor scheduling, learning-curve slowdownProduction supervisor
Efficiency (F)Skilled workers, learning gains, process improvements, high-quality inputsProduction supervisor
The interaction trap (again)

F rate variance + U efficiency variance often = lower-skill workers used. The cost saving on wages is offset (or worse) by extra hours. Net cost impact is the only honest verdict.

§3.4Idle time and overtime

  • Idle time — paid time when workers can't produce (machine breakdown, raw material shortage). Often segregated and shown as a separate variance, sometimes called "idle-time variance" = idle hours × SR.
  • Overtime premium — the premium portion of overtime is typically charged to overhead (general production cost), not to direct labour, when overtime is caused by general schedule pressure rather than specific job urgency.

  • $2,500 U
  • $2,100 F
  • $2,100 U
Why: Rate var = (AR − SR) × AH = ($24 − $25) × 2,100 = −$2,100 → $2,100 F. Note: AH (2,100), not SH (2,000), is used in the rate calc.

Check your understanding — Practice 3

A factory with persistent F labour-rate variance and persistent U labour-efficiency variance is MOST likely:

  • Performing well — wages are below standard
  • Substituting lower-skill (cheaper) workers who are slower; net cost may not be favourable
  • Buying lower-grade materials
  • Experiencing supply chain disruption
Why: The rate-and-efficiency interaction. Cheap labour is cheap because it's less skilled. The hours-saved-by-skill that you forfeit show up as U efficiency. Net cost — sum of variances — is the only fair test of whether the substitution helped.
Unit 04 · C.1 · Variance Analysis

Mix & Yield Variances

When multiple inputs combine to produce one output (a chemical formula, a chef's recipe, a labour mix of grades), the quantity variance further decomposes into mix (proportions) and yield (total efficiency).

§4.1The decomposition

For a multi-input setup, the total quantity variance breaks into two pieces:

Mix Variance = Σ (Actual mix − Standard mix) × Total actual quantity × SPi
    = Σ (AQi − Standard-mix·AQtotal) × SPi

Yield Variance = (Total actual quantity − Total standard quantity for output) × Average standard cost
    = (AQtotal − SQtotal) × Standard weighted-avg price

Mix + Yield = Total quantity variance.

§4.2Worked example — two materials

A chemical product needs two ingredients: A (standard 60% of mix at $10/lb) and B (standard 40% at $5/lb). Standard input = 5 lbs per unit of output. Output produced = 1,000 units (so SQtotal = 5,000 lbs). Actual: 3,200 lbs A + 1,900 lbs B = 5,100 lbs total.

Material AMaterial BTotal
Standard mix %60%40%100%
Standard price / lb$10$5
Standard input at actual total Q (5,100 × mix %)3,060 lbs2,040 lbs5,100 lbs
Actual quantity used3,200 lbs1,900 lbs5,100 lbs
Standard quantity for output (5,000 × mix %)3,000 lbs2,000 lbs5,000 lbs

Computations:

  • Mix variance = (3,200 − 3,060) × $10 + (1,900 − 2,040) × $5 = $1,400 U + $700 F = $700 U
  • Average standard price = (60% × $10) + (40% × $5) = $6 + $2 = $8/lb
  • Yield variance = (5,100 − 5,000) × $8 = $800 U
  • Total quantity variance = $700 U + $800 U = $1,500 U
  • Cross-check by direct DM quantity calc: each material's quantity var = (AQ − SQ) × SP_i = (3,200 − 3,000)×$10 + (1,900 − 2,000)×$5 = $2,000 U + $500 F = $1,500 U ✓

§4.3What the variances tell management

  • Mix variance = effect of using a different proportion of inputs than standard. Often controllable (substitute cheaper for more expensive within a recipe).
  • Yield variance = effect of total quantity being more or less than the standard for output. Reflects process efficiency — waste, spoilage, or overuse beyond proportional substitution.

§4.4Same logic for labour-mix variances

Where multiple grades of labour (skilled, semi-skilled, unskilled) work on a job, the labour quantity variance decomposes the same way: mix variance (used a different grade-mix) + yield variance (used more total hours than standard).

Unit 05 · C.1 · Variance Analysis

Overhead Variances

Overhead is split into variable and fixed for variance analysis. Each splits further: variable into spending + efficiency, fixed into budget (spending) + volume.

§5.1The four-variance overhead model

VarianceFormulaWhat it captures
VOH spending(Actual VOH rate − Std VOH rate) × Actual hours = Actual VOH − (Std VOH rate × AH)Did we pay the right rate for variable overhead?
VOH efficiency(AH − SH) × Std VOH rateDid we use the right hours? (Same hour-base as labour)
FOH budget (spending)Actual FOH − Budgeted FOHDid we over/underspend our fixed budget?
FOH volumeBudgeted FOH − Applied FOH = Budgeted FOH − (Std FOH rate × SH)Did we produce more or less than the denominator activity used to set the rate?

§5.2The fixed OH application rate

Std FOH rate = Budgeted FOH ÷ Denominator activity (e.g., budgeted DL hours, machine hours)

The denominator activity choice (theoretical / practical / normal / expected — see Unit 1 §1.5) directly affects the rate. A higher denominator → lower rate.

§5.3Worked example — variable overhead

Standard variable OH = $4 per DL hour. Standard hours allowed for output (SH) = 1,000. Actual DL hours (AH) = 1,050. Actual variable OH = $4,500.

  • Actual VOH rate = $4,500 ÷ 1,050 = $4.286/hr
  • SR × AH = $4 × 1,050 = $4,200
  • SR × SH = $4 × 1,000 = $4,000 (applied)
  • VOH spending = $4,500 − $4,200 = $300 U (paid more per hour than standard)
  • VOH efficiency = (1,050 − 1,000) × $4 = $200 U (used more hours)
  • Total VOH variance = $300 U + $200 U = $500 U ✓

§5.4Worked example — fixed overhead

Budgeted FOH = $50,000. Denominator activity (budgeted hours) = 10,000. Std FOH rate = $5/hr. SH = 9,500. Actual FOH = $52,000.

  • Applied FOH = $5 × 9,500 = $47,500
  • FOH budget = $52,000 − $50,000 = $2,000 U (overspent fixed budget)
  • FOH volume = $50,000 − $47,500 = $2,500 U (produced fewer hours' worth than denominator)
  • Total FOH variance (= over-applied vs. actual) = $52,000 − $47,500 = $4,500 U

§5.5The volume variance — why it's special

Volume variance is not a "manager" variance

FOH volume variance arises because total budgeted fixed OH is spread over the denominator activity to set a per-hour rate. Producing more or less than the denominator means under- or over-applying. It's a costing artifact, not a measure of operating efficiency. Don't punish the manager for a U volume variance unless the demand shortfall was within their control.

§5.6Two-way and three-way overhead variance models

ModelDecomposition
Two-wayControllable (= VOH total + FOH budget) + Volume
Three-waySpending (= VOH spending + FOH budget) + Efficiency (VOH efficiency only — FOH has no efficiency component) + Volume
Four-wayVOH spending + VOH efficiency + FOH budget + FOH volume (most informative)

Unit 06 · C.1 · Variance Analysis

Sales Variances

Cost variances measure how well we ran the factory. Sales variances measure how well we sold — at what price, what volume, and (for multi-product firms) what mix.

§6.1The single-product split

For a single-product firm, the static-budget variance on income decomposes into:

Sales Price Variance = (Actual price − Budgeted price) × Actual units sold
Sales Volume Variance = (Actual units − Budgeted units) × Budgeted contribution margin per unit

§6.2Worked example — single product

Budget: 10,000 units × $50 price × $30 budgeted CM/unit. Actual: 11,000 units × $48 price.

  • Sales Price Variance = ($48 − $50) × 11,000 = −$22,000 → $22,000 U
  • Sales Volume Variance (in CM terms) = (11,000 − 10,000) × $30 = $30,000 F
  • Net effect on contribution = $30,000 F − $22,000 U = $8,000 F
Why CM, not price, for the volume variance

When you sell one extra unit, you don't gain the full price — you gain the contribution margin (price − variable cost), because variable costs scale with volume. Always use CM (or budgeted gross profit) per unit for the volume calculation.

§6.3Multi-product: mix and quantity

For multi-product firms, the volume variance decomposes further:

Sales Mix Variance = Σ (Actual unitsi − Std-mix at total actuali) × Std CM/uniti
    or equivalently = Total actual units × (Avg actual CM − Avg std CM)

Sales Quantity Variance = (Total actual units − Total budgeted units) × Avg std CM

Mix + Quantity = total volume variance.

§6.4Worked multi-product example

Two products. Budget: A = 6,000 units at $20 CM, B = 4,000 units at $10 CM. Actual: A = 7,500 units, B = 2,500 units (total 10,000 — same as budgeted total).

Product AProduct BTotal
Budgeted units6,0004,00010,000
Std mix %60%40%100%
Std-mix at actual total (10,000)6,0004,00010,000
Actual units7,5002,50010,000

  • Mix variance = (7,500 − 6,000) × $20 + (2,500 − 4,000) × $10 = $30,000 F + $15,000 U = $15,000 F
  • Average std CM = (60% × $20) + (40% × $10) = $12 + $4 = $16
  • Quantity variance = (10,000 − 10,000) × $16 = $0 (total volume met budget)
  • Total volume variance = $15,000 F + $0 = $15,000 F

Reading: the firm sold the same total units, but a more-profitable mix (more A, less B). Pure mix improvement.

§6.5Sales variance for a service company

Same logic but in service units (billable hours, contracts, customer count). For a consulting firm: budgeted billable hours × budgeted rate, with similar price (rate) variance and volume (hours) variance decomposition. The "contribution" concept may use revenue minus directly variable cost (e.g., contractor pay).

Unit 07 · C.1 · Variance Analysis

Market Variances

When sales volume differs from budget, was it because the whole market grew/shrank (market size), or because we won/lost share within it (market share)? The decomposition holds management accountable for what they actually controlled.

§7.1Decomposing the sales QUANTITY variance

The sales quantity variance further splits into two:

Market Size Variance = (Actual market size − Budgeted market size) × Budgeted market share % × Avg budgeted CM/unit
(Effect of overall market growing or shrinking, holding our share constant)

Market Share Variance = (Actual share % − Budgeted share %) × Actual market size × Avg budgeted CM/unit
(Effect of us winning or losing share, given the market size that occurred)

Market size + Market share = Sales quantity variance.

§7.2Why this decomposition matters

  • If overall demand collapsed (recession), unfavourable volume variance is partly a market-size effect — beyond management's control.
  • If demand was strong but we lost share to a competitor, that's an unfavourable market-share variance — squarely a management issue.
  • The same total U volume variance can mask completely different stories. Decomposing tells you which one.

§7.3Worked example

Budget: total industry market = 500,000 units; firm's budgeted share = 8% (so 40,000 units), at $20 budgeted CM/unit. Actual: industry contracted to 450,000 units; firm's actual share = 9% (so 40,500 units).

  • Sales quantity variance = (40,500 − 40,000) × $20 = $10,000 F overall
  • Market size variance = (450,000 − 500,000) × 8% × $20 = (−50,000 × 0.08) × $20 = −4,000 × $20 = $80,000 U (market shrank)
  • Market share variance = (9% − 8%) × 450,000 × $20 = (0.01 × 450,000) × $20 = 4,500 × $20 = $90,000 F (we won share)
  • Net = $90,000 F − $80,000 U = $10,000 F ✓

The headline "$10K F volume variance" hides the real story: management gained share aggressively in a contracting market — much more impressive than the modest net suggests.

§8.3Common-cost allocation methods

Corporate-level costs (CEO salary, central HR, IT, audit) must be split among operating units. Three approaches:

  • Stand-alone — allocate based on each unit's standalone cost or benefit. Simple but ignores synergies.
  • Incremental — first unit gets allocated its standalone cost; remaining units share the residual incremental savings. Order matters; can feel arbitrary.
  • Activity-based — allocate by causal driver (e.g., HR cost by headcount, IT by tickets). Most defensible; data-intensive.

If a manager has no influence over a cost, evaluating them on it is at best motivationally inert and at worst toxic:

  • Plant manager rated on allocated head-office IT spend → can't change it; ignores it
  • Sales manager rated on currency translation losses → builds defensive narratives instead of selling
  • R&D head rated on production-line variance → no rational response

Strict controllability is rarely 100% achievable, but the closer the evaluation tracks what the manager controls, the more rational the management response.

Transfer Pricing

When one division of a firm sells to another, the price they use — the transfer price — is internal but matters enormously: it shifts profit between divisions, drives make-or-buy decisions, and (across borders) is regulated by tax authorities.

§10.1The four common transfer-pricing methods

MethodHow setStrengthWeakness
Market priceExternal market price for the same/similar goodReflects opportunity cost; most goal-congruent if market existsRequires a true external market; may not exist for specialised internal products
Cost-basedVariable cost, full cost, or cost-plus-markupSimple; useful when no market existsInflates buyer's costs (full cost); doesn't motivate selling division to be efficient (cost-plus passes inefficiencies on)
NegotiatedTwo divisions negotiateReflects local information; both parties commitTime-consuming; favours the better negotiator
DualSelling division credited at one price (e.g., market); buying division charged at another (e.g., variable cost). HQ absorbs the difference.Goal-congruent for both divisions individuallyComplex; total firm profit overstated unless eliminated in consolidation

§10.2The general rule for goal congruence

Minimum transfer price (selling division's perspective)
Min transfer price = Variable cost per unit + Opportunity cost per unit

where opportunity cost = lost contribution margin from forgoing the next-best external sale (typically the external market CM if the selling division has limited capacity).

  • If selling division has idle capacity: opportunity cost = $0 → Min transfer price = Variable cost.
  • If selling division is at capacity: opportunity cost = market price − variable cost → Min transfer price = Market price.

§10.3The buying division's view

The buying division will internally source if the transfer price is at or below the external market price for the same input. So the negotiation range, when it exists, is:

[Min seller price] ≤ Transfer price ≤ [External market price]

If this range is empty (min seller price > external market price), the firm should buy externally rather than transfer internally.

§10.4Worked example — idle capacity

Component division: variable cost per unit $40, market price $60, currently has 5,000 units of idle capacity. Assembly division wants to source 3,000 units internally. External market would charge Assembly $58.

  • Component's min transfer price = $40 (idle capacity → no opportunity cost)
  • Assembly's max transfer price = $58 (would otherwise buy externally)
  • Negotiation range = $40 to $58. Any price in this range is goal-congruent — both divisions and the firm benefit.
  • Outside this range: negotiation breaks down, but the FIRM still benefits at any price ≤ $58 (because variable cost $40 < $58 external).

§10.5Worked example — at capacity

Same Component division but at full capacity (no idle hours). Each internal sale displaces an external sale at $60 market price.

  • Component's min transfer price = $40 + ($60 − $40) = $60 (must recover the lost external CM).
  • If Assembly's external price is $58, the negotiation range is empty — Component has no incentive to transfer below $60, and Assembly has no incentive to pay above $58. Buy externally.
  • This is the right decision for the firm: forcing the transfer would cost more than buying outside (firm-wide cost = forgone CM $60 + variable $40 produced = $100, vs. external purchase at $58 + opportunity to sell at $60 = $98 net).

§10.6International transfer pricing — tax minimisation

Tax authorities watch closely

Cross-border transfers shift profit between tax jurisdictions. Setting the transfer price high in low-tax jurisdictions and low in high-tax jurisdictions can move profit toward favourable taxation. Tax authorities require arm's-length pricing — what unrelated parties would charge — and audit aggressively. OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines provide the global framework; the U.S. uses IRC §482.

§10.7Behavioural and goal-congruence concerns

  • Transfer prices that don't reflect economic reality push managers toward decisions that hurt firm-wide profit (e.g., sourcing externally when internal would be cheaper, or vice versa).
  • Compensation tied tightly to divisional profit + arbitrary transfer prices = political battles, suboptimal sourcing, gaming.
  • Dual pricing solves divisional incentives but creates consolidation reconciliation work.

Unit 11 · C.3 · Performance Measures

ROI, RI, and EVA

Investment-center managers control both profit and capital. The right measure is one that captures both. Three classic choices, each with different incentives.

§11.1Return on Investment (ROI) — the workhorse

ROI = Operating Income ÷ Average Operating Assets
   = (Margin × Turnover) — DuPont decomposition
   = (Operating Income / Sales) × (Sales / Avg Operating Assets)

The DuPont decomposition shows two ways to raise ROI:

  • Improve margin (more profit per dollar of sales) — pricing, cost reduction, mix shift.
  • Improve turnover (more sales per dollar of assets) — reduce inventory, AR, redundant capacity.

Worked example

Operating income $400K. Average operating assets $2,000K. Sales $4,000K.

  • Margin = $400K ÷ $4,000K = 10%
  • Turnover = $4,000K ÷ $2,000K = 2.0×
  • ROI = 10% × 2.0 = 20%
ROI's dysfunctional incentive

A division with current ROI of 25% will reject a project earning 18% — even though the firm's cost of capital might be 12% and the project would create value. ROI penalises good projects below the divisional average. RI fixes this.

§11.2Residual Income (RI)

RI = Operating Income − (Required Rate × Average Operating Assets)

Required rate is typically the firm's cost of capital (or a hurdle rate). RI measures profit above the cost of capital — a dollar amount.

Worked example

Same numbers + required rate of 12%:

  • Required return = 12% × $2,000K = $240K
  • RI = $400K − $240K = $160K

Now consider a $500K incremental investment generating $90K incremental profit (18% return). Cost-of-capital charge = 12% × $500K = $60K. Incremental RI = $90K − $60K = $30K positive — a value-creating investment that ROI would have rejected.

§11.3Economic Value Added (EVA)

EVA = NOPAT − (WACC × Invested Capital)

Where:
NOPAT = Net Operating Profit After Tax = Operating Income × (1 − Tax rate)
WACC = Weighted Average Cost of Capital
Invested Capital = LT debt + Equity (or Total assets − Non-interest-bearing current liabilities)

EVA is RI's after-tax cousin with stricter, more explicit definitions of capital and cost of capital. Stern Stewart's "EVA" framework added >160 GAAP adjustments to operating income (recapitalising R&D, leases, etc.) — most firms use a simplified version in practice.

§11.5Definition issues — what counts as "operating income" and "operating assets"

Unit 12 · C.3 · Performance Measures

Balanced Scorecard, Customer & Product Profitability

Financial measures alone are backward-looking — they tell you what already happened. The Balanced Scorecard pairs financial outcomes with leading indicators across three other perspectives, giving an early-warning system tied to strategy.

§12.1The four perspectives (Kaplan & Norton)

PerspectiveQuestion answeredExample measures
FinancialHow do we look to shareholders?Revenue growth, ROI, RI, EVA, gross margin, FCF
CustomerHow do customers see us?NPS, customer retention, on-time delivery, defect rate, market share
Internal processWhat must we excel at?Cycle time, yield, time-to-market, R&D pipeline progression
Learning & growthCan we keep improving?Employee satisfaction, training hours, employee retention, IT system uptime
Lagging vs. leading indicators

Financial measures are lagging — they reveal results after the fact. Learning & growth and internal-process measures are leading — they predict future financial outcomes. The scorecard's value is in connecting them: improvements in employee training (L&G) → better internal processes (P) → happier customers (C) → financial results (F).

§12.2Strategy maps — the visual link

Cause-and-effect relationships across the four perspectives form a strategy map. For example, a "low-cost airline" strategy map might cascade:

  1. Train ground crew on rapid turnaround (L&G) →
  2. Reduce gate time per flight (Process) →
  3. More flights per plane per day, on-time performance (Customer) →
  4. Higher revenue per asset, lower cost per seat-mile (Financial)

The map clarifies which leading measures actually drive strategic outcomes. Random KPIs without strategic linkage clutter the scorecard.

§12.3Designing a scorecard — practical principles

  • 4–7 measures per perspective — more becomes unmanageable; fewer misses key drivers.
  • Mix of leading and lagging — financial alone is too late; only-leading lacks accountability.
  • Aligned with strategy — different strategies (cost leader vs. differentiator) need different measures.
  • Cascadable — corporate scorecard breaks down to divisional, then team-level scorecards. Each level supports the one above.
  • Tied to compensation cautiously — when measures drive bonuses, gaming risk rises. Pair with periodic redesign and cross-checks.

§12.4Customer profitability analysis

Not all customers are equally profitable. Customer profitability analysis decomposes margin per customer (or customer segment) using activity-based costing:

Customer profit = Revenue from customer
 − Variable cost of goods sold
 − Customer-specific costs (sales rep time, customer service, returns, special packaging, fast shipping)
 = Customer-level contribution

Common findings:

  • 20-80 rule: ~20% of customers generate ~80% of profit. Sometimes 5% of customers destroy profit.
  • "Hidden unprofitable customers": high-revenue customers can be unprofitable due to special pricing, demanding service, frequent returns.
  • Strategic responses: re-pricing, service-level changes, account exits, sales-rep redirection.

§12.5Product profitability analysis

Same logic applied to products. Use ABC to allocate overhead by activity drivers (machine setups, design changes, inspections), revealing that low-volume specialised products often consume disproportionate overhead.

  • Volume-based overhead allocation (per unit) systematically under-costs low-volume products.
  • ABC reveals products that look profitable under standard costing but actually destroy value once their full overhead consumption is recognised.
  • Strategic responses: reprice, redesign, simplify, drop, consolidate to higher-volume runs.

§12.6Strategic issues with multi-measure systems

  • Over-measurement paralysis — too many KPIs dilute attention. Pick the small set that actually drives strategy.
  • Gaming and surrogate measures — what gets measured gets gamed. Triangulate with multiple measures so a single one can't be optimised at the expense of strategic intent.
  • Short-term focus — financial measures dominate; long-horizon learning & growth measures often get cut first under pressure. Defend them explicitly.
  • Linking compensation — too tightly coupled invites gaming; too loosely coupled invites indifference. Empirical research suggests anchoring 60-80% of bonus on financial / lagging measures and 20-40% on leading measures.
