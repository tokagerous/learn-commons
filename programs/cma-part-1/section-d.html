Unit 1 · D.1 Measurement Concepts

Cost behavior & cost objects

Before you can manage cost, you need a vocabulary for it. A cost object is anything we want to measure cost for — a unit, a job, a customer, a department. Costs behave in patterns: variable scales with activity, fixed doesn't, mixed has both.

§1.1Cost objects, cost drivers, and assignment

A cost object is the thing being costed. A cost driver is what causes the cost to vary. A cost is direct if it can be traced to the object economically; otherwise it is indirect (overhead) and must be allocated.

Analogy

Think of a restaurant. The cost object is one meal. Direct costs: the steak, the sauce. Indirect: rent, electricity, the manager's salary — real costs, but you can't trace them to one meal without an allocation rule.

§1.2Variable, fixed, and mixed costs

Variable: total scales with activity, per-unit constant (e.g. raw materials). Fixed: total constant, per-unit decreases as activity rises (e.g. factory rent). Mixed (semi-variable): a fixed component plus a variable rate (e.g. utility with a base fee + per-kWh charge).

Total cost (mixed) = Fixed component + (Variable rate × Activity)

§1.3Relevant range and the high-low method

The relevant range is the activity span over which cost-behavior assumptions hold. Outside it, fixed costs may step (need a second machine, second supervisor), and variable rates may change (volume discounts kick in).

The high-low method separates a mixed cost into fixed + variable using only two data points: the highest and lowest activity levels.

Variable rate = (Costhigh − Costlow) ÷ (Activityhigh − Activitylow)
Fixed = Total cost − (Variable rate × Activity) [at either point]
Watch out

High-low uses only the extremes — outliers (a one-off shutdown month, a crash month) distort the result. Regression analysis is more robust because it uses all observations.

Practice 1 — Cost classification

A factory pays $5,000/month rent regardless of output, plus $1.20 per unit for direct materials. The combined cost for one month is best classified as:

  • Variable
  • Fixed
  • Mixed (semi-variable)
  • Step-fixed
Why: Rent is fixed within the relevant range; materials are variable. The aggregate has a fixed component + variable rate, which is the definition of a mixed cost. Step-fixed would mean the fixed portion jumps at thresholds (e.g. needing a second supervisor at 10K units).

Practice 2 — High-low method

Activity high: 20,000 units, total cost $48,000. Activity low: 8,000 units, total cost $24,000. Variable cost per unit and total fixed cost are:

  • $3.00 per unit; $0 fixed
  • $2.00 per unit; $8,000 fixed
  • $1.50 per unit; $12,000 fixed
  • $2.40 per unit; $0 fixed
Why: Variable rate = ($48K − $24K) ÷ (20K − 8K) = $24K ÷ 12K = $2.00/unit. Fixed = $48K − ($2 × 20K) = $48K − $40K = $8,000. Verify with low: $24K − ($2 × 8K) = $24K − $16K = $8K ✓.

Practice 3 — Per-unit fixed cost

As production volume increases (within the relevant range), the per-unit fixed cost:

  • Increases
  • Stays the same
  • Becomes variable
  • Decreases
Why: Total fixed cost is constant; spreading it over more units means each unit absorbs less. This is why high-volume operations enjoy lower per-unit fixed cost — a key driver of operating leverage. The total fixed cost doesn't change inside the relevant range — only per-unit does.

Practice 4 — Direct vs indirect

The salary of a factory supervisor who oversees three product lines is, with respect to one of those product lines, BEST classified as:

  • Indirect cost (overhead)
  • Direct cost
  • Variable cost
  • Period cost
Why: Tracing the supervisor's time to one specific product line isn't economical, so the salary is allocated as overhead — an indirect cost. It's also fixed (one supervisor, fixed salary). With respect to the factory it's a direct factory cost, but with respect to one product line it's indirect.
Unit 2 · D.1 Measurement Concepts

Actual, normal, and standard costing

Three philosophies for how to charge production with overhead and direct costs. They differ in when a cost rate is set (actual vs predetermined) and what that rate represents (last-period actual, this-period budgeted, or an engineered ideal).

§2.1Three approaches

MethodDirect materialsDirect laborManufacturing overhead
ActualActual costActual costActual cost
NormalActual costActual costPredetermined rate × actual activity
StandardStandard rate × standard quantityStandard rate × standard hoursStandard rate × standard hours

Actual costing is timely (you wait for invoices) but volatile — months with idle capacity show high per-unit cost. Normal costing smooths overhead with a budgeted rate. Standard costing benchmarks every input — variances surface deviations for performance review (the engine of Section C).

§2.2The predetermined overhead rate

Predetermined rate = Budgeted total OH ÷ Budgeted activity (e.g. DLH, MH, $)

Applied OH = Predetermined rate × actual activity. The difference between applied OH and actual OH at year-end is the over- or under-applied overhead, which is closed to COGS (or prorated).

Pattern

Under-applied OH ⇒ DR COGS / CR Manufacturing OH (raises COGS). Over-applied ⇒ reverse (lowers COGS). Material amounts can be prorated across WIP, FG, and COGS based on relative balances.

Practice 1 — Predetermined rate

Budgeted overhead $600,000; budgeted direct labor hours 40,000; actual DLH 38,500; actual overhead $585,000. Applied overhead is:

  • $600,000
  • $577,500
  • $585,000
  • $562,500
Why: Predetermined rate = $600K ÷ 40K = $15/DLH. Applied OH = $15 × actual 38,500 DLH = $577,500. The actual rate ($585K ÷ 38.5K = $15.19) isn't used; that's the whole point of normal costing.

Practice 2 — Over- vs under-applied

Using the data above, manufacturing overhead is:

  • Under-applied by $7,500
  • Over-applied by $7,500
  • Under-applied by $15,000
  • Over-applied by $15,000
Why: Actual $585K − Applied $577.5K = $7,500 under-applied. Mnemonic: "AAU" — Actual > Applied = Under (we didn't apply enough). Closing entry: DR COGS $7.5K / CR Manufacturing OH $7.5K.

Practice 3 — Standard cost philosophy

Standard costs are best described as:

  • Last period's actual costs adjusted for inflation
  • The lowest possible cost ever achieved
  • Carefully engineered, achievable benchmarks for inputs and rates
  • Industry averages collected from competitors
Why: A standard is an engineered, attainable target — neither historical nor "ideal/perfect". Two flavors: currently attainable (allows for normal waste; most common) and theoretical/ideal (zero waste, only used as a stretch target). The variances Section C decomposes are differences between actual and these standards.
Unit 3 · D.1 Measurement Concepts

Absorption vs variable costing

Two ways to treat fixed manufacturing overhead. Absorption (full) costing assigns FMOH to each unit — required for U.S. GAAP and tax. Variable (direct) costing expenses FMOH as a period cost — used internally for decision-making and CVP analysis.

§3.1What goes into product cost

CostAbsorptionVariable
Direct materialsProductProduct
Direct laborProductProduct
Variable MOHProductProduct
Fixed MOHProductPeriod
SG&A (variable & fixed)PeriodPeriod

§3.2NI reconciliation

The two methods produce the same NI when production = sales. They diverge when inventory changes:

NIabsorption − NIvariable = ΔInventory units × Fixed MOH per unit
  • Production > Sales (inventory ↑): absorption NI is HIGHER — more FMOH defers to inventory rather than expense.
  • Production < Sales (inventory ↓): absorption NI is LOWER — prior-period FMOH releases through COGS.
Manager incentive

Absorption costing rewards over-production: build extra inventory and you push fixed cost into the balance sheet, raising current-period NI. This is one reason JIT and lean operations correlate with variable-costing internal reporting.

Practice 1 — Method definition

Variable costing differs from absorption costing by treating fixed manufacturing overhead as:

  • A product cost
  • A direct cost
  • A period cost
  • A non-manufacturing cost
Why: Variable costing expenses FMOH in the period incurred (a period cost) rather than capitalizing it into inventory. The other three terms aren't right: it's still a manufacturing cost, can be indirect, and is fixed by definition.

Practice 2 — Reconciliation

Production 10,000 units; sales 8,000 units; FMOH $5/unit. NI under absorption is $40,000. NI under variable costing is:

  • $30,000
  • $40,000
  • $50,000
  • $45,000
Why: ΔInventory = 10K − 8K = +2K units. Absorption NI exceeds variable NI by 2K × $5 = $10K. So Variable NI = $40K − $10K = $30,000. Intuition: absorption deferred $10K of FMOH into the 2K units sitting in ending inventory; variable expensed all of it.

Practice 3 — When NI matches

Absorption and variable costing produce the same NI when:

  • FMOH is zero
  • Beginning inventory equals zero
  • The company uses standard costs
  • Production volume equals sales volume
Why: When units produced = units sold, no FMOH is deferred to or released from inventory. NI is identical. (FMOH = 0 also makes them equal but isn't realistic.) On the CMA, this is a conceptual gimme — make sure you can recite it.
Unit 4 · D.1 Measurement Concepts

Joint costs & by-products

When a single process yields multiple products at a split-off point (e.g. cracking crude oil into gasoline, diesel, jet fuel), the shared cost up to split-off is the joint cost. After split-off, each product can be sold or further processed separately.

§4.1Three allocation methods

  • Sales value at split-off: allocate by each product's market value at the split-off point. Most common; requires reliable market prices at split-off.
  • NRV (net realizable value): when products require further processing before sale, allocate by (final sales − separable costs). Use when no market exists at split-off.
  • Physical units: allocate by quantity (gallons, pounds). Simple but ignores value differences — gasoline and bottled water by the gallon would absorb the same cost.

§4.2By-products and decision-making

A by-product has minor sales value relative to the main products. Two acceptable accountings: (1) credit COGS or joint-cost pool with by-product's sales value (reduces main-product cost), or (2) treat by-product revenue as other income.

Decision rule

Joint costs are sunk the moment they're incurred. The decision to process further or sell at split-off depends ONLY on incremental revenue vs incremental separable cost — never on allocated joint cost.

Practice 1 — Sales value at split-off

Joint cost $90,000 yields Product A (sales value at split-off $80,000) and Product B (sales value at split-off $40,000). Joint cost allocated to A using sales-value-at-split-off is:

  • $45,000
  • $60,000
  • $80,000
  • $30,000
Why: A's share = $80K ÷ ($80K + $40K) = 2/3. Joint cost to A = $90K × 2/3 = $60,000. To B = $90K × 1/3 = $30K. Total $90K ✓.

Practice 2 — Process further

Product B can be sold at split-off for $40,000 or processed further at incremental cost of $15,000 and sold for $60,000. The company should:

  • Process further — incremental margin is $5,000
  • Sell at split-off — joint cost already allocated
  • Process further only if joint cost < $5,000
  • Sell at split-off — separable cost too high
Why: Incremental analysis: ($60K − $40K) − $15K = +$5K. Process further. Joint cost is sunk and irrelevant. The trap is letting the allocated joint cost color the decision; CMA tests this aggressively.

Practice 3 — When NRV is needed

NRV (net realizable value) allocation is preferable to sales-value-at-split-off when:

  • The products are sold in equal physical quantities
  • The joint cost is large relative to total revenue
  • There is no reliable market price at split-off
  • The company wants to discourage further processing
Why: If a product can't be sold at split-off (no market, e.g. crude pulp for paper mills), there's no split-off price to use. NRV imputes a value: (final sales − separable costs). Both methods produce the same allocation when separable costs are zero.
Unit 5 · D.2 Costing Systems

Job-order costing

When products are unique or batched (custom homes, audit engagements, ad campaigns), each job carries its own cost record. The job-cost sheet accumulates direct materials, direct labor, and applied overhead until the job is complete and sold.

§5.1Cost flow through the accounts

Three inventory accounts track the journey: Raw Materials → Work in Process → Finished Goods → COGS.

DM used = Beg RM + Purchases − End RM
COGM = Beg WIP + DM + DL + Applied OH − End WIP
COGS = Beg FG + COGM − End FG

Direct materials and labor trace to specific jobs. Overhead is applied using a predetermined rate (Unit 2). At year-end, any over- or under-applied OH is closed to COGS (or prorated if material).

§5.2Vs. process costing

Process costing is for high-volume, homogeneous output (paint, soft drinks, gasoline). Costs accumulate by department and divide by equivalent units of production. Use job-order when units are distinguishable; use process when they're interchangeable.

Practice 1 — Cost of goods manufactured

Beginning WIP $20,000; DM used $80,000; DL $50,000; applied OH $60,000; ending WIP $25,000. COGM is:

  • $190,000
  • $185,000
  • $210,000
  • $165,000
Why: COGM = $20K (Beg WIP) + $80K + $50K + $60K (current cost added) − $25K (End WIP) = $185,000. The "T-account" view: opening WIP + costs added − ending WIP = costs of jobs that finished and moved to FG.

Practice 2 — Job-order vs process

Which firm is most likely to use process costing rather than job-order?

  • Custom home builder
  • Public accounting firm
  • Soft-drink bottler
  • Movie production studio
Why: A bottling plant produces millions of identical cans through continuous processes — a perfect fit for process costing (cost per equivalent unit, by department). The other three produce distinguishable jobs/projects.

Practice 3 — COGS adjustment

Applied overhead $200,000; actual overhead $215,000. The under-applied overhead is closed to COGS. The journal entry is:

  • DR Manufacturing OH $15,000 / CR COGS $15,000
  • DR Applied OH $15,000 / CR COGS $15,000
  • DR COGS $200,000 / CR Manufacturing OH $200,000
  • DR COGS $15,000 / CR Manufacturing OH $15,000
Why: Actual > Applied → under-applied $15K. We didn't charge enough OH to product cost; closing it raises COGS: DR COGS $15K / CR Manufacturing OH $15K. If material, prorate across WIP, FG, and COGS by relative balances instead.
Unit 6 · D.2 Costing Systems

Activity-based costing (ABC)

Traditional costing pools all overhead and applies one rate (often DLH-based). When products differ in complexity — small batches, special setups, custom packaging — that single rate distorts product cost. ABC traces overhead to activities, then to products by each activity's driver.

§6.1Two-stage allocation

Stage 1: assign indirect resource costs to activity cost pools (e.g. machine setup, quality inspection, order processing). Stage 2: assign each pool to products using its cost driver (setups, inspections, orders).

ActivityDriverHierarchy
Machine setupNumber of setupsBatch-level
Quality inspectionInspectionsBatch-level
Direct laborLabor hoursUnit-level
Engineering changesEngineering hoursProduct-level
Plant security(can't drive)Facility-level

§6.2When ABC matters

ABC pays off when (1) overhead is large vs DM/DL, (2) products differ widely in volume, batch size, or complexity, and (3) the company makes pricing or mix decisions on full cost. Low-volume specialty products under traditional costing usually appear cheaper than they really are; ABC reveals the truth.

CMA cue

Phrases like "the company wants more accurate product costs," "complex/customized products consume disproportionate support," or "pricing decisions" point to ABC as the recommended fix.

Practice 1 — When ABC helps most

ABC provides the GREATEST improvement in product-cost accuracy when:

  • Overhead is large and product complexity varies widely
  • All products are made in identical batches
  • Direct labor is the largest cost component
  • The company sells a single product
Why: Traditional costing distorts when overhead is high AND products differ in how much overhead they truly consume. If everything is identical or DL dominates, traditional costing is fine — and cheaper to operate.

Practice 2 — Cost hierarchy

"Engineering changes specific to one product line" is a:

  • Unit-level activity
  • Batch-level activity
  • Product-level activity
  • Facility-level activity
Why: The four levels (Cooper & Kaplan): unit (per-unit, e.g. machine time), batch (per-batch, e.g. setup), product/sustaining (per-product-line, e.g. engineering, advertising), facility (whole plant, e.g. building security). Product-level cost gets allocated based on which products they support, not units produced.

Practice 3 — Effect on low-volume products

Compared with traditional volume-based costing, ABC typically:

  • Lowers the cost of low-volume specialty products
  • Raises the cost of high-volume standard products
  • Has no effect on individual product costs
  • Raises the cost of low-volume specialty products and lowers the cost of high-volume products
Why: Specialty products consume disproportionate setup, engineering, and inspection — but traditional costing spreads overhead by labor or volume, masking those costs. ABC reveals them. The result: specialty products often look more expensive (and high-volume staples cheaper) under ABC than under volume-based costing.
Unit 7 · D.2 Costing Systems

Life-cycle costing & target costing

Life-cycle costing tracks all costs of a product across R&D, design, production, marketing, distribution, and customer service — not just manufacturing. Target costing works backward from a market-determined price: subtract a required margin, and the remainder is what the product can cost.

§7.1Why look across the whole life cycle

Up to 90% of total life-cycle cost is locked in by design decisions made before manufacturing starts. Optimizing only manufacturing leaves most of the cost lever on the table. Life-cycle costing forces upstream decisions (R&D, design) and downstream costs (warranty, disposal) onto the same scoreboard.

Target cost = Target price − Required profit margin

§7.2Target costing in practice

Used heavily in Japanese manufacturing (Toyota, Sony). The product team works to fit cost to a price the market will accept — value engineering removes features or processes that cost more than they add to customer-perceived value. Iterate until target cost is met.

Contrast

Cost-plus pricing: build it, calculate the cost, add a margin, hope the market accepts the price. Target costing: lock the price first, then design the product to fit.

Practice 1 — Where costs are locked

In life-cycle costing, the largest portion of a product's total cost is typically committed:

  • During production
  • During design and development
  • During customer service and warranty support
  • Equally across all stages
Why: Studies (notably by Robin Cooper) suggest 80–90% of life-cycle cost is committed during R&D and design — material choice, parts count, manufacturability, ease of repair, energy consumption. Cutting cost after launch is far harder and far cheaper to prevent than to fix.

Practice 2 — Target cost calculation

Market price $400; required ROS 25%. Target cost is:

  • $400
  • $320
  • $300
  • $500
Why: Target cost = Target price − Required margin = $400 − ($400 × 25%) = $400 − $100 = $300. ROS (return on sales) is calculated as a % of revenue, not cost. If the target margin were 25% on cost (markup), it'd be $400 / 1.25 = $320 — read carefully.

Practice 3 — Tool that supports target costing

When target cost cannot be met by current designs, the most common tool used to bridge the gap is:

  • Value engineering
  • Variance analysis
  • Standard costing
  • Life-cycle costing
Why: Value engineering systematically reviews each function of a product, asking whether it adds customer-perceived value commensurate with its cost. Costly features that don't add proportional value are simplified or removed. Variance analysis is downstream (after the fact); life-cycle costing tracks costs but doesn't reduce them.
Unit 8 · D.3 Overhead Costs

Overhead behavior & allocation bases

Overhead is everything in production cost that isn't direct materials or direct labor. Some of it scales with output (variable OH: indirect materials, utilities for machines); some doesn't (fixed OH: rent, supervision, depreciation). Allocating it to products requires picking a base that approximates how products consume the resource.

§8.1Plantwide vs departmental rates

A plantwide rate uses one base for the whole plant — simple but inaccurate when departments differ in resource intensity. A departmental rate sets a separate rate for each department (machining vs assembly), each with its own base (machine hours vs labor hours).

Choosing the base: machine-intensive departments → machine hours; labor-intensive → labor hours; high-cost-of-material → material dollars. The base should cause overhead to be incurred, not just correlate.

§8.2Capacity definitions for the denominator

  • Theoretical capacity: 100% of the time, no breaks. Unrealistic; rarely used.
  • Practical capacity: theoretical minus normal downtime (maintenance, holidays). Best for cost-management decisions.
  • Normal/expected capacity: average over several years. Smooths out cyclical demand.
  • Master-budget capacity: this year's expected volume. Common for setting predetermined rates.
Trap

Using master-budget capacity in a downturn raises the predetermined OH rate (less volume to absorb fixed OH), making products look more expensive — which can drive misguided price increases that further reduce demand. Practical capacity isolates the unused-capacity cost as a separate variance.

Practice 1 — Plantwide vs departmental

A plantwide overhead rate is most likely to distort product cost when:

  • All products use the same proportion of resources in each department
  • Products differ in how much they use machine-intensive vs labor-intensive departments
  • Overhead is small compared to direct materials
  • The plant has only one department
Why: A plantwide rate spreads ALL overhead by ONE base. If product X is 90% machined and product Y is 90% assembled, but the rate is set on labor hours, X is undercosted and Y overcosted. Departmental rates fix this. ABC takes it further — at the activity level.

Practice 2 — Best base for a labor-light, machine-heavy department

The CNC machining department runs nearly autonomously with minimal labor. The most appropriate allocation base for its overhead is:

  • Direct labor hours
  • Direct labor cost
  • Machine hours
  • Direct material cost
Why: The base should cause the overhead. In a CNC department, the machine drives utility, depreciation, and maintenance — overhead correlates with machine hours, not labor. Material cost is a poor proxy unless overhead is procurement-driven.

Practice 3 — Capacity choice

A company in a cyclical industry wants product costs that don't swing with the cycle. The most appropriate denominator for the predetermined OH rate is:

  • Theoretical capacity
  • Master-budget capacity for the current year
  • Practical capacity
  • Normal (expected long-run) capacity
Why: Normal capacity averages activity across the cycle, producing a stable per-unit OH. Master-budget swings with the cycle; theoretical is unrealistic; practical isolates idle-capacity cost (also good but doesn't smooth cycles by itself).
Unit 9 · D.3 Overhead Costs

Service department allocation

Service departments (maintenance, IT, HR, cafeteria) don't produce sellable output but support the production departments that do. Their cost has to flow through to products via an allocation. Three methods, in increasing accuracy: direct, step, and reciprocal.

§9.1The three methods

  • Direct: allocate each service-dept cost directly to production departments only. Ignores any service rendered between service depts.
  • Step (sequential): rank service departments (often by cost or by % of services rendered to others). Allocate first one to ALL remaining departments. Continue down the list. Once allocated, no costs return.
  • Reciprocal (algebraic): solve simultaneous equations to fully recognize mutual services. Most accurate; used by sophisticated firms.

§9.2Single vs dual rates

Service-dept cost has variable and fixed components. A dual rate separates them: variable cost charged on actual usage, fixed cost charged based on long-term capacity reserved. This penalizes a department for over-using a variable service while keeping the fixed-cost charge predictable.

Why CMA tests this

Allocation choice changes which production department appears profitable. Bad allocation creates bad performance signals — and bad performance signals create bad decisions. The CMA wants you to read the allocation method and predict its effects.

Practice 1 — Direct method

Maintenance ($60K) serves Assembly (60%), Painting (30%), and HR (10%). Under the direct method, the cost allocated to Assembly is:

  • $40,000
  • $36,000
  • $30,000
  • $60,000
Why: Direct method ignores the 10% serving HR. Re-prorate Assembly + Painting only: 60 / (60+30) = 2/3. $60K × 2/3 = $40,000. The 10% HR portion is forced onto the production departments rather than charged to HR.

Practice 2 — Most accurate method

Which service-dept allocation method most accurately reflects the cross-services among service departments?

  • Direct
  • Step (sequential)
  • Reciprocal
  • Plantwide
Why: Reciprocal solves simultaneous equations: HR's full cost (including its share of Maintenance) is allocated, AND Maintenance's full cost (including its share of HR) is allocated. Direct ignores all cross-service; step recognizes one direction.

Practice 3 — Dual rate

A dual-rate allocation system most directly addresses the issue that:

  • Variable costs are easier to estimate than fixed costs
  • Fixed and variable cost components respond to different drivers
  • Reciprocal services exist among departments
  • Some departments have no usage at all
Why: Dual rate keeps variable cost on actual usage (penalizing waste) and fixed cost on reserved capacity (penalizing over-budgeted demand). Lumping them together creates incentives to under-use the service to dodge variable, or under-budget to dodge fixed.
Unit 10 · D.4 Supply Chain Management

Lean, JIT, ERP, and capacity

Production-philosophy questions on the CMA test vocabulary recognition more than calculation. Know what each term means, what it eliminates, and how it interacts with capacity.

§10.1Lean and JIT

Lean targets the seven wastes (Toyota Production System): defects, overproduction, waiting, non-utilized talent, transportation, inventory, motion, extra-processing. Just-in-time (JIT) is a key lean technique: produce or order only what the next stage needs, when it needs it.

JIT shrinks WIP and finished-goods inventory dramatically — but exposes the firm to supplier failures and demand spikes. JIT works best with reliable suppliers (often single-sourced with long-term contracts) and stable demand patterns.

§10.2ERP and theory of constraints

Enterprise resource planning (ERP) integrates finance, inventory, HR, manufacturing, and sales into a single system (SAP, Oracle, NetSuite). The benefit is one consistent view; the risk is implementation cost, organizational disruption, and over-customization.

Theory of constraints (TOC) (Goldratt): every process has a single bottleneck. Throughput is set by the bottleneck. The five focusing steps: identify the constraint, exploit it (run flat-out), subordinate everything else, elevate it, then repeat.

Throughput contribution = Sales − DM cost (NOT the same as contribution margin)

Practice 1 — JIT prerequisite

A successful JIT inventory system most depends on:

  • High inventory levels to absorb supplier disruptions
  • Reliable suppliers and predictable demand
  • A diverse pool of low-cost suppliers
  • Long lead times for raw material orders
Why: JIT works only when raw materials arrive on time, every time, and demand doesn't spike unpredictably. Disruption immediately stops production because there's no buffer. Tighter inventory = less margin for error = more discipline required from suppliers.

Practice 2 — TOC throughput

Theory of constraints defines throughput as:

  • Total revenue
  • Sales minus all variable costs
  • Sales minus full manufacturing cost
  • Sales minus direct material cost
Why: TOC's throughput contribution = Sales − Direct material. Goldratt argues that in the short run, only DM truly varies with output; labor and overhead behave more like fixed operating expense. Don't conflate this with traditional contribution margin.

Practice 3 — Lean waste

Producing 500 units when only 350 will be sold this period is an example of which lean waste?

  • Overproduction
  • Waiting
  • Defects
  • Motion
Why: Overproduction is considered the worst waste because it triggers others — extra inventory, extra movement, extra storage, hidden defects. Lean preaches "produce to demand" as the only safe rule.
Unit 11 · D.5 Business Process Improvement

Value chain & continuous improvement

Porter's value chain decomposes a firm into primary activities (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing, service) and support activities (firm infrastructure, HR, technology, procurement). Each activity adds value — or wastes it.

§11.1Value-added vs non-value-added

A value-added activity contributes to customer-perceived value (assembly, painting). A non-value-added activity doesn't (storage, rework, waiting, inspection of poor-quality output). Eliminate the second category as far as practical.

Manufacturing cycle efficiency: MCE = Processing time ÷ (Processing + Inspection + Move + Wait time)

§11.2Continuous improvement (kaizen)

Kaizen means small, continuous improvements driven by everyone in the organization. Business process reengineering (BPR) is a one-shot ground-up redesign — bigger swings, bigger risk. Six Sigma targets defect reduction (3.4 defects per million opportunities) using DMAIC: Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control.

Pattern

Kaizen is incremental and bottom-up; BPR is transformative and top-down; Six Sigma is data-driven and process-specific. CMA questions often ask which fits a given situation.

Practice 1 — Non-value-added

All of the following are typically non-value-added activities EXCEPT:

  • Inventory storage
  • Rework of defective products
  • Final assembly
  • Inspection of incoming materials
Why: Final assembly transforms parts into the finished product the customer pays for — value-added. Storage, rework, and inspection don't change the product itself; the customer wouldn't pay for them. Lean operations target their elimination, not their reduction.

Practice 2 — MCE

Processing 4 hours; inspection 1 hour; move 1 hour; wait 4 hours. Manufacturing cycle efficiency is:

  • 40%
  • 50%
  • 80%
  • 100%
Why: MCE = Processing ÷ (P + I + M + W) = 4 ÷ (4+1+1+4) = 4 ÷ 10 = 40%. World-class operations target 60%+. Improvement comes from cutting non-value-added time (move, wait, inspection).

Practice 3 — BPR vs kaizen

A company is replacing its 30-year-old order-processing system, redesigning workflows from scratch and retraining all staff. This is best described as:

  • Kaizen
  • Continuous improvement
  • Total quality management
  • Business process reengineering (BPR)
Why: BPR is the radical, ground-up redesign — fits a wholesale system replacement. Kaizen is small, frequent, incremental. Six Sigma focuses on defect rate. TQM is a broader culture, not a one-time redesign.
Unit 12 · D.5 Business Process Improvement

Cost of quality (PAF) & benchmarking

Quality is rarely free. The Cost of Quality (CoQ) framework groups quality-related costs into four categories. The first two prevent or detect; the last two are failures already incurred.

§12.1The PAF framework

CategoryWhat it isExamples
PreventionStop defects before they happenTraining, supplier qualification, design reviews, process control
AppraisalDetect defects after creationInspection, testing, audits
Internal failureDefects caught before shippingScrap, rework, downtime, retesting
External failureDefects caught by customersWarranty, returns, recalls, lost reputation, lost sales

The classic insight: investing in prevention reduces internal AND external failure spending — by far. External failure carries hidden costs (lost trust, lost future sales) that don't show up in the books.

§12.2Benchmarking

Compare a metric against best-in-class. Three flavors:

  • Internal: best-performing unit within the company. Cheapest, easiest, narrowest.
  • Competitive: against direct competitors. Hard to access data; legal/ethical guardrails.
  • Functional/best-practice: against best-in-class anywhere (e.g., Walmart studied UPS for logistics, not another retailer). Often most useful.

Practice 1 — Quality cost classification

Costs of customer warranty claims belong in which CoQ category?

  • External failure
  • Internal failure
  • Appraisal
  • Prevention
Why: The defect reached the customer; that's external failure by definition. Customer-facing failure is the most expensive of the four — it includes both the warranty cost AND the unmeasurable cost of lost trust and word-of-mouth.

Practice 2 — Investment trade-off

Increasing prevention spending by $200K causes external-failure cost to drop by $350K. The company should:

  • Decline — prevention isn't a value-added activity
  • Decline — only revenue increases create value
  • Accept only if internal-failure cost also drops
  • Accept — net cost decreases by $150K plus unbooked reputational benefit
Why: Direct savings of $150K AND an additional unbooked benefit (reputation, retained customers). Prevention is the highest-ROI category in PAF analysis. The "law of 1-10-100" rule of thumb: a defect costs $1 to prevent, $10 to detect internally, $100 to fix externally.

Practice 3 — Best benchmarking type

A regional bank wants to dramatically improve its call-center customer experience. The most useful benchmarking type is:

  • Internal — best-performing branch
  • Competitive — other regional banks
  • Functional — best-in-class call centers in any industry (e.g. Zappos, Amazon)
  • Strategic — overall industry trends
Why: Functional/best-practice benchmarking finds the absolute best, regardless of industry. A bank wanting world-class CX should learn from world-class CX operators (Zappos, Ritz-Carlton). Internal/competitive benchmarks stay within current industry norms — limited upside.

Question 1Cost behavior

A cost that increases in steps as activity rises (e.g. one supervisor per 10 line workers) is classified as:

  • Variable
  • Mixed
  • Step-fixed
  • Curvilinear
Why: Step-fixed costs jump at activity thresholds and stay flat between jumps. Inside one step, behavior is fixed; across multiple steps, it appears variable. Common for supervision, equipment capacity.
Question 2High-low

High activity 30,000 units, total cost $130,000; low activity 10,000 units, total cost $70,000. Variable rate is:

  • $3.00 per unit
  • $4.33 per unit
  • $2.00 per unit
  • $6.00 per unit
Why: ($130K − $70K) ÷ (30K − 10K) = $60K ÷ 20K = $3.00/unit. Fixed = $130K − ($3 × 30K) = $40K.
Question 3Normal costing

Under normal costing, manufacturing overhead is applied to production using:

  • Actual rate × actual activity
  • Actual rate × budgeted activity
  • Budgeted rate × budgeted activity
  • Budgeted (predetermined) rate × actual activity
Why: Normal costing's whole point: lock the rate at the start of the year (predetermined) but multiply by ACTUAL activity. Fluctuations in OH per unit are smoothed; idle-capacity months don't inflate per-unit cost.
Question 4Variable costing

If production exceeds sales, NI under absorption costing will be:

  • Lower than under variable costing
  • Equal to variable costing
  • Higher than under variable costing
  • Unrelated to variable costing
Why: Production > Sales → inventory rises → absorption defers FMOH into inventory → less FMOH expensed → higher NI. Variable costing expenses all FMOH immediately, producing lower NI when inventory grows.
Question 5Joint costs

A by-product can be sold for $4,000 net. Under the cost-reduction approach, this $4,000 is:

  • Recorded as other revenue
  • Credited against joint cost of main products
  • Ignored as immaterial
  • Allocated using NRV across all products
Why: The cost-reduction approach reduces the joint-cost pool charged to main products by the by-product's net realizable value. Alternative: treat as other revenue (income approach). Both are GAAP-acceptable; the question specifies cost-reduction.
Question 6Process further

A joint product sells at split-off for $20,000 and after further processing for $30,000 with separable cost of $7,000. The decision:

  • Process further — incremental margin $3,000
  • Sell at split-off — separable cost too high
  • Process further only if joint cost < $3,000
  • Sell at split-off — joint cost is sunk
Why: Incremental analysis ignores joint cost (sunk). ($30K − $20K) − $7K = +$3K margin from further processing. Accept it.
Question 7COGM

Beg WIP $15K; DM used $40K; DL $30K; applied OH $20K; End WIP $10K. COGM is:

  • $100,000
  • $95,000
  • $105,000
  • $90,000
Why: COGM = $15K + $40K + $30K + $20K − $10K = $95,000.
Question 8Over-applied OH

Applied OH $250K; actual OH $235K. Closing entry to COGS:

  • DR COGS $15K / CR Manufacturing OH $15K
  • DR Manufacturing OH $250K / CR COGS $250K
  • DR Manufacturing OH $15K / CR COGS $15K
  • No entry — applied = budget
Why: Applied > Actual → over-applied $15K (we charged too much OH to product). Reduce COGS: DR Manufacturing OH $15K / CR COGS $15K. Mnemonic AAU/AAO: Applied > Actual = Over.
Question 9ABC

ABC is most appropriate when:

  • The company has a single product
  • Direct labor is the largest cost
  • All products are produced in equal batches
  • Overhead is large and product diversity is high
Why: ABC pays off when overhead is high AND products vary widely in resource consumption. Otherwise the cost of operating ABC exceeds the accuracy benefit.
Question 10Target costing

Market price $250; required ROS 30%. Target cost:

  • $250
  • $192
  • $175
  • $325
Why: Target cost = $250 − ($250 × 30%) = $250 − $75 = $175. ROS is on revenue, not cost.
Question 11Plantwide rate

A plantwide overhead rate is acceptable when:

  • Overhead is large and product mix is diverse
  • Products consume departmental resources in similar proportions
  • The plant has many specialized departments
  • Customers demand granular cost transparency
Why: If every product passes through every department in roughly the same proportion, a plantwide rate produces the same allocation as departmental rates — for free. The simpler system wins.
Question 12Step method

In the step method of service-dept allocation:

  • All cross-services are recognized via simultaneous equations
  • No service-to-service allocations occur
  • Once a service dept's costs are allocated, none flow back to it
  • Only fixed costs are allocated
Why: Step is sequential: order the depts (often by % serving others), allocate the first to all remaining, the second to those after it, and so on. Costs flow forward only. The reciprocal method recognizes mutual services through simultaneous equations.
Question 13JIT

A successful JIT system reduces:

  • Inventory holding costs
  • Need for supplier reliability
  • Importance of quality control
  • Coordination requirements
Why: JIT's chief benefit is dramatically lower inventory at every stage (raw, WIP, finished). It REQUIRES (not reduces) supplier reliability, quality, and coordination — the buffer that inventory used to provide is gone.
Question 14MCE

Total throughput time 20 hours, of which processing is 5, inspection 3, move 2, wait 10. MCE:

  • 15%
  • 20%
  • 25%
  • 50%
Why: MCE = Processing ÷ Total = 5 ÷ 20 = 25%. Pulling waiting time down is usually the biggest lever.
Question 15Cost of quality

Training a new operator on quality procedures is BEST classified as:

  • Appraisal
  • Prevention
  • Internal failure
  • External failure
Why: Training prevents defects from being created in the first place — pure prevention. Appraisal would be inspection or testing. Spending on prevention typically reduces both internal and external failure spending several-fold.
