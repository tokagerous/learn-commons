Unit 1 · F.1 Information Systems

Accounting information systems & ERP

An AIS captures, processes, and reports accounting transactions. An ERP system extends that capability across the whole enterprise — finance, inventory, manufacturing, HR, sales — running on a single integrated database.

§1.1Cycles inside an AIS

  • Revenue cycle — order, ship, bill, collect.
  • Expenditure cycle — request, approve, receive, pay.
  • Production cycle — plan, manufacture, account.
  • HR/payroll — hire, time, compensate.
  • Financing — capital structure, equity, debt.
  • General ledger / reporting — close, consolidate, report.

§1.2ERP — promises and pitfalls

Benefits: single source of truth, real-time visibility, standardized processes, lower IT footprint long-term. Costs: large up-front license + implementation, organizational disruption, customization risk, vendor lock-in.

Implementation rule

Customize the ERP as little as possible. Adapt your processes to the system, not the other way around — every customization is a future upgrade tax.

Practice 1 — ERP advantage

A primary benefit of ERP over disconnected functional systems is:

  • Single source of truth across business functions
  • Lower implementation cost
  • Easier customization
  • Reduced training requirements
Why: One database means inventory, finance, sales all see the same data in real time. The downsides — implementation cost, training, customization complexity — are well-known. ERP wins on integration, not on simplicity or cost.

Practice 2 — Cycle classification

Receiving goods, matching the receipt to a PO, and recording the payable belongs to the:

  • Revenue cycle
  • Production cycle
  • Expenditure cycle
  • Financing cycle
Why: Expenditure (procure-to-pay): request → PO → receipt → 3-way match → payable → payment. Revenue is order-to-cash. Don't confuse "expenditure" (everyday operating spend) with "financing" (capital raises).

Practice 3 — ERP customization

A widely cited rule of ERP implementation is:

  • Customize the system to mirror current processes exactly
  • Adapt processes to the system; minimize customization
  • Buy multiple ERPs to mitigate vendor lock-in
  • Skip user training to reduce cost
Why: Heavy customization fights every future upgrade and tightens vendor lock-in. Best practice: change processes to fit configurable ERP options where possible. Reserve customization for true competitive differentiators.
Unit 2 · F.1 Information Systems

Enterprise performance management (EPM)

EPM (also called CPM or BPM) extends the ERP transactional layer with planning, budgeting, forecasting, consolidation, and management reporting. Examples: Oracle EPM Cloud, SAP BPC, Anaplan, Workday Adaptive Planning.

§2.1What EPM does that ERP doesn't

  • Driver-based planning — model revenue and cost from operational drivers, not just GL accounts.
  • Multi-scenario forecasting — base/upside/downside, sensitivity, what-if.
  • Consolidation & close — multi-entity, multi-currency, intercompany eliminations.
  • Management reporting — profitability by product/customer/segment, beyond GAAP-required reports.

§2.2Why it sits separately from ERP

ERP is optimized for transactional speed and accuracy; EPM for analytical flexibility. Trying to do both in one system usually compromises one or the other. Most large enterprises run both, with the EPM consuming actuals from the ERP.

Practice 1 — EPM scope

EPM systems primarily support:

  • Daily transaction processing
  • Customer order management
  • Planning, budgeting, forecasting, and consolidation
  • Cyber-security operations
Why: EPM = the management-accounting layer above the GL. ERP runs the transactions; EPM runs the planning and decision-support analytics on top of them.

Practice 2 — Driver-based planning

"Driver-based planning" means:

  • Modeling revenue and cost from operational drivers (units, customers, headcount)
  • Setting budgets equal to last year × inflation
  • Letting top management drive every line item
  • Building plans only from historical GL trends
Why: Driver-based plans tie financial outcomes to operational levers (units sold, headcount, square feet). When a driver changes, the plan recalculates. This is far more responsive than line-by-line GL incrementing.

Practice 3 — Consolidation features

Multi-entity, multi-currency consolidation with intercompany elimination is a core feature of:

  • An accounting information system (AIS)
  • A point-of-sale system
  • A CRM
  • An EPM (or specialized consolidation) system
Why: Consolidation tools are EPM territory (Oracle HFM, SAP BPC, OneStream). The base AIS/ERP records each entity's transactions, but rolling them up into group statements with translation and elimination is an EPM-class function.
Unit 3 · F.2 Data Governance

Data governance, lifecycle, and security

Data governance is the framework — people, processes, and policies — that makes data a managed asset. It covers ownership, quality, lifecycle, access, classification, and retention.

§3.1Data lifecycle

Create / capture → Store → Use → Share → Archive → Destroy. Each stage has its own controls (validation at capture, encryption at storage, role-based access at use, etc.).

§3.2Quality dimensions

  • Accuracy — values match reality.
  • Completeness — required values present.
  • Consistency — same value across systems.
  • Timeliness — current enough to be useful.
  • Validity — conforms to business rules.
  • Uniqueness — no duplicates.

§3.3Data ethics & privacy

GDPR (Europe), CCPA (California), HIPAA (US healthcare) — major privacy regulations with extraterritorial reach. Penalties scale with revenue. Beyond compliance: ethical use of data (avoiding biased models, respecting consent, transparency in algorithmic decisions).

Practice 1 — Quality dimension

A customer record showing "addr: 123 Main St" in CRM but "address: 123 Main Street" in billing fails which data quality dimension?

  • Accuracy
  • Completeness
  • Consistency
  • Timeliness
Why: Both values are accurate (same street); both are complete; neither is stale. They differ across systems — that's consistency. Master data management (MDM) addresses this by maintaining a single canonical record.

Practice 2 — Lifecycle stage

A retention policy that says "transaction records must be destroyed 7 years after creation" addresses which lifecycle stage?

  • Destruction / disposal
  • Capture
  • Use
  • Share
Why: Retention dictates how long data is kept and when/how it's destroyed. Beyond compliance (e.g. SOX 7-year rule), retention reduces breach exposure — data you don't have can't be stolen.

Practice 3 — Privacy regulation

GDPR's "right to be forgotten" requires:

  • Encrypting all stored data
  • Two-factor authentication for users
  • 30-day notice before any data sharing
  • Erasing personal data on a data-subject's request, where no other lawful basis applies
Why: Article 17 of GDPR creates the right of erasure. Data subjects can demand deletion of personal data; companies must comply unless retention is required by law (e.g. tax records). Implementation requires knowing where every personal-data copy lives — a major data-governance challenge.
Unit 4 · F.3 Finance Transformation

System development life cycle (SDLC)

A disciplined sequence — analysis, design, development, testing, implementation, maintenance — for building or buying systems. Two flavors dominate: waterfall (sequential, phase-gated) and agile (iterative, sprint-based).

§4.1The phases

  1. Analysis — define requirements with users.
  2. Design — translate requirements into system architecture.
  3. Development — build (or configure for COTS).
  4. Testing — unit, integration, UAT.
  5. Implementation — deploy. Strategies: parallel, pilot, phased, big-bang.
  6. Maintenance — fix, enhance, decommission.

§4.2Conversion strategies

StrategyHowRiskCost
ParallelOld + new run side-by-sideLowHigh
PilotOne unit/site converts firstLowMedium
PhasedModule by moduleMediumMedium
Big bangCut over all at onceHighLow
Choosing

Mission-critical systems with downtime intolerance get parallel. Limited-risk apps with budget constraints can big-bang. Most large ERP migrations are phased.

Practice 1 — Lowest-risk strategy

The implementation strategy with the LOWEST risk and HIGHEST cost is:

  • Big bang
  • Parallel
  • Pilot
  • Phased
Why: Parallel runs both systems together until the new one is proven, then retires the old. Lowest risk (always have a fallback) but highest cost (running two systems). Big bang is the opposite trade-off.

Practice 2 — Agile distinguishing feature

Agile development differs from waterfall primarily by:

  • Skipping the testing phase
  • Eliminating documentation
  • Delivering working software in short iterations with continuous user feedback
  • Avoiding user involvement until launch
Why: Agile = short iterations (sprints), working software at the end of each, continuous user feedback. Waterfall locks requirements upfront and delivers the whole system at the end. Agile doesn't eliminate testing or documentation — it integrates them throughout.

Practice 3 — UAT

User acceptance testing (UAT) primarily verifies:

  • The system meets business requirements as defined by users
  • Code passes unit tests
  • The infrastructure scales under load
  • Security vulnerabilities have been remediated
Why: UAT is the final go/no-go test before launch — done by actual end users, against actual business scenarios. Unit, integration, and security tests are earlier and technical. UAT failure means the system works as built but doesn't fit how the business operates.
Unit 5 · F.3 Finance Transformation

RPA, AI, blockchain — innovative finance applications

The CMA tests vocabulary and use-case fit, not deep technical detail. Know what each technology is, what finance problem it solves, and what it can't do.

§5.1Robotic process automation (RPA)

RPA = software bots that mimic human GUI actions across applications (open ERP, copy field, paste into spreadsheet, save, email). Best for high-volume, rule-based, repetitive tasks. Fast to deploy, cheap relative to humans, but brittle when underlying screens change.

Common finance uses: invoice processing, journal entries, account reconciliation, tax-form prep, regulatory filings.

§5.2AI / machine learning

AI goes beyond rules — pattern recognition, prediction, generation. ML learns from data; you don't program rules, you train models.

  • Anomaly detection — flag unusual transactions (fraud, errors).
  • Forecasting — demand, cash flow, churn.
  • NLP / GenAI — extract data from contracts, draft narratives, summarize variances.

§5.3Blockchain

Distributed ledger with cryptographic chaining and consensus. No single party controls; entries are tamper-evident. Use cases: supply-chain provenance, cross-border payments, smart contracts, tokenized assets. Often overhyped; not every problem needs decentralization.

Practice 1 — RPA fit

RPA is BEST suited to which type of task?

  • High-volume, rule-based, repetitive (e.g. invoice posting)
  • Low-volume, judgment-intensive (e.g. M&A negotiation)
  • Pure pattern recognition with no rules
  • Tasks requiring physical robotics
Why: RPA shines on structured, repetitive work. Judgment tasks need humans (or AI). Pattern recognition is ML/AI territory. RPA is purely software — no physical robots involved despite the name.

Practice 2 — Finance AI use

A bank uses ML to flag transactions that look unusual versus a customer's history. This is:

  • RPA
  • Blockchain
  • Anomaly detection
  • Process mining
Why: Anomaly-detection models learn baseline behavior and surface deviations — the workhorse of fraud detection and audit analytics. RPA performs predefined actions; blockchain stores ledgers; process mining extracts process flows from event logs.

Practice 3 — Blockchain claim

A core feature of blockchain is:

  • Centralized control by a single party
  • Tamper-evident, append-only distributed ledger
  • Faster than centralized databases for all queries
  • Always anonymous
Why: Each block links cryptographically to the previous one; modifying any record requires re-computing the chain — tamper-evident. It's distributed and append-only. Centralized DBs are usually faster than blockchains for read-heavy work, and many chains are pseudonymous (not anonymous) — addresses are public.
Unit 6 · F.4 Data Analytics

Business intelligence & data mining

Business intelligence (BI) packages historical data into dashboards and reports — typically structured, aggregated views of GL/sales/operations. Data mining applies statistical and ML techniques to large datasets to find patterns: clusters, associations, predictions, outliers.

§6.1Data warehouse vs data lake

  • Data warehouse — structured, schema-on-write, optimized for BI queries. Examples: Snowflake, BigQuery, Redshift.
  • Data lake — raw, schema-on-read, holds structured + unstructured (logs, images, video). Examples: S3, ADLS.
  • Lakehouse — hybrid: lake's flexibility + warehouse-style querying.

§6.2Common mining techniques

TechniqueQuestion it answers
ClassificationWhich category does this belong to? (fraud / not fraud)
RegressionWhat numeric value? (forecast revenue)
ClusteringWhich natural groups exist? (customer segments)
AssociationWhat goes with what? (market basket)
Anomaly detectionWhich observations don't fit? (fraud, errors)

Practice 1 — Warehouse vs lake

A data lake differs from a data warehouse primarily by:

  • Storing only structured data
  • Requiring schema-on-write before loading
  • Holding raw data of any type, applying schema on read
  • Being a real-time transactional system
Why: Lakes accept structured + unstructured + semi-structured data in raw form. Schema is applied later, when querying. Warehouses force schema upfront, optimizing for analytic queries.

Practice 2 — Mining technique

Identifying that customers who buy product A often also buy product B is an example of:

  • Association (market basket) analysis
  • Regression
  • Classification
  • Clustering
Why: Association rules ("if A → B with confidence X%") drive cross-sell and product-placement decisions. Classification predicts a category for a known label; clustering finds groups without labels.

Practice 3 — BI scope

Business intelligence dashboards primarily provide:

  • Real-time stock-trading recommendations
  • Predictive customer-lifetime-value models
  • Source-code reviews
  • Aggregated, historical views of operational and financial KPIs
Why: BI dashboards summarize "what happened" — sales, margin, headcount, AR aging. Predictive models live in the analytics/data-science stack on top of the warehouse, not in the dashboards themselves.
Unit 7 · F.4 Data Analytics

The four types of analytics

A canonical hierarchy of analytical sophistication, each answering a different question. The CMA tests recognition: which type does this scenario need?

§7.1The ladder

TypeQuestionTools
DescriptiveWhat happened?Dashboards, reports, GL summaries
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Drill-down, variance analysis, root cause
PredictiveWhat will happen?Forecasting, ML models, scenario analysis
PrescriptiveWhat should we do?Optimization, decision automation, recommendations

Each layer builds on the previous: prescriptive without predictive isn't grounded; predictive without diagnostic doesn't know which features matter; diagnostic without descriptive can't even identify the problem.

§7.2Maturity in finance

Most finance functions live in the descriptive/diagnostic layers. Predictive (rolling forecasts, churn prediction, demand sensing) is the modern frontier. Prescriptive (auto-pricing, portfolio optimization) is rare in mainstream finance but common in supply chain and trading.

Practice 1 — Diagnostic

"Sales were down 12% last quarter — why?" An analyst drills into product, region, and customer segment to find the root cause. This is:

  • Descriptive analytics
  • Predictive analytics
  • Prescriptive analytics
  • Diagnostic analytics
Why: "Why" is diagnostic. "What happened" (sales were down 12%) is descriptive. The driving question determines the type.

Practice 2 — Predictive

A model that estimates next quarter's cash flow from current AR aging, sales pipeline, and seasonality is:

  • Descriptive
  • Diagnostic
  • Predictive
  • Prescriptive
Why: "What will happen" — predictive. Add an optimization layer that says "to keep cash above $X, accelerate collections from these specific customers" and you're prescriptive.

Practice 3 — Prescriptive

A recommendation engine that suggests "increase price 4% on these SKUs to maximize Q4 margin within the demand-elasticity bounds" is:

  • Prescriptive analytics
  • Descriptive analytics
  • Predictive analytics
  • Diagnostic analytics
Why: Prescriptive recommends an action. It typically combines a predictive model (demand response to price) with an optimization (maximize margin subject to constraints). Mature analytics organizations can deploy this; many can't.
Unit 8 · F.4 Data Analytics

Data visualization

A good chart converts data into insight at a glance. A bad chart obscures or misleads. The CMA tests two things: chart-type fit (which chart for which question?) and recognition of misleading practices (truncated axes, dual-axis confusion, 3D distortion).

§8.1Chart-type fit

ChartBest for
Bar / columnComparing magnitudes across categories
LineTrends over time
PieParts of a whole (use only with few segments)
ScatterRelationship between two variables
HeatmapDensity across two dimensions
WaterfallSequential additions/subtractions to a total
Box plotDistribution + outliers

§8.2Misleading visualizations

  • Truncated y-axis: not starting at zero exaggerates differences. Acceptable on line charts of trends; problematic on bar charts.
  • Dual-axis confusion: comparing series on different scales can imply correlation that isn't real.
  • 3D effects: tilt distorts size perception. Avoid.
  • Cherry-picking time ranges: pick a window that supports a story.
CFO standard

For board-level finance reporting, prefer simple, defensible chart types. Bar/column for comparison, line for trends, waterfall for variance bridges. Reserve novelty (sankey, treemap) for cases where they genuinely help.

Practice 1 — Chart fit

The BEST chart to show monthly revenue from January through December (one year, one company) is:

  • Pie chart
  • Line chart
  • Stacked bar chart
  • Treemap
Why: Trend over time = line chart. Pie shows composition at a single point. Stacked bars are useful when each month has multiple components. Treemaps show hierarchical proportions.

Practice 2 — Variance bridge

Showing how Q1 NI moved from $3.0M last year to $3.4M this year via volume, price, mix, and cost effects is BEST done with a:

  • Pie chart
  • Scatter plot
  • Waterfall chart
  • Histogram
Why: Waterfall is built for sequential additions/subtractions starting from a base and ending at a total. Variance bridges, EBITDA bridges, cash-flow bridges — all waterfalls.

Practice 3 — Misleading practice

A bar chart whose y-axis starts at $9,500 instead of $0 is most likely to:

  • Exaggerate small differences between bars
  • Improve readability for trends
  • Be required for compliance with GAAP
  • Have no effect on perception
Why: Truncating the y-axis on a bar chart turns small absolute differences into large visual differences. Honest practice: bar charts start at zero. (Trend lines tolerate truncation since the eye reads slope, not bar heights.)

Question 1ERP

The single most-cited primary benefit of an ERP is:

  • Lower total cost of ownership
  • Faster implementation
  • Single integrated source of truth across functions
  • No customization
Why: Integration is the win. Cost and speed go the wrong way during ERP rollouts.
Question 2AIS cycles

The "order-to-cash" flow falls under which AIS cycle?

  • Revenue cycle
  • Expenditure cycle
  • Production cycle
  • Financing cycle
Why: Order, ship, bill, collect = revenue cycle (also "OTC"). Expenditure = procure-to-pay (P2P).
Question 3EPM

Which is most likely to live in an EPM (vs ERP)?

  • Real-time invoice posting
  • Inventory receipt
  • Daily payroll processing
  • Multi-scenario rolling forecast
Why: Forecasting and planning are EPM. ERP is the transactional engine.
Question 4Data quality

A vendor record missing the tax ID field fails which dimension?

  • Accuracy
  • Completeness
  • Consistency
  • Timeliness
Why: Required value not present = completeness failure. Accuracy = wrong value; consistency = differs across systems; timeliness = stale.
Question 5Privacy

GDPR principally regulates:

  • Personal data of EU residents
  • Healthcare data in the U.S.
  • Financial reporting in California
  • Trade secrets globally
Why: GDPR = EU/EEA personal data. HIPAA = US healthcare. CCPA = California consumer privacy.
Question 6SDLC

Cutting over from old to new system on a single date with no overlap is the:

  • Parallel strategy
  • Phased strategy
  • Big-bang strategy
  • Pilot strategy
Why: Big bang = one-day cutover. Highest risk, lowest cost. Reserve for low-impact systems or strict deadlines (e.g. tax-year boundaries).
Question 7Agile

Agile development is characterized by:

  • Locking all requirements before any coding
  • Building the entire system before any user feedback
  • Single end-of-project test phase
  • Short iterations producing working software with continuous user feedback
Why: Sprints, working software per iteration, continuous user feedback. The opposite of waterfall's phase-gated, big-bang delivery.
Question 8RPA

Which is RPA LEAST suitable for?

  • High-volume invoice posting
  • Investment-decision recommendations
  • Automated bank reconciliation
  • Data extraction from a fixed-format report
Why: Investment decisions require judgment under uncertainty — outside RPA's rule-based comfort zone. The other three are exactly the kind of work RPA excels at.
Question 9Blockchain

A claim about blockchain that is TRUE:

  • Always fully anonymous
  • Always faster than centralized databases
  • Tamper-evident through cryptographic chaining
  • Always centrally governed
Why: Tamper-evidence (via hash chaining and consensus) is the defining feature. Most chains are pseudonymous, slower than centralized DBs for read-heavy work, and decentralized.
Question 10Lake vs warehouse

Schema-on-read describes a:

  • Data lake
  • Data warehouse
  • OLTP database
  • Master data hub
Why: Lake stores raw data, schema applied at query time. Warehouse forces schema-on-write. OLTP is transactional and tightly schemed; MDM is canonical entity records.
Question 11Analytics types

"Which customers are most likely to churn next quarter?" is what type of analytics?

  • Descriptive
  • Diagnostic
  • Prescriptive
  • Predictive
Why: "Most likely to" = future state given evidence = predictive. Add "and what should we offer them to retain them" → prescriptive.
Question 12Visualization

A waterfall chart is best for:

  • Showing parts of a whole
  • Density across two dimensions
  • Sequential additions/subtractions to reach a total
  • Outlier detection
Why: Waterfalls visualize bridges: opening → +/− components → closing. Common in EBITDA bridges, variance analysis, cash-flow walks.
